# STAR Stories: Founding Product Engineer, Rockstar client (accounting AI)

Picked for a role built around end-to-end product ownership, ambiguity in messy customer workflows, and AI-agent-assisted implementation.

---

## 1. Building Alaya from zero to acquisition

In 2016 I co-founded Alaya, an employee purpose and impact platform, and I was the one who had to turn a blank page into a working product before there was a team to help. There was no customer yet, no codebase, and honestly no proof the idea would hold up once real enterprise clients started using it. My job was to architect and build the whole thing myself for the first stretch, then bring on engineers as the business started signing contracts. I picked a stack that was boring on purpose, Node.js, React, Postgres, so I wouldn't be fighting the tools while I was still fighting the product. The first three months went into getting something demoable, and after that I kept expanding it in layers: multi-tenancy, an API layer, impact reporting, campaign tooling. Once we had traction I hired the first three engineers and put in place the basics, CI/CD, code review, a written architecture doc, so the thing wouldn't collapse under its own growth. It didn't. We got to fifty-plus enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity acquired us in 2021, which told me the early bets were roughly right. Looking back, I'd have wired up real observability much sooner, we didn't get proper APM in until it was overdue, and I'd design for multi-tenancy from day one instead of retrofitting it under load.

## 2. Making a hard architecture call under investor pressure

At Alaya, the monolith started showing real scaling cracks right around the time investors wanted new features, not a rewrite. The team was split between a full microservices rewrite and something more conservative, and somebody had to make a call that wouldn't just be a coin flip dressed up as a technical opinion. I ran a two-week spike comparing both paths with actual load tests behind them, then took the numbers, not a slide deck of opinions, to the CTO and the investors. What we landed on was a strangler-fig approach: pull out the two highest-traffic modules first and leave the rest of the monolith alone until it earned extraction. We shipped the first extracted module in six weeks and saw about a 40% latency improvement on the busiest endpoints, and the rest of the monolith stayed boring and stable straight through the acquisition. Nobody had to live through a big-bang rewrite. The harder part honestly wasn't the technical decision, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the disciplined choice. Data closed that argument faster than debate ever would have, and next time I'd front-load even more benchmarking to cut that debate shorter.

## 3. Driving AI-assisted engineering adoption without a mandate

After the Benevity acquisition I was a senior IC with no direct reports, working across two teams that had zero obligation to change how they worked just because I thought AI-assisted development would help. Nobody handed me the authority to mandate anything, so I had to make the case one engineer at a time. I found three engineers who were curious rather than skeptical and started there, building small internal demos that showed real time saved rather than talking about the concept in the abstract. I ran informal lunch sessions, no calendar invite from a manager, just a demo and a Slack thread, and put together reusable prompt templates in a shared repo so the win wasn't locked in my head. One story that stuck was a two-hour reduction on a PR review cycle, and that kind of concrete number traveled faster than any pitch I could have given. Within about three months, something like 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted review and generation as part of their normal flow, and I ended up the informal go-to on AI tooling for the org. It was slower than a top-down rollout would have been, but it stuck, because people who adopt something on their own terms are the ones who defend it later. That's close to what this role asks for, holding AI-agent output to a real bar rather than trusting it by default.

## 4. Navigating a post-acquisition integration into unfamiliar territory

Joining Benevity in 2021 meant walking into a team roughly ten times the size of Alaya, a different language stack in places, Java plus microservices, and processes I hadn't had a hand in shaping. The task wasn't just to keep shipping, it was to fold Alaya's capabilities into a much larger platform without losing the speed that had made the acquisition worth doing in the first place. I spent the first month deliberately not proposing changes, just mapping how the existing architecture actually worked before I opened my mouth about how it should work. Once I understood the shape of things, I built relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing any technical opinions, and I ended up becoming an architecture liaison for the Europe-facing side of the product. We migrated the core Alaya features in successfully and I contributed to three cross-team architecture decisions along the way. My first instinct, to fix things that weren't actually broken in the new org, was wrong, and slowing down to listen first is what made the rest of it work. That instinct to map the terrain before acting is exactly what the first thirty days on a founding product role should look like.

## 5. Owning ambiguity in a messy, high-stakes domain

When Alaya started handling employee data tied to compliance-sensitive campaigns, the requirements weren't handed to me clean, they came in as half-formed asks from enterprise clients who each had their own regulatory constraints and their own idea of what "done" meant. There was no spec to follow, just a set of real customer workflows I had to go understand before I could design anything. I sat with the actual data flows, traced how information moved from a client's HR system into our platform and back out into reporting, and only then started sketching the product primitives, the states, the edge cases, the verification steps that would make the thing trustworthy. Building it meant writing code and also making judgment calls about what "good enough for launch" meant when there wasn't a clean answer. The result held up under real enterprise scrutiny during due diligence for the acquisition, which is about as hard a test of "does this actually work" as I've had. What I'd do differently is write down the edge cases I found along the way sooner, instead of carrying them in my head until something forced the issue.
