# Negotiation Script: hapiy.ai, Founding Software Engineer

## What's on the table

Posted range: $100K to $200K base, plus equity, for a founding software engineer at a pre-seed company founded in 2025. The role is remote initially, transitioning to in-person in Boston once the team is established. That structure matters more than the number does at this stage: the range assumes a US-based hire, and it says nothing about whether relocation is expected in month three or year two. Both of those need to be pinned down before a number means anything.

## Anchor point

At 17+ years of experience, a founding-engineer track record with an actual exit to a unicorn, and the specific ask here, owning core tech, product definition, and the first hires at once, the right anchor is the top of the posted band: $190K to $200K base, with equity discussed as a real percentage, not a placeholder. The justification isn't Geneva cost of living since this isn't a Swiss role; it's that "founding engineer" who also builds the team is normally two or three hires' worth of scope at a company this size, and that scope is worth paying for regardless of where the person sits.

Opening line: "Given that this covers architecture, product, and the first hires, that's a founding-team scope, not a single IC role. I'd want to start the conversation at the top of your posted range, call it $195K, with equity to match, and go from there."

## If they come in low

If the offer lands mid-band or below, say $130K to $150K, the counter is about scope, not need: "The responsibilities here, owning the stack end to end and building the team from scratch, are usually split across two or three early hires. I'm glad to take that on, but that's exactly why the number should sit closer to the top of the range." If the answer is that pre-seed cash is tight, that's the moment to move the conversation to equity instead of holding the line on cash: trade $10K to $15K of base for a meaningfully larger equity stake, since a company this early has more room on equity than on payroll.

## The Boston relocation question

This has to get addressed before comp gets finalized, not after. "Remote initially, in-person eventually" is doing a lot of work in that sentence, and it needs a real timeline attached to it. Script: "I'm in Geneva, and the posting mentions an eventual move to in-person in Boston. Before we get into exact numbers, I want to understand the timeline on that, whether it's six months out or two years out, since that changes what a fair package looks like. If relocation is genuinely on the table, visa sponsorship and relocation support need to be their own line items, separate from base and equity."

If in-person in Boston turns out to be a near-term hard requirement, the honest answer is that this becomes a different conversation about a US move, not a remote comp negotiation, and that's worth surfacing early rather than discovering it after an offer is signed.

## Equity notes

- Ask directly what percentage a founding engineer hire lands at, given the stage; don't accept a placeholder like "market rate" without a number attached.
- Confirm the fully diluted share count the percentage is calculated against. A headline number without the denominator tells you nothing.
- Push for a standard 4-year vest with a 1-year cliff, and monthly vesting after the cliff rather than a second annual cliff.
- Ask what happens to the equity stake through a priced seed round: is there anti-dilution protection, or at least a commitment to disclose future round terms before they close.
- At a company this early, ask about the option pool size and whether a refresh is planned in the next 12 to 18 months, since early hires are the ones who absorb an unflagged pool top-up.

## Competing offer leverage

Even without a live competing offer, the honest framing works: "I'm in conversation with a couple of other early-stage AI infrastructure roles at similar stage, so I'd like to move on both the comp and the relocation timeline within a week or two." Use it to compress the timeline, not to bluff a specific number that doesn't exist; a fabricated offer is easy to catch and expensive to be caught making.
