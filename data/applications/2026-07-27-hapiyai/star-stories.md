# STAR Stories: hapiy.ai, Founding Software Engineer

## 1. Building Alaya from zero to acquisition

Best for: "Tell me about building something from scratch," founding engineer questions, how I handle ambiguity when there's no playbook yet.

In 2016 I co-founded Alaya, a purpose and impact platform for employee engagement, at a point where there was no product, no customers, just a blank page and a business already talking to its first enterprise prospects. My job was to architect and build the whole thing, alone at first and then with a small team, while sales kept promising things engineering hadn't built yet. I picked Node.js, React, and PostgreSQL because they let me move fast without locking into infrastructure I didn't need at that stage, and I pushed to get a working demo in front of the first client within three months. From there it was constant iteration: multi-tenant architecture, an API layer, impact reporting, physical reward campaigns for clients who wanted more than a digital badge. Once the team grew past me, I hired the first three engineers and put in place CI/CD, code review norms, and lightweight architecture docs so decisions didn't live only in my head. The platform ended up serving 50+ enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity, a Canadian company valued north of a billion dollars, acquired us in 2021. Looking back, I'd have invested in observability much earlier, we bolted on proper monitoring later than we should have, and I underestimated how expensive it would be to retrofit multi-tenancy instead of designing for it from day one. That's the mistake I'd want to avoid repeating at a company building trust infrastructure from scratch, where the cost of getting the data model wrong early compounds fast.

## 2. Making a hard architecture call under investor pressure

Best for: technical trade-off questions, how I weigh rewrite risk against delivery pressure, production reliability at an early stage.

At Alaya our monolith started showing real scaling cracks right as investors were pushing hard for new features instead of infrastructure work, and the team was split between a full microservices rewrite and a more modest modular approach. I ran a two-week technical spike comparing both paths with actual prototype load tests rather than opinions, then took the results to the CTO and investors with numbers instead of a pitch. What we landed on was a strangler fig migration: pull out the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest of the monolith alone, and define clear criteria for when the next module would qualify for extraction. The first extracted module shipped in six weeks, performance on the busiest endpoints improved by roughly 40%, and the remaining system stayed stable all the way through the acquisition, so we never needed the big-bang rewrite everyone was afraid of. The hardest part wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the disciplined choice rather than the timid one. A founding engineer role at a pre-seed company means making calls like this constantly, usually with less data than you'd like, and I'd rather ship a scoped fix I can defend with numbers than a rewrite I can only defend with confidence.

## 3. Driving AI-assisted engineering adoption without a mandate

Best for: how I get people to trust and actually use a new system, influence without authority, tooling adoption questions.

At Benevity I was a senior staff IC with no direct reports, but I wanted two teams that didn't report to me to adopt AI-assisted development in a way that stuck, not as a novelty someone tried once and dropped. Rather than push a top-down policy, I started with three engineers who were already curious and built small internal demos showing concrete time saved on things like PR review and boilerplate generation. I ran informal lunch sessions with no mandate attached, just showing what worked, and committed prompt templates to a shared repo so people could adapt them instead of starting from nothing. I also wrote up results as they came in; one story was a two-hour PR review cycle that dropped to twenty minutes once the reviewer had a good template to start from. Within three months, about 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted review and generation regularly, and I'd become the person people asked when they wanted to get started. The slower, opt-in approach took longer than a decree from above would have, but the engineers who adopted it became its advocates internally. Trust in a system, whether it's an internal tool or a product being sold to enterprise buyers, gets built the same way: let people see it work on something small before asking them to depend on it.

## 4. Integrating into a much larger org after acquisition

Best for: navigating change, working inside someone else's system without breaking it, cross-functional collaboration under a different culture.

After Benevity acquired Alaya in 2021, I joined a team roughly ten times the size of what I'd been running, with a different codebase in Java and microservices, different processes, and a culture I hadn't shaped. My task was to get Alaya's capabilities integrated into Benevity's platform while still shipping new features, without losing the velocity that had gotten us acquired in the first place. I spent the first month mostly listening, mapping the existing architecture before proposing any changes, and built relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing technical opinions their way. That groundwork let me become a cross-team architecture liaison for the Europe-facing product surface and contribute to three major cross-team architecture decisions, on top of the AI tooling adoption from story three. I moved too fast early on, trying to fix things in the new org that weren't actually broken, and slowing down to listen first is what made the rest of the integration work. It's a habit that matters even more at a company just starting to hire, since the patterns a founding engineer sets are the ones everyone after them inherits.
