# STAR Stories, vCluster Labs Staff Software Engineer

Prepared from the master bank in `profile/star-bank.md`, picked for the parts of this role that stood out most: strategic technical leadership close to a CTO, rapid prototyping, customer-facing translation, and deep systems work under real constraints.

## 1. Building Alaya from zero (founding engineer / ambiguity)

In 2016 I co-founded Alaya with a blank page. No customers, no product, no team, just an idea about employee purpose and impact that a couple of us thought was worth building a company around. My task was to architect and build the whole platform alone at first, then grow a team, while the business was out signing its first enterprise clients on promises about a product that barely existed yet. I picked a pragmatic stack, Node.js, React, Postgres, that could scale without turning into an over-engineered mess on day one, and got a working demo out in about three months. From there I kept expanding it iteratively: multi-tenant architecture, an API layer, impact reporting, postal campaigns, and along the way I hired and onboarded our first three engineers and set up CI/CD and a real code review culture. The platform ended up running in production for 50+ enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity, a Canadian unicorn, acquired the company in 2021. Looking back, I'd invest in observability much earlier, we bolted on proper APM late, and I underestimated how expensive retrofitting multi-tenancy would be. I'd design for it from day zero next time.

## 2. The strangler-fig call instead of a rewrite (technical trade-offs under pressure)

At Alaya, our monolith was starting to show real scaling cracks, and the team was split between a full microservices rewrite and a more modest modular monolith. Investors wanted new features shipped, not months spent on a rewrite with no visible output. I ran a two-week technical spike comparing both approaches with actual prototype load tests, then presented the trade-offs to the CTO and investors using numbers instead of opinions. What I proposed was a strangler-fig migration: extract just the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest of the monolith alone, and define clear criteria for when to extract anything else. The first extracted module shipped in six weeks and performance improved about 40% on our busiest endpoints. The rest of the monolith stayed stable straight through the acquisition, and we never did the big-bang rewrite. The hard part wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was actually the disciplined choice. Data cut that debate short. Next time I'd do more of the early benchmarking up front to get there faster.

## 3. Leading two teams into AI-assisted workflows without a mandate (influence without authority)

After joining Benevity as a Senior Staff IC, no direct reports, I wanted two teams that didn't report to me to actually change how they worked and start using AI tooling in their day to day. Rather than push a top-down policy, I started with three engineers who were already curious, built small internal demos that showed concrete time saved, and ran informal lunch-and-learn sessions with no mandate attached, just working examples. I put together reusable prompt templates and committed them to a shared repo, and wrote up results as they came in, one story was a PR review cycle that dropped by about two hours. Within three months, roughly 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted review and generation regularly, and I'd become the informal go-to person for AI tooling across the org. It was slower than a decree from above would have been, but it stuck, because people who opted in on their own terms ended up advocating for it themselves. I couldn't have gotten that same result by mandating it.

## 4. Mapping the new architecture before touching anything (post-acquisition integration)

When Benevity acquired Alaya in 2021, I landed on a team roughly ten times the size of what I'd worked in before, a different culture, a Java-based microservices codebase instead of the one I'd built, different processes altogether. My job was to get Alaya's capabilities integrated into Benevity's platform while still shipping new features, without losing the product velocity we'd had as a small team. I spent the first month just mapping the existing architecture before I proposed a single change, and built relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing any technical opinions their way. From there I drove adoption of AI-augmented workflows across the org and became a de facto cross-team architecture liaison for the Europe-facing product surface, contributing to three major cross-team architecture decisions along the way. I'll admit I moved too fast early on trying to fix things in the new org that genuinely weren't broken. Slowing down and listening first is what actually made me effective, and it's the instinct I'd bring into any new codebase, including a Go-based control plane I haven't touched yet.
