# STAR Stories: Lamatic.ai, Founding Engineer

Prepared for behavioral rounds. Picked for founding-engineer signal on a small, capital-efficient team: building infrastructure from zero, making architecture calls under budget pressure, and driving AI-assisted workflows, since Lamatic's whole pitch is abstracting GenAI complexity behind a GraphQL layer with a team that got to breakeven on under a million dollars raised.

## Story 1: Building Alaya from zero to acquisition

This is the one I'd lead with for a founding engineer conversation. In 2016 I co-founded Alaya, an employee purpose and impact platform, and there was nothing yet, no customers, no product, not even a settled stack. My job was to architect and build the whole thing myself first, then grow a team around it while the business was closing its first enterprise deals. I picked Node.js, React, and PostgreSQL because they let me move fast without boxing myself into something I'd regret in year two, and I got the core product to a first demo inside three months. From there it was steady expansion: multi-tenant architecture, an API layer, reporting, and eventually hiring the first three engineers and setting up CI/CD and a code review culture that didn't exist before I wrote it down. We ended up with 50+ enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity, a Canadian unicorn valued over a billion dollars, acquired us in 2021. Looking back, I'd have added real observability much earlier, we bolted it on late and paid for that in debugging time. I also underestimated what retrofitting multi-tenancy would cost, and if I did it again I'd design for it from day one instead of week one.

## Story 2: A hard architecture call under investor pressure

At Alaya, our monolith started showing real scaling cracks right as investors were pushing hard for new features, not a rewrite. The team was split between a full microservices rewrite and just living with what we had, and I needed to make a call that wouldn't blow up either the roadmap or the system. I ran a two-week spike comparing both approaches with actual load tests instead of opinions, then brought the CTO and investors concrete numbers. What we landed on was a strangler fig migration: pull out the two highest-traffic modules first and leave the rest of the monolith alone, with clear criteria for when to extract the next piece. The first module shipped in six weeks and cut latency on our busiest endpoints by roughly 40%. The rest of the monolith held up fine all the way through the acquisition, no big-bang rewrite required. The hard part honestly wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the disciplined choice. That's the same instinct a small team watching runway has to apply constantly, and it's one I'd bring straight into how Lamatic decides what to build versus what to leave alone.

## Story 3: Driving AI tooling adoption with no authority to mandate it

After Benevity acquired us, I joined as a Senior Staff IC on a team roughly ten times the size of Alaya, no direct reports, and I wanted two teams that didn't answer to me to actually change how they worked day to day. Rather than push a mandate nobody asked for, I started with three engineers who were already curious, built small internal demos that showed real time saved on their own tickets, and ran informal lunch sessions with no agenda beyond showing what it looked like. I wrote up reusable prompt templates and committed them to a shared repo so people could just take what worked. Within about three months, something like 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted review and generation regularly, and I'd become the person people pinged when they wanted to get better at it. It stuck because people adopted it on their own terms, which is close to how I'd want a GraphQL abstraction layer to earn trust with developers too, by being genuinely faster, not mandated.

## Story 4: Integrating into a much bigger org without losing velocity

Joining Benevity after the acquisition meant a completely different codebase, mostly Java microservices instead of the Node stack I'd built, a much bigger team, and a culture I didn't know yet. My task was to fold Alaya's capabilities into their platform while still shipping new features, without the product velocity we'd built just evaporating in the transition. I spent the first month mostly listening, mapping how their architecture actually worked before I proposed anything, and built relationships with the platform and infra teams before I pushed a single technical opinion. That groundwork is what let me later become a cross-team architecture liaison for the Europe-facing product surface and contribute to three major architecture decisions that stuck. My early instinct was to move fast and start fixing things that, it turned out, weren't actually broken in their context, just different. Slowing down and listening first is what made me useful there, and it's a lesson I'd apply directly to walking into a small team that already has strong opinions about how their own platform should work.
