# STAR Stories: DeepSense, Founding DevOps / Platform Engineer

Prepared for behavioral rounds. Picked for founding-level infrastructure signal, since DeepSense needs someone who can own automation end to end on a small team, not just operate infrastructure someone else designed.

## Story 1: Building Alaya's platform from zero

This is the one I'd lead with, since DeepSense is small enough that whoever takes this role will own the stack the way I owned Alaya's. In 2016 I co-founded Alaya, an employee purpose and impact platform, and there was nothing yet: no customers, no product, not even a settled stack. My job was to architect and build the whole thing myself first, then grow a team around it while the business was closing its first enterprise deals. I picked Node.js, React, and PostgreSQL because they let me move fast without boxing myself into something I'd regret in year two, and I got the core product to a first demo inside three months. From there it was steady expansion: multi-tenant architecture, an API layer, reporting, and eventually hiring the first three engineers and setting up CI/CD and a review culture that didn't exist before I wrote it down. We ended up with 50+ enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity, a Canadian unicorn valued over a billion dollars, acquired us in 2021. Looking back, I'd have added real observability much earlier since we bolted it on late and paid for that in debugging time later.

## Story 2: A hard architecture call under investor pressure

DeepSense is running production automation at a stage where the wrong infrastructure bet gets expensive fast, and this is the story that shows how I make that call. At Alaya, our monolith started showing real scaling cracks right as investors were pushing hard for new features, not a rewrite. The team was split between a full microservices rewrite and just living with what we had, and I needed a decision that wouldn't blow up either the roadmap or the system. I ran a two-week spike comparing both approaches with actual load tests instead of opinions, then brought the CTO and investors concrete numbers instead of a recommendation dressed up as certainty. What we landed on was a strangler fig migration: pull out the two highest-traffic modules first and leave the rest of the monolith alone, with clear criteria for extracting the next piece. The first module shipped in six weeks and cut latency on our busiest endpoints by roughly 40%. The hard part wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the disciplined choice. I'd push for the benchmarking earlier next time, just to shorten that debate.

## Story 3: Driving AI tooling adoption with no authority to mandate it

Since DeepSense is AI-first by design, this is probably the closest match to how I'd actually spend my time there. After Benevity acquired us, I joined as a Senior Staff IC on a team roughly ten times the size of Alaya, no direct reports, and I wanted two teams that didn't answer to me to change how they worked day to day. Rather than push a mandate nobody asked for, I started with three engineers who were already curious, built small internal demos showing real time saved on their own tickets, and ran informal sessions with no agenda beyond showing what the tools could do. I wrote reusable prompt templates and committed them to a shared repo so people could take what worked without asking permission first. Within about three months, something like 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted review and generation regularly, and I'd become the person people pinged when they wanted to get better at it. It stuck because people adopted it on their own terms, not because someone told them to, and that's the same approach I'd bring to getting a small team comfortable with heavier automation.

## Story 4: Integrating into a bigger org without losing velocity

Joining Benevity after the acquisition meant a different codebase, mostly Java microservices instead of the Node stack I'd built, a bigger team, and a culture I didn't know yet. My task was to fold Alaya's capabilities into their platform while still shipping new features, without losing the velocity we'd built at a fraction of the size. I spent the first month mostly listening, mapping how their architecture actually worked before proposing anything, and built relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing a single technical opinion. That groundwork is what let me become a cross-team architecture liaison for the Europe-facing product surface and contribute to three major decisions that stuck. My early instinct was to move fast and fix things that, it turned out, weren't broken, just different. Slowing down and listening first is what made me useful there, and it's the instinct I'd bring to a small team where trust gets built fast or not at all.
