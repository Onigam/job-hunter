# STAR Stories: Ripple, Staff Software Engineer Infrastructure

Prepared for the Custody / Platform Engineering interview loop. Picked for relevance to infrastructure reliability, multi-team architecture decisions, and mentoring without formal authority, since those are the load-bearing parts of this role.

## Building Alaya's Infrastructure From Zero

I co-founded Alaya in 2016 with a blank page. No customers, no product, no infrastructure. My job was to architect and build the whole platform alone at first, then with a small team, while the business was closing its first enterprise clients, so there wasn't room to get the foundation wrong and redo it later. I picked a pragmatic stack, Node.js, React, PostgreSQL, that could scale without turning into a science project, and got the core features to a working demo inside three months. From there it was iterative: multi-tenant architecture, an API layer, impact reporting, postal campaigns, each one built on infrastructure that had to keep serving existing clients while it grew. I hired and onboarded the first three engineers and set up CI/CD and a code review culture before the team got big enough that skipping it would have hurt. By the time Benevity acquired us in 2021, we had over fifty enterprise clients across Switzerland and France running on infrastructure I'd designed from day one. Looking back, I'd have invested in observability much earlier, we bolted on proper APM late, and I underestimated how expensive it would be to retrofit multi-tenancy instead of designing for it from the start. That's a mistake I don't plan to repeat.

## Choosing a Strangler Fig Migration Under Investor Pressure

At Alaya, our monolith was starting to crack under load, and the team was split between a full microservices rewrite and a more modest modular monolith. Investors wanted new features, not a quarter spent on plumbing, so I had to make a call that held up on both fronts. I ran a two week spike comparing both approaches with real load tests instead of arguing from opinion, then took the numbers to the CTO and investors directly. Rather than the big rewrite, I proposed extracting only the two highest traffic modules first and leaving the rest of the monolith alone, with clear criteria for when we'd extract more. We shipped the first extracted module in six weeks and saw a roughly 40% performance improvement on the busiest endpoints, and the remaining monolith stayed stable all the way through the acquisition. No big bang rewrite, no six month gap with no new features. The hard part wasn't the technical design, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the disciplined choice. Data closed that argument faster than any amount of talking would have, and next time I'd run the benchmarks even earlier to cut the debate short.

## Integrating Alaya Into Benevity's Platform Post-Acquisition

After Benevity acquired Alaya in 2021, I joined as Senior Staff Engineer on a team roughly ten times the size of what I'd worked with before, a different codebase in Java and microservices, different culture, different process, and my job was to get Alaya's capabilities integrated without losing the product velocity we'd built. I spent the first month just mapping the existing architecture before I proposed anything, and built relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing any technical opinions on them. Once there was trust, I pushed adoption of AI-assisted engineering practices, prompt patterns and tooling, across the org and became the architecture liaison for the Europe-facing product surface. We got the core Alaya features migrated, I contributed to three major cross-team architecture decisions, and became the person two teams went to for AI tooling questions. My first instinct was to move fast and fix things that weren't actually broken in the new org, and that cost me some trust early on. Slowing down and listening first is what actually made the integration work, and it's the same instinct I'd bring to any team with infrastructure conventions I didn't write.

## Driving AI Tooling Adoption With No Direct Reports

At Benevity I was a Senior Staff IC with no direct reports, but I needed engineers on two teams that didn't report to me to actually change how they worked day to day. Mandating it wasn't an option, so I started small: three curious engineers, a few internal tooling demos that showed concrete time saved on real PRs, informal lunch and learn sessions with no attendance requirement. I built reusable prompt templates and committed them to a shared repo so the win wasn't just a demo, it was something people could take and use immediately. Within three months, about 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted review and generation in their normal workflow, and I'd become the informal lead for AI tooling across the org without anyone appointing me to it. It was slower than a top-down rollout would have been, but it stuck, because people who adopted it on their own terms were the ones telling their teammates to try it. That's the version of influence I trust, and it's the one I'd want to use mentoring engineers on infrastructure decisions here, where I won't have blockchain-specific seniority to lean on for the first few months.
