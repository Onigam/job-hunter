# STAR Stories, Staff Frontend Engineer @ SMG Swiss Marketplace Group

Picked for this role because SMG's Staff Frontend posting leans hard on cross-team technical direction, shared libraries adopted org-wide, mentoring without formal authority, and architecture decisions under delivery pressure. All four show up below.

---

## 1. Building Alaya from zero (blank-page architecture, ambiguity)

Back in 2016 I co-founded Alaya with nothing more than an idea for an employee purpose and impact platform. No customers yet, no product, just a blank page and a small window before the first enterprise deals needed something real to demo. I picked a pragmatic stack, Node.js, React, PostgreSQL, deliberately avoiding anything fancier than we needed, and got the core features working in about three months so we had a first demo to show. From there it was iterative: multi-tenant architecture, an API layer, impact reporting, postal campaigns, each added as the business actually needed it rather than up front. I also hired and onboarded our first three engineers and set up CI/CD and a code review culture while the platform was still forming under us. By the time we were acquired by Benevity in 2021, we had over fifty enterprise clients across Switzerland and France running on that foundation, which felt like a pretty direct validation of the early calls. If I did it again I'd invest in observability much sooner, we added proper APM late, and I'd design for multi-tenancy from day zero instead of retrofitting it once it started to hurt.

## 2. Shipping a strangler-fig migration instead of a rewrite (architecture trade-off under pressure)

A couple of years into Alaya, our monolith was starting to show real scaling cracks, and the team was split between a full microservices rewrite and something more modest. Investors, meanwhile, wanted new features, not a multi-month rewrite with no visible output. I ran a two-week technical spike comparing both approaches with actual prototype load tests, then took the numbers, not opinions, to the CTO and investors. What we landed on was a strangler-fig approach: extract the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest of the monolith alone, and set clear criteria for when to peel off the next piece. The first module shipped in six weeks and cut latency on our busiest endpoints by around 40%. The rest of the monolith stayed stable straight through the acquisition, so we never needed the big-bang rewrite people were originally arguing for. The hard part honestly wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the right call; having real benchmarks in hand shortened that debate a lot, and next time I'd do that early benchmarking even sooner.

## 3. Driving AI tooling adoption across two teams with no mandate (influence without authority)

After Benevity acquired Alaya, I moved into a Senior Staff IC role on a team roughly ten times the size of what I was used to, no direct reports, different codebase, different culture. At some point I wanted two teams that didn't report to me to actually adopt AI-assisted development, and I knew a top-down push wouldn't land well in that org. So I started small: three engineers who were already curious, a few internal demos showing concrete time saved on things like PR review cycles, and informal lunch-and-learn sessions with no mandate attached. I built out reusable prompt templates and committed them to a shared repo so people could just take what worked. Within three months, about 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted review and generation as part of their normal workflow, and I ended up becoming the informal go-to person for AI tooling across the org. It was slower than an official rollout would have been, but it stuck, people who opted in on their own terms ended up advocating for it themselves, which isn't something a decree from above tends to produce.

## 4. Slowing down before pushing architecture opinions post-acquisition (cross-team collaboration)

Joining Benevity meant landing in a much bigger org with an established Java microservices codebase, a long way from the Node.js and React world I'd built at Alaya. My instinct early on was to start proposing changes to things that looked off to me, but I held back and spent the first month just mapping how the existing architecture actually worked before opening my mouth. I also put real effort into building relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing any technical opinions their way. That groundwork paid off: I became a cross-team architecture liaison for the Europe-facing product surface and contributed to three major cross-team architecture decisions that followed. Looking back, I moved too fast in my first few weeks trying to fix things that genuinely weren't broken, just unfamiliar, and deliberately slowing down to listen first is what actually made me effective in that org. It's a lesson I'd apply directly to a multi-team frontend architecture role like this one, where credibility across teams matters more than being right first.
