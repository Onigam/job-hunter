# STAR+R Stories, Almedia, Founding Engineer

Picked for this role because Almedia wants someone who's actually done zero-to-one, owns scope and architecture end to end, and moves fast without a rewrite six months later. These four cover that ground.

## 1. Building a platform from zero to acquisition

In 2016 I co-founded Alaya with literally nothing built and no customers signed. My job was to architect and build the entire platform, first alone and then with a small team, while the business closed its first enterprise deals in parallel. I picked a stack, Node.js, React, PostgreSQL, that could scale without over-engineering for a problem we didn't have yet, got a working demo out in three months, and then layered in multi-tenancy, an API layer, and reporting as real usage showed up. Along the way I hired and onboarded the first three engineers and put in place CI/CD and a code review culture that outlived me on the team. The platform reached 50+ enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity, a Canadian company valued north of a billion dollars, acquired us in 2021. If I did it again, I'd invest in observability from day one instead of bolting on proper monitoring late, and I'd design the data model for multi-tenancy up front rather than retrofitting it under load.

## 2. Choosing a strangler-fig migration over a full rewrite

At Alaya, our monolith started showing real scaling cracks right as the business was signing bigger enterprise clients, and investors wanted new features, not a rewrite. I was asked to make a call that would hold up technically and wouldn't blow up the roadmap. Instead of picking a side in the microservices-versus-monolith debate by opinion, I ran a two-week spike with load tests on both approaches and brought the CTO and investors numbers instead of a pitch. What I proposed was a strangler-fig migration: pull out the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest alone, and define clear criteria for when to extract more. We shipped the first extracted module in six weeks and saw about a 40% latency improvement on the busiest endpoints, and the remaining monolith stayed stable all the way through the acquisition. Looking back, the data is what shortened the debate, and next time I'd run those benchmarks earlier so the argument never has to happen the same way twice.

## 3. Driving adoption of AI-assisted engineering without authority

After joining Benevity as a Senior Staff IC with no direct reports, I wanted two teams that didn't report to me to adopt AI-assisted development practices, and there was no mandate backing that up. Rather than pushing a top-down rollout, I found three curious engineers, built small demos that showed real time saved on actual PRs, and ran informal lunch sessions with no attendance requirement. I wrote up concrete results, one story was saving about two hours on a single review cycle, and put reusable prompt templates in a shared repo anyone could pull from. Within three months, roughly 70% of engineers across both teams were using the practices on their own, and I became the person people asked about AI tooling org-wide. It was slower than a decree from above would have been, but the engineers who opted in ended up advocating for it themselves, which a mandate never would have produced.

## 4. Shipping the first version alone under real deadline pressure

Before Alaya had a single line of committed backend code, we already had a demo date with our first prospective enterprise client, about three months out, and no team to spread the work across. My task was to get a working, demoable product in front of that client without cutting corners that would come back to bite us at the first renewal. I scoped ruthlessly: one core workflow end to end instead of five half-built ones, a schema I was confident wouldn't need to be thrown out, and deployment automated from week one so I wasn't hand-pushing code the night before the demo. We closed that first client on schedule, and the core of what I shipped in those three months was still running, mostly unchanged, when Benevity acquired the company five years later. What I'd do differently is write down the reasoning behind a few of those early scoping calls, because later hires kept asking why things were built the way they were and I was relying on memory to answer.
