# STAR Stories: Head of Engineering, AI Security (Check Point / Lakera, Zurich)

Selected and adapted from the master bank for this role. The role is heavy on scaling teams post-acquisition, developing engineering managers, and setting architectural direction for security products under a bigger corporate umbrella, so I picked stories that speak to those specifically.

## 1. Building Alaya from zero, and what it takes to make a founding bet pay off

In 2016 I co-founded Alaya, a purpose and impact platform for employee engagement, starting from an actual blank page with no customers and no product. My task was to architect and build the whole thing, alone at first, while the business was already out signing its first enterprise clients, so there wasn't room to spend six months on a perfect foundation. I picked a pragmatic stack, Node.js, React, PostgreSQL, that could scale without turning into an over-engineered mess, and pushed to a first demo within three months. From there I expanded iteratively into multi-tenant architecture, an API layer, and impact reporting, and hired the first three engineers, setting up CI/CD and a code review culture as the team grew. The platform reached production with over 50 enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity, a Canadian unicorn valued north of a billion dollars, acquired us in 2021. Looking back, I'd invest in observability much earlier since we bolted on proper APM late, and I'd design for multi-tenancy from day zero rather than retrofit it under load.

## 2. Leading two teams that didn't report to me

At Benevity I was a Senior Staff IC with no direct reports, and I wanted to drive adoption of AI-assisted development practices across two teams that had no reason to listen to me beyond the merit of the idea. Rather than push a mandate I didn't have, I started with three curious engineers, built small internal demos that showed concrete time saved, and ran informal lunch sessions with no obligation to attend. I documented specific wins, like a two-hour cut on a PR review cycle, and committed reusable prompt templates to a shared repo so people could adopt at their own pace. Within three months, about 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted review and generation regularly, and I became the informal AI tooling lead for the org. It was slower than a top-down rollout would have been, but it stuck, because people who opt in on their own terms end up defending the change to their peers, which a decree from above never gets you.

## 3. Choosing not to rewrite everything under investor pressure

At Alaya, our monolith was starting to strain under load right as investors were pushing hard for new features rather than a rewrite, and the team was split between a full microservices migration and something more conservative. I ran a two-week technical spike comparing both paths with real prototype load tests, then took the trade-offs to the CTO and investors as numbers rather than opinions. We landed on a strangler-fig approach, extracting the two highest-traffic modules first and leaving the rest of the monolith intact, with explicit criteria for when to peel off the next piece. The first extraction shipped in six weeks and cut latency on our busiest endpoints by around 40%, and the remaining monolith held up all the way through the acquisition without a big-bang rewrite ever being necessary. The hard part wasn't the technical call, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was actually the disciplined choice, and I'd front-load even more benchmarking next time to shorten that debate.

## 4. Integrating into a much bigger org without breaking what worked

After Benevity acquired Alaya in 2021, I landed on a team roughly ten times the size of what I'd known, with a different codebase in Java and microservices, and different processes for basically everything. My task was to fold Alaya's capabilities into Benevity's platform while still shipping new features, without losing the velocity that had gotten us acquired in the first place. I spent the first month just mapping the existing architecture before proposing any changes, and built relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing technical opinions I hadn't earned the standing for yet. That patience paid off: I ended up contributing to three major cross-team architecture decisions and became the go-to architecture liaison for the Europe-facing product surface. I moved too fast early on trying to fix things that weren't actually broken in the new org, and slowing down to listen first is what turned me from an outsider into someone people brought decisions to.

## 5. Making a defensible call under ambiguity, for a Head of Engineering audience

This one draws on the same monolith decision above but told through a different lens, since interviewers at this level often want to hear how you handle organizational persuasion, not just the engineering trade-off. The technical answer, strangler-fig extraction over full rewrite, mattered less in the room than how I got a CTO and investors to trust a plan that sounded, on its face, like doing less. What worked was refusing to argue from conviction and instead building the smallest experiment that could produce a number, then letting that number do the persuading. It's the same instinct I'd bring to setting architectural direction for Red Teaming and Agentic Security products here: get a team aligned behind evidence before asking them to trust a bet, especially when the stakes involve security-sensitive systems where credibility with senior engineers has to be earned, not asserted.
