# STAR Stories: Founding Engineer / Product Architect, Stealth AI Startup

Picked for a founding architect role: zero-to-one ownership, hard architecture calls under pressure, working across a much bigger org after acquisition, and driving adoption of new tooling without formal authority. The last one maps directly onto the "AI-driven development workflows" line in their posting.

## 1. Building Alaya from zero to acquisition

In 2016 I co-founded Alaya, an employee purpose and impact platform, and there was nothing to inherit: no product, no customers, no codebase. My job was to architect and build the whole thing, alone at first and then with a small team, while the business side was already out signing enterprise clients who expected something working. I picked a stack that could scale without turning into a science project, Node, React, PostgreSQL, and pushed to get a working demo in front of the first prospects within three months. From there it was a steady expansion: multi-tenancy, an API layer, impact reporting, campaign tooling, and hiring the first three engineers while setting up the basics, CI/CD, code review, some architecture docs so decisions didn't only live in my head. We ended up with over fifty enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity, a Canadian company valued north of a billion dollars, acquired us in 2021. If I did it again I'd bring in real observability much earlier, we didn't have proper APM until things were already painful, and I'd design for multi-tenancy from day one instead of retrofitting it once it started to hurt.

## 2. The monolith-versus-microservices call under pressure

At Alaya we hit the point every growing product hits: the monolith was starting to creak, and the team was split between a full microservices rewrite and just cleaning up what we had. Investors wanted new features, not a multi-quarter rewrite, so I couldn't just pick the technically purest answer and hope everyone would live with the delay. I ran a two-week spike comparing both approaches with actual load tests, not opinions, then took the numbers to the CTO and investors and proposed a strangler-fig path instead: pull the two highest-traffic modules out first, leave the rest monolithic, and set explicit criteria for when we'd extract more. The first module shipped in six weeks, the busiest endpoints got about 40% faster, and the remaining monolith stayed stable straight through the acquisition, no big-bang rewrite needed. The hard part honestly wasn't the technical design, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the disciplined choice. Data closed that argument faster than debate did, and next time I'd run the early benchmarks even sooner to cut the back-and-forth short.

## 3. Landing inside Benevity after the acquisition

Right after Benevity acquired Alaya in 2021, I went from a small team where I knew every line of code to a Senior Staff role on an org roughly ten times the size, a different language, Java instead of Node, different processes, a different culture entirely. My task was to get Alaya's capabilities integrated into their platform without losing the speed we'd built the company on. I spent the first month mostly listening, mapping how their architecture actually worked before proposing anything, and built relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing any technical opinions their way. That patience paid off: the core Alaya features made it into the platform, I ended up contributing to three cross-team architecture decisions, and became the person both regions pulled in when a European-facing question came up. My first instinct, early on, was to fix things that weren't actually broken in the new org, and slowing down instead of pattern-matching from Alaya made me a lot more useful.

## 4. Driving AI-assisted development adoption with no mandate

As a Senior Staff IC at Benevity I had no direct reports, but I wanted two teams that didn't report to me to actually change how they worked day to day by folding AI tooling into their normal workflow. Rather than push a policy nobody asked for, I started with three engineers who were already curious, built small demos that showed real time saved on things like PR review, and ran informal lunch sessions, no mandate attached, just "here's what this looks like on a real ticket." I put together reusable prompt templates and committed them to a shared repo so people didn't have to start from scratch. Within about three months, something like 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted review or generation regularly, and I'd become the person people asked when they wanted to get more out of the tooling. It was slower than an announcement from a manager would have been, but it stuck, because people adopted it because it actually helped them, not because someone told them to, and that's the version of this I'd want to run again for a platform like the one in this posting.
