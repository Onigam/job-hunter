# STAR Stories: Founding Engineer, Hub

## 1. Building Alaya from a blank page

Best for: "tell me about building something from zero" / founding engineer questions / how you handle ambiguity when there's no playbook.

In 2016 I co-founded Alaya, a purpose and impact platform for employee engagement, and there was nothing to start from, no customers, no product, no team. My job was to architect and build the whole thing while the business was out signing its first enterprise clients, so the pressure to ship something real was immediate, not theoretical. I picked a pragmatic stack, Node.js, React, PostgreSQL, that could carry weight without forcing us into premature complexity, and I got the core features to a first demo inside three months. From there it was iteration: multi-tenant architecture, an API layer, impact reporting, postal campaign tooling, and eventually hiring the first three engineers and setting up CI/CD and a code review culture that didn't exist a year earlier. The platform ended up running production for more than 50 enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity, a Canadian unicorn valued north of a billion dollars, acquired us in 2021, which told me the early technical bets had held up. If I did it again I'd invest in observability much earlier, we bolted on proper APM late, and I'd design for multi-tenancy from day zero instead of retrofitting it once it started to hurt.

## 2. Choosing a strangler-fig migration over a rewrite

Best for: technical trade-offs under pressure / how you handle scaling and technical debt / infrastructure decisions with strict deadlines.

At Alaya our monolith started showing scaling cracks right as investors were pushing hard for new features, not a rewrite, and the team was split between a full microservices rewrite and something more modest. I ran a two-week technical spike comparing both paths with actual load tests instead of opinions, then took the results to the CTO and investors and proposed a strangler-fig approach: pull out the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest of the monolith alone, and define clear criteria for when to extract more later. We shipped the first extracted module in about six weeks, the busiest endpoints got roughly 40% faster, and the remaining monolith stayed stable all the way through the acquisition, so we never had to justify a big-bang rewrite that would have eaten months we didn't have. The hardest part honestly wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the disciplined choice rather than the timid one. Data closed that argument faster than any amount of talking would have, and next time I'd push for that benchmarking even earlier to cut the debate short.

## 3. Leading AI tooling adoption without any authority to mandate it

Best for: influence without authority / driving adoption of new practices / agentic engineering and tooling culture.

At Benevity I was a senior staff IC with no direct reports, but I wanted two teams that didn't report to me to actually change how they worked and bring AI-assisted development into their day to day. Mandating it wasn't an option, so I started small: three engineers who were curious, a few internal demos that showed concrete time saved rather than a pitch deck, and informal lunch sessions with no attendance requirement attached. I built reusable prompt templates and committed them to a shared repo, and I wrote up specific results, like the time a templated review cycle saved someone about two hours on a single PR, so people could see the win instead of taking my word for it. Within three months roughly 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted review and generation regularly, and I'd become the person people went to for it without ever being assigned the role. It was slower than a top-down rollout would have been, but engineers who opted in on their own terms ended up advocating for it to their peers in a way no mandate could have produced, and that stickiness is the part I'd repeat.

## 4. Integrating into a much larger org after an acquisition

Best for: navigating change / cross-functional collaboration / working inside an established engineering org as a founder used to owning everything.

When Benevity acquired Alaya in 2021 I went from a five-person engineering team to one roughly ten times that size overnight, with a different codebase, mostly Java microservices instead of our Node stack, and a different set of norms I hadn't written myself. My task was to fold Alaya's capabilities into Benevity's platform while keeping the product velocity we'd built without becoming the founder who shows up assuming his old playbook still applies. I spent the first month just mapping the existing architecture and meeting the platform and infra teams before proposing anything, which felt slow given how fast we'd always moved, but it meant that when I did push technical opinions people already trusted where they came from. That groundwork paid off: the core Alaya features migrated successfully, I contributed to three major cross-team architecture decisions, and I became the informal architecture liaison for the Europe-facing product surface. My honest reflection is that I moved too fast in the first few weeks trying to fix things that weren't actually broken in the new org, and slowing down to listen first is what turned me from an outsider into someone people wanted in the room.
