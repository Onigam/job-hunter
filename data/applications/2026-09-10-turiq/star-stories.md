# Behavioral Prep, Turiq (Technical Co-Founder / Founding Engineer)

## 1. Building Alaya from a blank page to acquisition

In 2016 I co-founded Alaya, an employee purpose and impact platform, and I was staring at a blank page. No customers yet, no product, just a signed pipeline of enterprise clients we needed to be ready for. I had to pick a stack and an architecture almost alone, knowing that whatever I chose in month one would either bend or break under whatever came in month twelve. I went with Node.js, React and Postgres, deliberately boring choices, and pushed hard to get a working demo in front of our first prospects within three months rather than polishing something nobody had seen yet. From there it was a steady expansion: multi-tenancy, an API layer, impact reporting, and eventually a small team of engineers I hired and onboarded myself, setting up code review and basic architecture docs as we went so the thing didn't live only in my head. By the time Benevity, a Canadian unicorn, acquired us in 2021, we had over 50 enterprise clients across Switzerland and France running on that platform. Looking back, I'd invest in observability much earlier than we did; we didn't have real APM until things were already big enough that debugging blind was painful. I'd also design for multi-tenancy from day zero instead of retrofitting it later, since that retrofit cost us more engineering time than almost anything else in year one.

## 2. Choosing a modular monolith over a rewrite under investor pressure

A couple of years into Alaya, our monolith was starting to crack under load, and the obvious conversation was whether to rewrite it as microservices. Investors were pushing for new features, not a multi-month rewrite with no visible output, so I had to make a call that would hold up technically without stalling the business. I ran a two-week spike comparing a full rewrite against a more targeted approach, backed it with actual load test numbers instead of opinions, and brought that to the CTO and investors directly. What I proposed instead was a strangler fig migration: pull out the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest of the monolith alone, and set clear criteria for when a module earned its own extraction. The first module shipped in six weeks and cut response times by around 40% on our busiest endpoints, and the rest of the monolith held up fine all the way through the acquisition, no big rewrite ever needed. The hard part wasn't the technical work, it was convincing a room that not rewriting everything was the disciplined choice rather than the lazy one. If I did it again I'd front-load even more benchmarking before that meeting, since the data ended the debate faster than any argument I made.

## 3. Landing inside a much bigger org after the acquisition

When Benevity acquired Alaya in 2021, I went from being one of a handful of engineers who knew everything to a Senior Staff Engineer on a team roughly ten times the size, with a different language, a Java microservices codebase, and processes I hadn't touched before. My job was to get Alaya's capabilities integrated into that platform while keeping up the product velocity we'd built a reputation on, without becoming the person who shows up and tries to fix everything on day two. I spent my first month mostly listening, mapping how the existing architecture actually worked before I proposed changing any of it, and building relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing any technical opinion their way. That patience paid off: I ended up contributing to three major cross-team architecture decisions and became the informal architecture liaison for the Europe-facing product surface. My honest reflection is that I moved too fast in the first few weeks trying to fix things that weren't actually broken, just unfamiliar, and slowing down to listen first is what made the rest of the integration work.

## 4. Getting two teams to adopt AI-assisted workflows without any authority to mandate it

A couple of years ago I noticed how much time engineers around me were losing to repetitive review and boilerplate work, and I wanted to change how two teams worked day to day even though neither reported to me and I had no mandate to tell anyone what tools to use. I started small, with three engineers who were already curious, and built quick internal demos that showed real time saved rather than just talking about potential. From there I ran informal lunch-and-learn sessions, wrote up reusable prompt templates and committed them to a shared repo so people could just take them rather than build their own from scratch. Within about three months, roughly 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted review and generation regularly, and I'd become the person people came to for that kind of tooling question across the org. It was slower than a top-down rollout would have been, but the engineers who picked it up on their own terms ended up being the ones advocating for it internally, which a mandate from above would never have produced. That's roughly the same bet I'd want to make at a small founding team: build something people actually want to use before you ask them to change how they work.
