# STAR Stories: Albert (Principal / Distinguished Engineer)

Selected for a principal-level role at a profitable, 20M-user fintech building net-new products from scratch. The mix leans on "built from zero," "made the hard architecture call," and "drove change without formal authority," since those map most directly to what a principal or distinguished engineer is expected to do without a manager forcing the outcome.

## 1. Building Alaya from zero to acquisition

In 2016 I co-founded Alaya, a purpose and impact platform for employee engagement. There was no product yet, no customers, just a blank page and a small team trying to figure out what to build first. My job was to architect and build the whole platform, alone at first and then with a growing team, while the business was already out signing its first enterprise clients, so there wasn't much room to get the foundation wrong.

I picked a pragmatic stack, Node.js, React, PostgreSQL, that could scale without forcing us into premature complexity, and got the core features to a working demo within three months. From there I expanded iteratively: multi-tenant architecture, an API layer, impact reporting, postal campaigns. I also hired and onboarded the first three engineers and set up CI/CD and a code review culture before either was strictly necessary, because retrofitting that stuff later is worse. The platform reached production with 50+ enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity, a Canadian unicorn valued north of a billion dollars, acquired the company in 2021. Looking back, I'd invest in observability earlier than we did; our APM was an afterthought for too long. I also underestimated what retrofitting multi-tenancy would cost. If I built something from zero again, that's a day-one decision, not a year-two one.

## 2. Making a hard architecture call under investor pressure

At Alaya, our monolith started showing scaling cracks right around the time investors were pushing hard for new features, not a rewrite. The team was split between a full microservices rewrite and a more modest modular monolith, and somebody needed to make a call that wouldn't blow up either the roadmap or the system.

I ran a two-week technical spike comparing both approaches with actual load tests instead of opinions, then presented the trade-offs to the CTO and investors with numbers they could argue with. What I proposed was a strangler fig migration: pull out the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest monolithic, and define clear criteria for when to extract the next one. The first extracted module shipped in six weeks and performance on our busiest endpoints improved about 40%. The rest of the monolith stayed stable straight through the acquisition; we never needed the big-bang rewrite everyone was afraid of. The hardest part honestly wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the disciplined choice. Data made that argument faster than I could have on my own. Next time I'd front-load more of that benchmarking so the debate doesn't eat two weeks before the decision even starts.

## 3. Driving adoption without being anyone's manager

At Benevity I was a senior staff IC with no direct reports, and I wanted two teams that didn't report to me to actually change how they worked, specifically to bring AI-assisted development into their daily workflow. There was no mandate I could reach for, so influence had to do all the work.

I started small, with three engineers who were already curious, and built internal demos that showed concrete time saved rather than talking about potential. From there it was informal lunch-and-learn sessions with no obligation attached, plus a shared repo of reusable prompt templates so people didn't have to reinvent them. I wrote up results as they came in, one story was a PR review cycle that dropped by about two hours, because a specific number travels further than a general claim. Within three months, roughly 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted review and generation regularly, and I ended up the informal go-to person for AI tooling across the org. It was slower than a top-down rollout would have been, but it stuck, because the engineers who adopted it did so on their own terms and then argued for it to their peers, which isn't something you can order into existence.

## 4. Integrating into a much larger org after acquisition

Joining Benevity after the Alaya acquisition in 2021 meant landing on a team roughly ten times the size of what I'd known, with a different codebase, Java and microservices instead of what I'd built, and a different culture entirely. The task was to fold Alaya's capabilities into Benevity's platform while still shipping new features, without losing the speed we'd had as a small team.

I spent the first month just mapping the existing architecture before I proposed anything, and I made a point of building relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing any technical opinions their way. From there I helped drive AI-augmented workflows across the org and became a cross-team architecture liaison for the Europe-facing product surface. The migration of core Alaya features into Benevity's platform succeeded, I contributed to three major cross-team architecture decisions, and I became the person two engineering teams went to for AI tooling questions. My honest reflection is that I moved too fast early on trying to fix things in the new org that weren't actually broken, just unfamiliar. Slowing down and listening first is what made the rest of it work, and it's a lesson I've carried into every new team since.
