# STAR Stories: Staff Backend Engineer, Nango

Picked with a small, remote engineering team in mind, one running a backend that has to stay reliable across 900+ APIs and a lot of traffic while still shipping AI-generated integration code that customers actually read and trust. These lean toward building infrastructure from a blank page, making an architecture call under pressure, working inside someone else's codebase without breaking stride, and driving change on a team that doesn't report to you, since that's the closest match to what a staff-level backend hire does day to day.

## Building Alaya's backend from zero

In 2016 I co-founded Alaya, an employee purpose and impact platform, with nothing built yet and an enterprise client already signed. My job was to architect and build the whole backend, alone at first, while the business kept selling around me. I picked Node.js, Express, and Postgres, a stack that could scale without turning into a science project, and pushed to get a working demo out in the first three months. From there it was steady iteration: a multi-tenant architecture, an API layer other services could build on, impact reporting, postal campaigns, plus hiring and onboarding the first three engineers and setting up the code review habits we kept for years afterward. We got to production serving over 50 enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity, a Canadian unicorn, acquired the company in 2021. If I did it again I'd invest in observability much earlier, we bolted on proper APM late, and I'd design the multi-tenancy in from day zero instead of retrofitting it under load. That's the kind of lesson that matters a lot when the product you're shipping is other people's integrations.

## A load-tested call instead of a rewrite

By year three, Alaya's monolith was starting to crack under load, and the team was split between a full microservices rewrite and a smaller modular approach, while investors wanted new features shipped, not months spent rebuilding. I needed a decision that would hold up technically without stalling the business. I ran a two-week spike comparing both approaches with real load tests, then walked the CTO and investors through the numbers instead of arguing opinions. What I proposed was a strangler fig migration: pull the two highest-traffic modules out first, leave the rest of the monolith alone, and define clear criteria for extracting anything further. The first module shipped in six weeks and cut latency on our busiest endpoints by roughly 40%, and the rest of the system stayed stable straight through the acquisition with no big rewrite ever needed. The hard part wasn't the engineering, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the right call, and the data ended up doing most of that convincing. Next time I'd front-load even more benchmarking so the argument takes less time to win.

## Learning someone else's backend fast

Joining Benevity after the acquisition meant landing on a team roughly ten times the size of Alaya's, with a Java and microservices codebase I hadn't touched before and conventions I didn't know yet. The task was to fold Alaya's capabilities into that platform while still shipping new features, without losing the speed that had made Alaya work in the first place. I spent the first month reading the architecture and talking to people before proposing a single change, which felt slow at the time but meant that once I did push an opinion, the platform and infra teams already trusted I understood what I was touching. We migrated the core Alaya features in successfully, and I ended up part of three major cross-team architecture decisions afterward. Looking back, I moved too fast early on trying to fix things that weren't actually broken in the new org, and slowing down to listen first is what turned that around. It's the kind of credibility that gets built the same slow way when customers are running their own security reviews on a backend they didn't write.

## Getting two teams to adopt AI tooling without a mandate

At Benevity I was a Senior Staff IC with no direct reports, but I wanted two teams outside my own to actually use AI-assisted development rather than treat it as a novelty. There was no mandate behind it, so I had to make people want it. I started with three curious engineers, built small internal demos that showed real time saved instead of talking about the idea in the abstract, and ran informal lunch sessions nobody was obligated to attend. I put together reusable prompt templates in a shared repo and wrote up concrete outcomes, one story was saving about two hours on a single PR review cycle. Within three months roughly 70% of engineers across both teams were using it regularly, and I became the informal lead for it across the org without ever holding the title. The slower, no-mandate approach produced people who advocated for the practice on their own terms, which a top-down rollout never would have gotten me. A fourteen-person engineering team doesn't have room for top-down mandates either, so this is close to how I'd expect to operate day to day.
