# STAR Stories: Staff Software Engineer (Java), SonarSource Identity Squad

Prepared for behavioral rounds. Picked for relevance to identity/access work, Java at scale, mentoring, and leading cross-team architecture without formal authority.

---

## 1. Post-acquisition integration into a Java microservices platform

This one usually comes up when they ask how I handle joining a much bigger, more established codebase. After Benevity acquired Alaya in 2021, I went from a small Node and Postgres platform I'd built myself to a Java and microservices system with an order of magnitude more engineers and a culture I didn't know yet. My job was to get Alaya's core features integrated into Benevity's platform without losing the product speed we'd had as a startup, and without breaking things for a much larger customer base. I spent the first month mostly reading and asking questions instead of proposing changes, which was hard for someone used to deciding everything alone. Once I understood the existing service boundaries, I started building relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing any technical opinions, and that's when I became the informal architecture liaison for our Europe-facing services. Over the next year I was part of three cross-team architecture decisions that touched shared services, the kind of decisions where getting the API contract wrong means five teams feel it. The features migrated cleanly and I ended up as the go-to person when questions about that part of the system came up. Looking back, I moved too fast in the first few weeks trying to fix things that weren't actually broken, just unfamiliar. Slowing down and mapping the system before touching it is the biggest thing I'd tell a younger version of myself, and it's exactly the instinct I'd bring to a squad that owns something as blast-radius-sensitive as identity.

## 2. A hard architecture call under investor pressure

Good for questions about trade-offs or technical debt, and relevant here because identity infrastructure lives or dies on getting the extraction boundaries right. At Alaya, our monolith was starting to crack under load from our first big enterprise clients, and the team was split between a full microservices rewrite and just patching the monolith. Investors wanted new features, not a rewrite, so I couldn't just pick the technically prettiest option. I ran a two-week spike comparing both approaches with actual load tests instead of opinions, then presented the numbers to the CTO and investors directly. What we landed on was a strangler-fig migration: pull out the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest alone, and define clear criteria for when to extract the next piece rather than promising a full rewrite timeline nobody could commit to. The first extracted module shipped in six weeks and performance improved about 40% on our busiest endpoints. The rest of the monolith stayed stable all the way through the acquisition, so we never needed the big rewrite everyone was scared of. The hardest part wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the disciplined choice. If I did it again I'd push for the benchmarking spike even earlier, because the data ended the debate faster than any argument I made.

## 3. Driving AI-assisted engineering adoption with no authority to mandate it

This maps directly to the Codex and Claude line in the job posting, and it's also my best answer for influence without authority. At Benevity I was an individual contributor with no direct reports, but I wanted two teams that didn't report to me to actually change how they worked and bring AI tooling into daily development. I started with three engineers who were already curious rather than trying to convince skeptics first, built small internal demos that showed concrete time saved instead of talking in the abstract, and ran informal lunch sessions with no mandate attached, just a working example. I put together reusable prompt templates and committed them to a shared repo so the habit would outlast the demos. Within about three months, something like 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted review and generation regularly, and I'd become the person people asked when they wanted to get started. It was slower than a top-down rollout would have been, but it stuck, because people who adopted it on their own terms ended up advocating for it themselves. That's the version of leadership I'd want to bring to mentoring inside a squad, not telling people what to do but making the better way obvious enough that they choose it.

## 4. Building a platform from zero with no domain expert to lean on

Useful for ambiguity questions and for demonstrating end-to-end ownership, which matters for a role that's expected to lead delivery of critical features with minimal supervision. In 2016 I cofounded Alaya with literally nothing built yet, no customers, no existing architecture to inherit, and no one around who'd solved this particular problem before. I had to pick a stack that could scale without over-engineering for a company that didn't have product-market fit yet, so I went with Node, React, and Postgres rather than something more fashionable, and got a working demo in front of the first prospective client within three months. From there it was constant iteration: multi-tenant architecture once we had more than one enterprise customer, an API layer, impact reporting, and eventually hiring and onboarding the first three engineers while setting up CI/CD and a code review culture from scratch. By the time Benevity acquired us, the platform was running in production for more than fifty enterprise clients across Switzerland and France. If I'm honest, I underestimated how expensive retrofitting multi-tenancy would be once we already had paying customers, and I'd design for it from day zero next time rather than bolting it on later. That lesson is a big part of why I take service boundaries and access control seriously from the start now, not as an afterthought.

## 5. First month mapping an unfamiliar system before touching it

Short version of a pattern that keeps coming up, useful if they want a quick example rather than a long story. Any time I've joined a system I didn't build, whether that was the Benevity codebase or an internal tool at Alaya inherited from a contractor, I default to spending real time reading before I propose anything. At Benevity that meant tracing how requests actually flowed through the Java services before I opened my mouth about what should change. It's a small habit but it's saved me from several bad first impressions and at least one architecture proposal that would have broken an assumption three other teams were relying on. For identity work specifically, where a wrong assumption about a trust boundary isn't just embarrassing but a security problem, that habit matters more than usual.
