# STAR Stories: Head of Software Engineering @ Adaptyv Biosystems

Picked for a player-coach leadership role: roadmap ownership, architecture across a hardware/software boundary, team-building, and driving AI-assisted development. Source: profile/star-bank.md.

## 1. Building Alaya from zero to acquisition

In 2016 I co-founded Alaya, an employee purpose and impact platform, and we started with nothing, no product, no customers, just a blank page and a business that was already trying to sign enterprise clients. My job was to architect and build the whole thing, alone at first. I picked a pragmatic stack, Node.js, React, PostgreSQL, deliberately avoiding anything clever that would slow down a first demo, and got the core product working inside three months. From there it was iterative: multi-tenant architecture, an API layer, impact reporting, postal campaign tooling, and along the way hiring the first three engineers and setting up the basics, CI/CD, code review, some architecture docs so decisions didn't live only in my head. By the time Benevity acquired us in 2021, the platform was running production workloads for more than fifty enterprise clients across Switzerland and France. Looking back, I'd invest in observability much earlier; we bolted on proper APM late and paid for it in debugging time. I also underestimated how expensive it would be to retrofit multi-tenancy after the fact. If I were starting Adaptyv's platform today, that's a decision I'd make on day one rather than day two hundred.

## 2. A hard architecture call under investor pressure

At Alaya, our monolith started showing real scaling cracks, and the team split into two camps: rewrite as microservices, or keep it as a modular monolith. Investors wanted new features, not a rewrite, so I couldn't just pick a side on instinct. I ran a two-week technical spike, built load-test prototypes of both approaches, and brought the CTO and investors numbers instead of opinions. The plan I proposed was a strangler-fig migration: extract only the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest alone, and define clear criteria for when to pull the next piece out. The first module shipped in six weeks, performance improved 40% on the busiest endpoints, and the rest of the monolith stayed stable straight through the acquisition. No big-bang rewrite, no six-month feature freeze. The hard part wasn't the technical spike, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the disciplined choice. Data shortened that argument. Next time I'd run the early benchmarks even sooner, before the debate had a chance to calcify into camps.

## 3. Leading adoption without formal authority

At Benevity I was a Senior Staff IC with no direct reports, on a team ten times the size of anything I'd worked in before, and I wanted two teams that didn't report to me to actually change how they worked by bringing AI tooling into their daily development. Mandating it wasn't an option and probably wouldn't have stuck anyway. So I started small: three curious engineers, a few internal demos showing concrete time saved, informal lunch sessions with no agenda beyond "here's what this looks like." I built reusable prompt templates and committed them to a shared repo, and wrote up results in plain terms, like the PR review cycle that dropped by two hours. Within three months, about 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted review and generation, and I'd become the person people asked when they wanted to get started. It was slower than a top-down rollout would have been, but it stuck, because people who adopt something on their own terms end up defending it to others. That's the version I'd want to run again at a five-person engineering team deciding how AI agents fit into the workflow.

## 4. Integrating into a much bigger, unfamiliar codebase

When Benevity acquired Alaya in 2021, I went from being the person who'd written most of the code to one engineer on a team ten times larger, working in Java and microservices instead of the Node stack I knew cold. My task was to get Alaya's capabilities folded into Benevity's platform without losing the speed we'd built the company on. I spent the first month doing nothing but mapping their existing architecture, resisting the urge to propose changes before I understood why things were built the way they were, and built relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing any technical opinion. That patience paid off: the core Alaya features migrated in, I contributed to three major cross-team architecture decisions, and became the informal bridge for AI tooling adoption across the org. I moved too fast early on, trying to fix things in the new codebase that weren't actually broken, just unfamiliar. Slowing down and listening first is the thing I'd tell myself to do from week one, not week five.
