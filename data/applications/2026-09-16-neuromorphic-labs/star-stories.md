# STAR+R Stories: Founding Engineer, Full Stack @ Neuromorphic Labs

Picked for a founding-engineer, seed-stage, infra-trust context: building from zero, making architecture calls that need to hold under real load, and driving adoption of new practices without a mandate.

## Story 1: Building Alaya from a blank page to acquisition

Back in 2016 I co-founded Alaya, an employee purpose and impact platform, and there was genuinely nothing to start from: no customers yet, no product, just a thesis and a deadline because the business was already talking to its first enterprise clients. My job was to architect and build the whole thing, alone at first, in a way that wouldn't collapse once real usage showed up. I picked a pragmatic stack, Node.js, React, PostgreSQL, that let me move fast without boxing myself in, got a working demo out in about three months, and then kept expanding it into a multi-tenant system with an API layer and impact reporting as the client list grew. Once we had traction I hired the first three engineers and put in CI/CD and a real code review culture before the team got big enough that skipping it would have hurt. The platform ended up running production for over fifty enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity, a Canadian unicorn, acquired the company in 2021, which felt like the clearest possible validation of the early calls. If I did it again I'd invest in observability much sooner; we didn't have proper APM until late, and I underestimated how expensive it would be to retrofit multi-tenancy instead of designing for it from day one.

## Story 2: Choosing a strangler-fig migration over a rewrite

At Alaya our monolith started showing real scaling cracks right as investors were pushing hard for new features, not a rewrite, so the team was split between a full microservices rewrite and something more incremental. I needed a decision that would hold up technically without stalling the roadmap the business actually needed. I ran a two-week spike comparing both approaches with real load tests, then brought the CTO and investors numbers instead of opinions, and proposed extracting just the two highest-traffic modules using a strangler-fig pattern while leaving the rest of the monolith alone, with clear criteria for when to peel off the next piece. We shipped the first extracted module in six weeks, the busiest endpoints got roughly 40% faster, and the remaining monolith stayed stable all the way through the acquisition, so we never needed the big-bang rewrite people were originally arguing for. The hard part wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the right call, and having the benchmark data ready made that argument much shorter than it would have been otherwise.

## Story 3: Driving AI tooling adoption with no authority to mandate it

After joining Benevity as a Senior Staff IC, I wanted two teams that didn't report to me to actually change how they worked and start using AI-assisted development practices, and I had no formal authority to make that happen. I started small: three engineers who were already curious, a few internal demos that showed concrete time saved rather than a slide about the future of AI, and informal lunch sessions instead of anything that looked like a mandate. I built reusable prompt templates, committed them to a shared repo, and documented specific wins, like one story where a two-hour PR review cycle dropped substantially. Within three months about 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted review and generation, and I became the person people asked when they wanted to get started with it. It was slower than a top-down rollout would have been, but it stuck, since the engineers who adopted it on their own terms were the ones who ended up advocating for it internally, and I don't think I could have manufactured that with a decree.

## Story 4: Integrating into a much larger, unfamiliar codebase after acquisition

When Benevity acquired Alaya in 2021 I landed on a team roughly ten times the size of what I'd worked in, with a different stack, mostly Java and microservices, and different processes than anything I'd built myself. My task was to fold Alaya's capabilities into that platform while still shipping new features, without losing the velocity that had gotten us acquired in the first place. I spent the first month just mapping the existing architecture before proposing anything, and deliberately built relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing any technical opinions their way. From there I ended up migrating the core Alaya features into Benevity's platform and became a cross-team architecture liaison for the Europe-facing product surface, contributing to three major architecture decisions along the way. Early on I moved too fast trying to fix things that weren't actually broken in the new org, just unfamiliar, and slowing down to listen first is what turned that around and made the rest of the integration go smoothly.
