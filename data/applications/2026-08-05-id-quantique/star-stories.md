# STAR Stories: Staff DevSecOps Engineer @ ID Quantique

## 1. Building CI/CD, code review culture and architecture standards from zero

Best for: internal developer platforms, establishing engineering standards, "how do you bootstrap a delivery pipeline with nothing in place."

When I co-founded Alaya in 2016 there was no pipeline, no review process, nothing to inherit. I picked Node.js, React and PostgreSQL because I could move fast without over-building infrastructure the company didn't need yet, and spent the first three months getting to a working demo alone. Once the first three engineers joined, my job shifted from writing features to giving them something to work inside of: I set up CI/CD, wrote the first code review guidelines, and started keeping architecture docs current instead of letting decisions live only in my head. None of that was glamorous, but it's the reason a team that size could ship to fifty-plus enterprise clients across Switzerland and France without the codebase turning into a liability. What I got wrong was waiting too long on observability, we ran on ad hoc logging and gut feel well past the point where proper instrumentation would have caught incidents in minutes instead of hours. If I set up a platform from scratch again, monitoring and audit trails go in on day one, not after the first outage makes the case for me.

## 2. Choosing a strangler-fig migration over a full rewrite under delivery pressure

Best for: release engineering trade-offs, risk management in a live pipeline, technical decisions defended with data rather than opinion.

By our third or fourth year at Alaya, the monolith was starting to buckle under real production load, and the team was split between a full microservices rewrite and just absorbing the pain longer. Investors wanted new features shipped, not a multi-month infrastructure project with nothing visible to show for it, so whatever I proposed had to hold up technically without stalling the roadmap. I ran a two-week spike comparing both paths with actual load tests, not opinions, then took the results to the CTO and investors. The plan I landed on was a strangler-fig extraction: pull the two highest-traffic modules out first, leave the rest alone, and set explicit criteria for extracting anything further. The first module shipped in six weeks and latency on the busiest endpoints improved by roughly 40%. The remaining monolith stayed stable straight through the acquisition, and we never did the big-bang rewrite people were bracing for. The harder part wasn't the technical work, it was convincing a room that the less dramatic option was the responsible one. Data did that job better than I could have. Next time I'd front-load the benchmarking so that debate doesn't eat as much calendar time.

## 3. Driving adoption of AI-assisted engineering workflows with no mandate

Best for: agentic automation and AI-enabled workflow adoption, influence without authority, mentoring across teams that don't report to you.

At Benevity I wanted AI-assisted development practices adopted across two teams that had no obligation to listen to me and no manager telling them to. Ordering people to change how they worked wasn't an option, and it wouldn't have stuck even if it were, so I started with three engineers who were already curious. I built small internal demos showing concrete time saved, ran informal sessions with no mandate attached, and wrote reusable prompt templates that I committed to a shared repo so people could take what worked and skip what didn't. I also documented specific wins, like one engineer saving a couple of hours on a single PR review cycle, because a vague claim of "this helps" convinces nobody. Within about three months, something like 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted review and generation regularly, and I'd become the person people came to with AI tooling questions without anyone appointing me to that role. It was slower than a top-down rollout would have been, but the engineers who adopted it on their own terms ended up advocating for it internally in a way a mandate never produces. That's the same pattern I'd want for rolling out agentic automation in a delivery pipeline: prove the time saved before asking anyone to trust it with something that touches production.

## 4. Establishing cross-team standards after an acquisition, without formal authority

Best for: driving organizational engineering standards, working across teams and codebases you don't own, navigating a much larger org.

After Benevity acquired Alaya in 2021, I joined as Senior Staff Engineer on a team roughly ten times larger than what I'd worked in before, a different codebase, different processes, and no direct reports. The job was to fold Alaya's capabilities into Benevity's platform without slowing down on new features and without stepping on toes I didn't yet understand the shape of. I spent the first month mostly listening, mapping the existing architecture before proposing anything, and building relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing any technical opinion their way. That patience paid off: I became the cross-team architecture liaison for the Europe-facing product surface and the person other teams looped in before a change touching a Tier 1 service went out. Early on I did move too fast trying to fix things that weren't actually broken, just unfamiliar to me, and that cost some credibility I had to rebuild. The lesson was that standards only stick when the people already doing the work trust that you understand what they're protecting, and that trust has to be earned before you get to set any rules.
