# STAR Stories: Clera, Founding Engineer (Backend & Platform)

Picked for a founding-engineer, backend-heavy, AI-native ops role: building from zero, a hard architecture call under pressure, driving AI tooling adoption without authority, and navigating a much bigger org after an acquisition. Each one maps to something Clera will hit early: designing the core backend alone, deciding when to split a monolith as more firms come on, and getting an AI-assisted workflow trusted by people who didn't ask for it.

## 1. Building Alaya's platform from a blank page

In 2016 I co-founded Alaya with no customers, no product, and no existing code to lean on. My job was to architect and build the whole platform, on my own at first, while the business was already signing its first enterprise clients, so there wasn't room for a slow ramp. I picked a pragmatic stack, Node.js, React, PostgreSQL, that could get us to a working demo fast without locking us into something we'd regret later, and had core features live within three months. From there I kept expanding: a multi-tenant architecture, an API layer, impact reporting, and eventually the first three engineering hires, along with the CI/CD setup and code review habits they'd inherit. The platform ended up running production for 50-plus enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity, a Canadian unicorn, acquired the company in 2021. Looking back, I'd design for multi-tenancy from day zero instead of retrofitting it later, and I'd put real observability in place months earlier than I did. Both would have saved weeks of debugging blind.

## 2. Choosing a strangler-fig migration over a rewrite

A couple years into Alaya, our monolith was starting to strain under real traffic, and the team split into two camps: rewrite as microservices, or keep going as a modular monolith. Investors wanted new features, not a multi-month rewrite with no visible output. I had to make a call that held up technically without stalling the roadmap, so I ran a two-week spike comparing both approaches with actual load tests, then took the CTO and investors through the trade-offs using numbers instead of opinions. The plan I proposed was a strangler-fig extraction: pull out the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest alone, and set clear criteria for when to extract anything further. The first module shipped in six weeks, latency on our busiest endpoints dropped by around 40 percent, and the rest of the monolith stayed stable all the way through the acquisition. No big-bang rewrite ever happened. The hardest part wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the right call; having load-test data ready before the debate started would have made that conversation shorter.

## 3. Getting AI-assisted engineering adopted without a mandate

At Benevity I was a senior staff IC with no direct reports, on a team roughly ten times the size of Alaya, and I wanted two teams that didn't answer to me to actually change how they worked by folding AI tooling into their daily workflow. Since I had no authority to require anything, I started small: three curious engineers, a couple of internal demos showing real time saved on things like PR review, and informal lunch sessions with no mandate attached. I built reusable prompt templates, committed them to a shared repo, and wrote up concrete results as they came in rather than promising them upfront. Within three months, about 70 percent of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted review and generation on their own, and I'd become the person people asked when they wanted to get started. It was slower than a top-down rollout would have been, but it stuck, because people who opted in on their own terms ended up selling it to their teammates for me.

## 4. Learning a new codebase before pushing changes

Right after Benevity acquired Alaya in 2021, I joined a team with a different culture, a different codebase in Java and microservices, and different processes than anything I'd worked in before. I needed to fold Alaya's capabilities into that platform while still shipping new features, without losing the pace we'd built as a five-person team. Instead of leading with opinions, I spent the first month just mapping the existing architecture, and I built relationships with the platform and infra teams before proposing anything. That groundwork let me become a cross-team architecture liaison for the Europe-facing product surface and contribute to three major cross-team decisions once I actually had context that mattered. My first instinct had been to fix things that looked broken to an outsider but weren't, and slowing down to listen first is what made the rest of the integration work.
