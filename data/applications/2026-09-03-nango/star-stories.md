# STAR Stories - Nango, Staff Backend Engineer

Picked for what a staff backend hire on a small team actually gets asked about: owning an API layer end to end, making a defensible call under delivery pressure, working inside someone else's backend without breaking it, and moving engineers without having authority over them.

## Story 1 - Building Alaya's backend from a blank page

In 2016 I co-founded Alaya, a purpose and impact platform for employee engagement, and there was no backend to inherit, nothing to extend. My job was to design and build the whole API layer, alone at first and then with a small team, while the business was already signing its first enterprise clients, so there wasn't room to over-engineer or wait for a clean slate. I picked a stack that could scale without dragging in complexity we didn't need yet, Node.js, Express, PostgreSQL, shipped the core API inside three months to get to a first demo, then expanded it iteratively into a multi-tenant architecture with proper auth boundaries between customers. I also hired and onboarded the first three engineers and set up CI/CD and a real code review habit, because an API only stays coherent if more than one person understands why an endpoint is shaped the way it is. That backend ended up serving 50+ enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and in 2021 Benevity, a Canadian unicorn, acquired the company, which was about as direct a validation of those early API decisions as you can get. If I did it again I'd invest in observability much earlier; we bolted on proper request tracing late and spent weeks after the fact reconstructing what had actually gone wrong in production.

## Story 2 - Choosing the load-tested answer over the rewrite

At Alaya, our backend started showing real scaling cracks right as investors were pushing hard for new features, not months of infrastructure work. The team was split between a full microservices rewrite and something more modest, and I needed a decision that would hold up technically without stalling the roadmap everyone else cared about. Instead of arguing opinions in a room, I ran a two-week technical spike with prototype load tests comparing both approaches, then took the results to the CTO and investors as numbers rather than a pitch. What came out of that was a strangler fig migration: extract the two highest-traffic endpoints first, leave the rest of the monolith alone, and set clear criteria for when to peel off the next piece. The first extraction shipped in six weeks, latency on the busiest endpoints dropped about 40%, and the remaining backend stayed stable all the way through the acquisition, no big-bang rewrite required. The hard part honestly wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the disciplined choice rather than the lazy one. Data did most of that convincing, and next time I'd push for that early benchmarking sooner just to shorten the debate.

## Story 3 - Working inside a backend I didn't build

The first month after the Benevity acquisition, I made a point of not proposing anything. I spent that time mapping their existing Java microservices architecture, a codebase and set of backend conventions I had no hand in designing, before forming opinions about what should change. Once I understood how the pieces actually fit together, my task became integrating Alaya's API capabilities into that platform while keeping the product velocity we'd built without breaking what already worked for the larger org. I built relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing any technical position, and that groundwork is what let me become a cross-team architecture liaison for the Europe-facing product surface later on. Alaya's core features migrated into Benevity's backend successfully, I contributed to three major cross-team architecture decisions, and I became a go-to person for questions that crossed team boundaries. My first instinct after the acquisition was to move fast and fix things that, it turned out, weren't actually broken in the new context, just different. Slowing down and listening before touching anything made me considerably more effective once I did start changing things.

## Story 4 - Driving change on teams that didn't report to me

After the acquisition I joined Benevity as a Senior Staff engineer on a team roughly ten times the size of what I'd worked with before, running on Java instead of the stack I knew, with no direct reports and no formal authority to change how anyone worked. I wanted to get AI-assisted development practices adopted across two teams that had no reason to listen to me just because I thought it was a good idea. So I started small: found three curious engineers, built internal demos that showed concrete time saved rather than talking about potential, ran informal lunch sessions with no mandate attached, and put together reusable prompt templates in a shared repo so the win was easy to copy. Within three months about 70% of engineers on both teams were using AI-assisted review and generation day to day, and I ended up as the informal lead for that tooling across the org without ever being asked to take the title. It was slower than a top-down rollout would have been, but people who opt in on their own terms end up defending the change to others, which a decree from a manager never really achieves.
