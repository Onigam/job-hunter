# STAR Stories: Aleph, Staff Fullstack Engineer, Applied AI

Picked for a role that's fullstack, data-heavy (GCP, BigQuery, Mongo), and applied-AI-facing, where ownership from data model to shipped feature matters more than any one framework.

---

## Story 1: Building Alaya from zero, owning the whole stack

In 2016 I co-founded Alaya, a purpose and impact platform for employee engagement, and I was the one who had to turn a blank page into something enterprise clients would trust with their data. There was no legacy system to lean on and no existing team to hand pieces off to, so the task was to architect and build the entire platform myself at first, then bring a team in without slowing down the business, which was already signing its first clients. I picked a pragmatic stack, Node.js, React, PostgreSQL, that could scale without over-engineering for problems we didn't have yet, got the core features to a working demo in three months, and then expanded iteratively into multi-tenancy, an API layer, and impact reporting as real usage demanded it. I hired and onboarded the first three engineers along the way and set up CI/CD and a code review culture before it became urgent. The platform reached production with more than 50 enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity, a Canadian unicorn valued north of a billion dollars, acquired the company in 2021. Looking back, I'd invest in observability much earlier; we bolted on proper APM late, and multi-tenancy retrofitting cost more than designing for it from day one would have.

## Story 2: A hard architecture call under investor pressure

A few years into Alaya, the monolith started showing real scaling cracks right as investors were pushing hard for new features, not a rewrite. My task was to make a technical trade-off I could defend to both engineers and the board, not just pick a side and hope. I ran a two-week spike comparing a full microservices rewrite against a modular monolith, backed by actual load tests instead of opinions, and presented the trade-offs to the CTO and investors with numbers on the table. From there I proposed and led a strangler-fig migration: extract only the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest alone, and define clear criteria for when to extract more. The first module shipped in six weeks and cut latency on our busiest endpoints by roughly 40 percent, and the remaining monolith stayed stable straight through the acquisition, so we never needed the big-bang rewrite everyone was afraid of. The hardest part honestly wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the disciplined choice; more upfront benchmarking would have shortened that debate.

## Story 3: Driving AI-tooling adoption with no authority to mandate it

After Benevity acquired Alaya, I was a senior staff IC with no direct reports, but I wanted two teams that didn't report to me to actually change how they worked and adopt AI-assisted development. Since I couldn't hand down a mandate, the task became proving the value informally, one engineer at a time, and letting the results do the persuading. I started with three curious early adopters, built small internal demos showing concrete time saved, and ran lunch-and-learn sessions with zero mandate attached. I also documented wins as they happened, things like shaving two hours off a single PR review cycle, and put reusable prompt templates in a shared repo anyone could pull from. Within three months about 70 percent of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted review and generation, and I'd become the informal go-to person for it across the org. It was slower than a top-down rollout would have been, but engineers who opted in on their own terms ended up advocating for it themselves, which a decree from above never would have produced.

## Story 4: Integrating into a much bigger, unfamiliar codebase without losing momentum

Joining Benevity post-acquisition meant landing on a team roughly ten times the size of Alaya, working in Java microservices instead of the Node stack I'd built for years. My task was to integrate Alaya's capabilities into that platform while still shipping new features, without the product velocity we'd built taking a hit. I spent the first month mostly listening, mapping the existing architecture before proposing anything, and built relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing technical opinions on people who didn't know me yet. That groundwork let me become a cross-team architecture liaison for the Europe-facing product surface and contribute to three major architecture decisions once I actually understood the constraints. The Alaya features migrated in successfully and the velocity held. My first instinct, honestly, was to move fast and start fixing things that weren't actually broken in the new org; slowing down and listening first is what made the rest of it work.
