# STAR Stories: The Outdoor Builders (Founding Full Stack & AI Engineer)

Selected for the founding-engineer, AI-heavy, SaaS-transition context of this role.

## 1. Building Alaya from zero to acquisition

In 2016 I cofounded Alaya, a purpose and impact platform for employee engagement, and there was nothing to work with at the start: no customers, no product, just a blank page and a signed first enterprise client waiting on a delivery date. My job was to architect and build the whole thing myself, then grow a team around it while the business kept signing new accounts. I picked a pragmatic stack, Node.js, React, PostgreSQL, that could scale without forcing me into premature architecture decisions, and got the core features to a first demo inside three months. From there I built out the multi-tenant layer, an API for integrations, and impact reporting, and I hired and onboarded the first three engineers, setting up CI/CD and a review culture along the way. The platform ended up with more than 50 enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity, a Canadian unicorn valued north of a billion dollars, acquired the company in 2021. Looking back, I'd invest in observability much earlier since we didn't add proper APM until things were already painful, and I'd design for multi-tenancy from day one instead of retrofitting it later, which cost weeks I didn't need to lose.

## 2. Making a hard architecture call under investor pressure

A couple of years into Alaya, the monolith started showing real scaling cracks, and the team split between rewriting everything as microservices and just patching the modular monolith we had. Investors wanted new features shipped, not months spent on a rewrite, so I had to find a defensible middle path fast. I ran a two-week spike comparing both approaches with actual load tests rather than opinions, then brought the CTO and investors concrete numbers instead of a pitch. What I proposed was a strangler-fig migration: pull out the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest of the monolith alone, and set clear criteria for when to extract the next piece. The first module shipped in six weeks and performance on our busiest endpoints improved about 40%, and the remaining monolith held steady all the way through the acquisition with no big-bang rewrite ever needed. The hard part wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the right call, and having the load-test data ready made that argument a lot shorter than it would have been otherwise.

## 3. Driving AI-assisted engineering adoption with no formal authority

At Benevity I was a senior staff IC with no direct reports, but I wanted two teams that didn't answer to me to start using AI tooling in their day-to-day workflow. Mandating it wasn't an option, so I started small: three engineers who were already curious became the first users, and I built internal demos that showed real time saved on actual PRs rather than abstract claims. I ran informal lunch-and-learn sessions with no mandate behind them, just working examples, and put together a shared repo of prompt templates anyone could pull from. Within three months about 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted review and generation as part of their normal flow, and I ended up the informal go-to person for AI tooling across the org. The slower, no-mandate approach turned out to be stickier than a top-down rollout would have been, since the people who adopted it on their own terms were the ones telling their teammates to try it too.

## 4. Owning a product end to end as a founding engineer

Alaya put me in the position most founding-engineer roles ask about directly: I was the only engineer for the first stretch, responsible for the full stack from database schema to the UI a client would actually click through during a sales demo. There was no backend team to hand off to and no design team to wait on, so decisions about data model, API shape, and user flow all landed on me at once, usually with a sales call already booked. I kept scope tight on purpose, shipping the minimum that let the sales team close the first few contracts, then used what those early clients actually did with the product to decide what to build next instead of guessing. That discipline is what got a working platform in front of paying enterprise clients within the first year, and it's the same discipline I'd bring to compressing your customer journey work: build the smallest version that gets a contractor from job-site visit to signed quote, watch what they do with it, then expand from there rather than speculating upfront.

## 5. Translating a single-workflow tool into a multi-account platform

The retrofit I mentioned in the architecture story deserves its own telling, because it's close to what a SaaS transition actually requires. Alaya started as a tool built around one client's workflow, and as more enterprise accounts signed on, I had to turn that single-tenant design into something that could onboard a new client without me touching the schema each time. I mapped every place tenant assumptions were baked into the data model and the API layer, then rebuilt the access and data-isolation logic incrementally, client by client, so the platform never went down for existing accounts while the migration was happening. It took longer than a clean rewrite would have on paper, but it meant we never had to freeze feature work to do it, and by the time of the acquisition the platform was onboarding new enterprise clients in days rather than the weeks it used to take. If I were doing it again, I'd build the tenant boundary into the schema from the very first migration, since almost all of the retrofit cost came from assumptions baked into early tables that nobody thought to question at the time.
