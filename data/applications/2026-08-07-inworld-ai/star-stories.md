# STAR Stories: Inworld AI, Staff/Principal ML Engineer, Serving

## Learning a new codebase before touching it

The month right after the Benevity acquisition taught me something about pace that I still lean on. I could have walked in with opinions about the Java microservices architecture I'd just landed in, since I had plenty from years of building Alaya, but I spent that first month mapping how the existing system actually worked and building relationships with the platform and infra teams before proposing a single change. That patience is what let me become the cross-team architecture liaison for the Europe-facing product surface later, and contribute to three architecture decisions that stuck. Early on I did try to fix a couple of things that weren't broken, just unfamiliar to me, and got politely pushed back on both. Slowing down and listening first turned out to matter more than being right fast, and it's the same instinct I'd bring to an unfamiliar serving stack: map it before I touch it.

## The architecture call under investor pressure

This is the one I reach for when someone asks about a hard technical trade-off. At Alaya, a few years into building the platform, our monolith started cracking under load and the team split into two camps: rewrite everything as microservices, or keep shipping features and deal with it later. Investors wanted the second option, and a full rewrite would have been the easy answer that impressed nobody. I ran a two-week spike instead, load testing both approaches against the two highest-traffic modules rather than arguing about it in the abstract, then brought the numbers to the CTO and investors. What we landed on was a strangler fig migration: pull the two busiest modules out first, leave the rest of the monolith alone, and set clear criteria for extracting the next one. The first module shipped in six weeks and cut latency 40% on our worst endpoints, and the monolith held up fine through the acquisition without ever needing the big rewrite. The hardest part wasn't the technical work, it was convincing a room that "don't rewrite everything" was the disciplined choice. Next time I'd run the benchmarks even earlier, since the data closed the debate faster than any argument did.

## Building something from nothing

In 2016 I co-founded Alaya with a blank page and no customers, and my job was to architect and build the entire platform pretty much alone at first while the business tried to close its first enterprise clients around me. I picked Node, React and Postgres because it was pragmatic rather than fashionable, something that could scale without forcing me to over-engineer before there was even a product to validate. The first three months went into getting a working demo in front of prospects, then I expanded iteratively into multi-tenancy, an API layer, and reporting as real usage told me what mattered. Once we had traction I hired the first three engineers and had to figure out, in real time, what code review and CI/CD should look like for a team that size. Five years later we had over 50 enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity acquired us, which is about as close to external validation of those early calls as it gets. What I'd change is observability. We bolted on proper APM late, and multi-tenancy was retrofitted rather than designed in from day zero, which cost more than it should have.

## Owning architecture without owning the org chart

After the Benevity acquisition I landed on a team roughly ten times the size of Alaya, different codebase, different stack, different culture, and no formal authority over any of it. My task was to help drive adoption of AI-assisted engineering practices across two teams that had zero reporting line to me. Rather than push a mandate nobody asked for, I started with three engineers who were already curious, built small demos that showed real time savings on things like PR review cycles, and ran informal lunch sessions with no agenda beyond "here's what this saved me." I also put together shared prompt templates and committed them somewhere the whole team could find and improve on. Within three months about 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted review and generation day to day, and I ended up the informal go-to person for it across the org. It was slower than a top-down rollout would have been, but it stuck, because people adopted it on their own terms instead of being told to.
