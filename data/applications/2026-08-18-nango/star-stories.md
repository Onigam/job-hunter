# STAR Stories: Staff Backend Engineer, Nango

Picked for a small backend team that just replaced Temporal with a homegrown Postgres orchestrator and is scaling that system, plus enterprise customers who need to trust it during security reviews. These lean toward architecture trade-offs under pressure, building core infrastructure from nothing, and driving change on a team without formal authority over it, since that's closest to what a staff-level backend hire actually does here.

## A hard architecture call under investor pressure

At Alaya, our monolith was starting to crack under load, and the team was split between a full microservices rewrite and a more modest modular monolith, while investors wanted new features shipped, not months spent on a rewrite. I had to make a call that held up technically without stalling the business. I ran a two-week spike comparing both approaches with actual load tests, then took the CTO and investors through the trade-offs with numbers instead of opinions. What I proposed was a strangler fig migration: pull out the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest of the monolith alone, and set clear criteria for when we'd extract the next piece. The first module shipped in six weeks and cut latency by about 40% on our busiest endpoints, and the rest of the monolith stayed stable straight through the acquisition, no big rewrite ever needed. The hardest part honestly wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the right call. The data did most of that convincing for me, and next time I'd front-load even more benchmarking to shorten that argument. It's the same shape of decision I read into your Temporal to Postgres move, pick the boring option once you've actually measured it, not the one that looks better on a slide.

## Building Alaya from zero to acquisition

In 2016 I co-founded Alaya, a purpose and impact platform for employee engagement, with nothing behind it, no customers, no product, just a blank page and a first enterprise client we'd already signed before we'd built anything for them. My job was to architect and build the whole thing, alone at first, while the business kept selling. I picked a stack that could scale without me over-engineering it on day one, Node.js, React, Postgres, and pushed to get a working demo in the first three months. From there it was iteration: multi-tenant architecture, an API layer, impact reporting, postal campaigns, and along the way hiring and onboarding the first three engineers and setting up the code review culture we'd run on for years. We got to production with roughly 50 enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and in 2021 Benevity, a Canadian unicorn, acquired the company. Looking back, I'd invest in observability a lot earlier than we did, we bolted on proper APM late, and I underestimated how expensive retrofitting multi-tenancy would be. If I did it again I'd design for it from day zero instead of treating it as a later problem, which matters a lot for a company whose product is other people's integrations.

## Integrating after the acquisition

The acquisition wasn't the end of the hard part, it was the start of a different one. I joined Benevity as a Senior Staff Engineer on a team roughly ten times the size of Alaya's, a different codebase in Java and microservices, and a culture I didn't know yet. My task was to fold Alaya's capabilities into their platform while still shipping new features, without losing the speed that made Alaya work in the first place. I spent the first month just reading the architecture and talking to people before I suggested a single change, which felt slow at the time but paid off, because by the time I did push opinions, the platform and infra teams already trusted that I understood what I was proposing to touch. We migrated the core Alaya features in successfully, and I ended up part of three major cross-team architecture calls. My honest reflection is that I moved too fast early on trying to fix things that weren't actually broken in the new org, and slowing down to listen first is what turned that around. That instinct matters for a role where enterprise customers are evaluating a self-hosted, open-source backend during their own security reviews, credibility gets built the same slow way.

## Leading a change nobody asked me to lead

At Benevity I was a Senior Staff IC with no direct reports, but I wanted two teams that didn't report to me to actually adopt AI-assisted development instead of treating it as a novelty. There was no mandate behind this, so I had to make people want it. I started with three curious engineers, built small internal demos that showed real time saved rather than talking about the idea abstractly, and ran informal lunch sessions with no obligation to attend. I put together reusable prompt templates in a shared repo and wrote up concrete results, one story was saving about two hours on a single PR review cycle. Within three months roughly 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted review or generation regularly, and I'd become the informal lead for it across the org without ever having the title. What stuck with me is that the slower, no-mandate approach produced advocates who pushed the practice on their own terms, something a top-down rollout would never have gotten me. A fourteen-person team doesn't have room for top-down mandates anyway, so this is closer to how I'd expect to operate day to day at Nango.
