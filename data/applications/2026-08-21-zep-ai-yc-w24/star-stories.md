# STAR Stories: Zep AI, Staff Engineer

A note before these: my direct experience is platform engineering, distributed systems, and AI-augmented workflows rather than agent memory or knowledge graphs specifically. These stories lean on the parts that transfer, architecture decisions under pressure, owning a system end to end, and driving adoption of new tooling without a mandate, since that's the closest match to what a Staff Engineer here would actually be asked to do.

## Story 1: The monolith vs. rewrite call at Alaya

Our monolith at Alaya was starting to buckle as traffic grew, and the team had split into two camps, rip it out for microservices or keep patching what we had. Investors wanted new features, not a quarter spent on infrastructure, so I couldn't just pick a side on instinct. I ran a two-week spike building small prototypes of both approaches and load-testing them against real traffic instead of arguing from opinion, then presented the results to the CTO and investors with numbers instead of a recommendation dressed up as one. What came out of it was a strangler fig migration, extracting only the two highest-traffic modules and leaving the rest alone until it actually needed attention. We shipped the first extraction in six weeks with about a 40% latency improvement on the busiest endpoints, and the system held stable through the acquisition. If I ran it again I'd start the benchmarking even earlier, since the data ended the debate faster than any meeting did.

## Story 2: Building Alaya from nothing

In 2016 I co-founded Alaya with a blank page, no customers, no product, no infrastructure. I had to architect and build the whole platform, alone at first and then with a small team, while the business side was already signing enterprise clients who expected something to exist. I picked a deliberately boring stack, Node.js, React, PostgreSQL, because getting to a first demo in three months mattered more than being clever about it. From there we layered in multi-tenancy, an API layer, and reporting as usage actually demanded them, and I hired the first three engineers along with the CI/CD and review practices we'd need once it wasn't just me anymore. Five years later the platform had over 50 enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity acquired the company. I underestimated how expensive retrofitting multi-tenancy would be after the fact; starting from zero again, I'd design for it on day one instead of bolting it on later.

## Story 3: Earning adoption without a mandate at Benevity

After the acquisition I joined Benevity as a senior staff IC with no direct reports, on a team roughly ten times the size of Alaya, and I wanted engineers across two teams to adopt AI-assisted development practices that nobody had put me in charge of rolling out. A top-down announcement wasn't on the table, so I started with three curious engineers, a handful of internal demos showing concrete time saved, and informal lunch sessions with no attendance requirement. I documented the wins as they happened, things like a two-hour reduction on a specific PR review cycle, and put reusable prompt templates into a shared repo anyone could pull from. Within three months about 70% of engineers on both teams were using the workflow, and people started coming to me instead of the other way around. It was slower than a mandate would have been, but it stuck because people chose it, which is the part I'd try to repeat with any new tooling I introduced at a company like Zep.

## Story 4: Slowing down after the acquisition

My first instinct at Benevity was to start fixing things that looked wrong from the outside, and that instinct was mostly wrong. The team was ten times bigger than what I'd worked in, the codebase was Java microservices instead of the Node stack I knew, and the processes had reasons behind them I hadn't learned yet. I spent the first month mapping the existing architecture and talking to the platform and infra teams before proposing anything, which felt slow at the time but wasn't. I ended up contributing to three major cross-team architecture decisions and became a liaison for the Europe-facing product surface, none of which would have happened if I'd shown up in week one with opinions. It's the opposite of my default mode, which is exactly why the lesson stuck, and it's how I'd approach ramping into a new knowledge graph system too, by understanding how it behaves under real load before touching it.

## Story 5: Choosing a pragmatic stack under delivery pressure

Early at Alaya I had to decide on a technology stack with almost no room to get it wrong twice, since a bad early choice would have meant a costly rewrite mid-fundraise. I was tempted by newer frameworks that looked more impressive on paper, but I picked Node.js, React, and PostgreSQL specifically because the team could hire for them easily and the community support meant fewer unknowns when something broke at 11pm. That decision held up for five years and through an acquisition, which isn't something I take credit for alone, but it did teach me that boring and well-understood usually beats interesting when the goal is shipping something real clients can depend on. The one thing I'd reconsider is how long we waited to introduce proper observability tooling; we treated it as a nice-to-have until an outage made it obviously not one.
