# STAR+R Stories: Senior/Staff Engineer, n8n Labs

Picked for this role because n8n Labs is a small skunkworks team building AI-native products from scratch under a founder, not a core-engine team shipping against a roadmap. These four cover zero-to-one building, moving fast on thin evidence, driving adoption without formal authority, and working closely with leadership before pushing an opinion, which maps fairly directly onto what the posting is asking for.

## 1. Building Alaya from a blank page to acquisition

When I co-founded Alaya in 2016, there was no product, no customers, and no team yet, just an idea about employee engagement software. I had to architect and build the whole platform myself at first, then bring people in, while the business was already out signing its first enterprise clients, so the ground kept shifting under the design. I picked Node.js, React, and PostgreSQL specifically because they would let me move fast without locking in a rigid architecture too early, and I got the core features working in about three months so we would have something real to demo. From there I kept expanding it: multi-tenancy, an API layer, impact reporting, and eventually postal campaign tooling, while hiring and onboarding the first three engineers and setting up CI/CD and a code review culture along the way. The platform ended up running for 50-plus enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity, a Canadian company valued north of a billion dollars, acquired us in 2021, which told me the early technical bets had actually held up. If I did it again I would invest in observability much sooner, we bolted on proper APM late, and I would design for multi-tenancy from day zero instead of retrofitting it once it started to hurt.

## 2. A two-week spike instead of a six-month rewrite

Alaya's monolith started showing real scaling cracks just as investors were pushing hard for new features rather than a pause to fix infrastructure. The team was split between a full microservices rewrite and something more conservative, and I needed to land on a decision I could defend fast, not one that just felt safer. So I spent two weeks running a technical spike with load tests on both approaches, then brought the CTO and investors concrete numbers instead of opinions about which way to go. What I proposed was a strangler-fig migration: pull out only the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest of the monolith alone, and set clear criteria for when we would extract the next piece. The first extracted module shipped in six weeks, the busiest endpoints got about 40% faster, and the remaining monolith stayed stable straight through the acquisition, so we never needed the big rewrite people were afraid of. The hard part was not the technical work, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the disciplined choice; with more benchmarking up front I think I could have shortened that debate by a couple of weeks.

## 3. Getting AI-assisted engineering adopted with no mandate

After Benevity acquired Alaya, I joined as a Senior Staff IC on a team about ten times the size of what I was used to, with no direct reports and no formal authority over anyone. I wanted engineers across two teams to actually change how they worked and bring AI tooling into their daily flow, but ordering that from the outside was not an option and probably would not have stuck anyway. I started small, with three engineers who were already curious, built internal demos that showed real time saved rather than talking in the abstract, and ran informal lunch-and-learn sessions with no mandate attached. I also put together reusable prompt templates and committed them to a shared repo, and wrote up concrete results, like a PR review cycle that dropped by two hours, so people could see the payoff themselves. Within three months, around 70% of engineers on both teams were using AI-assisted review and generation regularly, and I ended up the informal go-to person for AI tooling across the org. It was slower than a top-down rollout would have been, but the adoption held because people chose it, and they were the ones telling their peers to try it, which I could not have manufactured with a directive.

## 4. Listening before pushing an opinion on someone else's turf

Joining Benevity's platform team, I was dropped into a much larger codebase built in Java with a microservices setup very different from anything at Alaya, and a different culture around decision-making entirely. My instinct early on was to start proposing fixes for things that looked wrong to me, but that would have burned trust fast on a team that did not know me yet. Instead I spent the first month mapping the existing architecture and understanding why things were built the way they were before I said much of anything, and I built relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing any technical opinions their way. That patience paid off: I contributed to three major cross-team architecture decisions afterward and became the architecture liaison for the Europe-facing product surface. Looking back, I moved too fast in the first couple of weeks trying to fix things that were not actually broken, just unfamiliar to me, and slowing down to listen first is what made the rest of it work. That is roughly the posture I would bring into a small team sitting close to a founder, earn the read on the problem before proposing what to build.
