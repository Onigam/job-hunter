# STAR Stories: ML Platform Engineer, Synthesia

Picked for a systems-generalist platform role: architecture trade-offs under pressure, operating distributed infra post-acquisition, building tooling adoption without authority, and founding-engineer ownership. Framed toward platform reliability and internal tooling rather than pure ML depth, since the posting is explicit that this isn't a pure ML engineer seat.

## 1. The strangler fig migration instead of the rewrite

Someone will probably ask about a hard infrastructure trade-off, and this is the one I go to. At Alaya our monolith was starting to crack under load right as we were signing bigger enterprise clients, and half the team wanted a full microservices rewrite while investors wanted new features, not months of plumbing work. I ran a two-week spike comparing both paths with actual load tests instead of opinions, then took the numbers to the CTO and investors. What we agreed on was a strangler fig approach: pull out the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest of the monolith alone, and define clear criteria for when to extract the next piece. The first module shipped in six weeks and cut latency about 40% on our busiest endpoints, and the remaining monolith stayed stable all the way through the acquisition, so we never needed the big-bang rewrite. Looking back, the slow part wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the disciplined choice. If I ran this again I'd push for the benchmarking earlier, since the data ended the debate faster than any argument did.

## 2. Learning someone else's platform before touching it

When Benevity acquired Alaya in 2021 I went from a team of a handful to an engineering org ten times the size, running a Java microservices stack instead of the Node stack I'd built. My instinct was to start fixing things that looked wrong to me, and I caught myself doing exactly that in the first couple of weeks. I stopped, spent the rest of the first month just mapping how their architecture actually worked and why, and built relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing any technical opinion. That groundwork is what let me become a cross-team architecture liaison for the Europe-facing product surface afterward, and I ended up contributing to three major architecture decisions once people trusted that I understood the system before I tried to change it. The lesson stuck with me: moving fast to "fix" an unfamiliar system usually just breaks trust, and trust is the thing you actually need to get anything done at that scale.

## 3. Getting AI tooling adopted with no authority to mandate it

At Benevity I wanted two teams that didn't report to me to actually change how they worked, specifically to bring AI-assisted development into their daily workflow, and I had zero formal authority to make that happen. Instead of pushing a top-down rollout, I started with three engineers who were already curious, built small internal demos that showed concrete time saved rather than talking about the idea abstractly, and ran informal lunch-and-learn sessions with no mandate attached. I also committed reusable prompt templates to a shared repo so the tooling itself did some of the persuading. Within about three months, something like 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted review and generation day to day, and I'd become the informal lead for AI tooling adoption across the org. It was slower than an announcement from a director would have been, but the adoption stuck because people chose it, and they ended up advocating for it internally in a way a mandate never would have produced. That experience is a lot of what makes building internal tools that people actually want to operate feel like familiar ground.

## 4. Building the whole platform from a blank page

This is the founding story, useful for anything about ownership or ambiguity. In 2016 I cofounded Alaya with nothing built yet and the business already signing its first enterprise clients, so the platform had to exist fast without boxing us in later. I picked a pragmatic stack, Node.js, React, Postgres, that could scale without over-engineering for problems we didn't have yet, got core features to a first demo inside three months, then layered in multi-tenancy, an API layer, and impact reporting as the business grew. I also hired and onboarded our first three engineers and set up CI/CD and a code review culture from close to zero. The platform reached production with more than 50 enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity's acquisition of us in 2021 was the clearest validation that the early technical calls held up. If I did it again I'd invest in proper observability much earlier, since we didn't add real APM until problems were already hard to diagnose, and I'd design for multi-tenancy from day one instead of retrofitting it under pressure.
