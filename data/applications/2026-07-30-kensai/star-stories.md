# STAR Stories: Kensai (Product/Backend Founding Engineer)

Selected for a third-technical-hire, 0-to-1 backend role: building data infrastructure from scratch, hacker-DNA problem solving, working alongside a small founding team without much process to lean on.

## 1. Building the platform from a blank page (Alaya)

In 2016 I co-founded Alaya, a purpose and impact platform for employee engagement, and there was nothing to start from: no product, no customers, not even a settled idea of what the MVP looked like. My job was to architect and build the whole thing, alone at first, while the business side was already out there signing enterprise clients on a promise. I picked a stack I could move fast in without painting myself into a corner, Node.js, React, PostgreSQL, and spent the first three months getting to a working demo rather than a polished one. Once we had real customers I kept expanding the architecture in slices, multi-tenancy, an API layer, impact reporting, hiring the first three engineers and setting up the basics of CI/CD and code review along the way. The platform ended up with more than fifty enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity, a Canadian unicorn, acquired us in 2021, which felt like the clearest possible validation of the early calls. If I'm honest, I'd have put real observability in much earlier, we bolted on proper APM late and paid for it in debugging time, and I underestimated how expensive retrofitting multi-tenancy would be. That second one especially shapes how I think about data infrastructure now: design for the awkward scaling case on day one, not after it hurts.

## 2. Choosing not to rewrite under pressure

At Alaya our monolith started showing real scaling cracks, and the team split into two camps, rewrite everything as microservices, or keep it a modular monolith. Investors wanted new features shipped, not months spent on a rewrite, so the actual task was finding a technical answer that held up under that pressure rather than just picking a side. I ran a two-week spike prototyping both approaches with load tests, then took the results, not opinions, to the CTO and investors. What we landed on was a strangler-fig migration: pull out the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest alone, and set clear criteria for when to extract the next piece. The first extracted module shipped in six weeks and cut latency by roughly 40% on our busiest endpoints, and the rest of the monolith stayed stable straight through the acquisition, no big-bang rewrite required. The hard part wasn't the engineering, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the disciplined choice. Data made that argument for me, and next time I'd push for the benchmarking earlier so the debate doesn't eat two extra weeks before it starts.

## 3. Landing in unfamiliar territory without breaking things

After Benevity acquired Alaya in 2021, I joined a team roughly ten times the size of what I'd worked in before, a different codebase in Java and microservices, different culture, different everything. My task was to fold Alaya's capabilities into Benevity's platform without losing the product velocity we'd built our reputation on. I spent the first month just mapping how the existing architecture actually worked before I proposed anything, and built relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing any technical opinions on them. From there I ended up driving adoption of AI-assisted engineering practices, prompt patterns, tooling, across the org, and became the informal architecture liaison for the Europe-facing product surface. That work fed into three major cross-team architecture decisions and made me the go-to person for AI tooling across two teams. Early on I moved too fast trying to fix things that weren't actually broken in the new org, just unfamiliar, and slowing down to listen first is what turned that around. It's the same instinct I'd bring to joining a founding team already three people deep: understand what's there before deciding what to change.

## 4. Driving adoption with no authority to mandate it

At Benevity I was a Senior Staff IC with no direct reports, but I wanted two teams that didn't answer to me to actually change how they worked and bring AI tooling into their daily development. Since I had no mandate to hand down, the task was to make the case entirely on results. I started with three curious engineers, built small internal demos that showed concrete time saved rather than talking about potential, and ran informal lunch-and-learn sessions with no pressure attached. I wrote up reusable prompt templates and committed them to a shared repo, and documented specific wins, like the time we shaved two hours off a PR review cycle, so people could see the argument rather than take my word for it. Within three months about 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted review and generation regularly, and I became the informal lead on AI tooling for the org. It was slower than a top-down rollout would have been, but it stuck, because people adopted it on their own terms and then went and told their peers why. That's the version of leadership I'd bring to a founding engineering team: earn the change, don't decree it.

## 5. Turning messy, unstructured problems into working systems

A recurring thread across both companies is comfort with ambiguity where the data itself is the hard part, not just the code around it. At Alaya, impact reporting meant pulling inconsistent data out of dozens of different client systems and turning it into something a nonprofit or an HR team could actually trust, long before "data pipeline" was a term anyone on the team used casually. The task was less about writing clean code and more about deciding what "correct" even meant when every input source disagreed with the others slightly. I built validation and normalization layers incrementally, treating each new client integration as a chance to tighten the rules rather than special-case around them, and kept the team looking at edge cases in the actual data instead of trusting a schema on paper. The reporting layer became one of the more trusted parts of the platform, cited directly in the acquisition due diligence as evidence the data model held up under real client volume. What I'd change is starting with stricter input contracts sooner, we spent too long being permissive with bad data before tightening it up. That's exactly the muscle I'd bring to turning fragmented global regulatory text into structured ground truth: assume the sources disagree, build for that from the start, and tighten as you learn where they actually diverge.
