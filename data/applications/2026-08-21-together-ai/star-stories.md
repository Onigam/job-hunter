# STAR Stories: Together AI, Staff Engineer (Distributed Storage & HPC/AI Infra)

A note before these: my hands-on depth is in platform engineering, distributed systems, and Kubernetes/AWS operations, not in Ceph, Weka, Lustre, or GPU storage networking specifically. These stories are picked to speak to how I make architecture calls under pressure, ramp into unfamiliar systems, and drive change without formal authority, since that's the transferable ground for this role. I'd expect early conversations to probe honestly on the storage-specific gap.

## Story 1: The monolith vs. rewrite call at Alaya

At Alaya our monolith was starting to buckle under load, and the team had split into two camps: rip it out for microservices, or keep patching the thing we had. Investors wanted new features, not a quarter spent on plumbing, so I couldn't just pick a side and hope. I ran a two-week spike where we built small prototypes of both approaches and load-tested them against real traffic patterns instead of arguing from opinion. What came out of that was a middle path, a strangler fig migration where we'd extract only the two highest-traffic modules and leave the rest of the monolith alone until it actually needed attention. We shipped the first extraction in six weeks and saw about a 40% latency improvement on the busiest endpoints, and the rest of the system stayed stable all the way through the acquisition. If I did it again I'd push for the benchmarking phase to start even earlier, because the data is what actually ended the debate, not the meetings before it.

## Story 2: Building Alaya from nothing

In 2016 I co-founded Alaya with literally a blank page: no customers, no product, no infrastructure. My job was to architect and build the whole platform, alone at first and then with a small team, while the business side was already out signing enterprise clients who expected something to exist. I picked a deliberately boring stack, Node.js, React, PostgreSQL, because the goal was to get to a first demo in three months, not to build something clever. From there we layered in multi-tenancy, an API layer, and reporting as real usage demanded it, and I hired and onboarded the first three engineers along with the CI/CD and review practices we'd need once it wasn't just me. Five years later the platform had over 50 enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity acquired the company in a deal that valued it in the tens of millions. Looking back, I underestimated how expensive it would be to retrofit multi-tenancy after the fact; if I were starting from zero again I'd design for it on day one instead of bolting it on later.

## Story 3: Earning influence without a mandate at Benevity

After the acquisition I landed at Benevity as a senior staff IC with no direct reports, on a team roughly ten times the size of Alaya and running a completely different stack. I wanted engineers across two teams to actually adopt AI-assisted development practices, but nobody had put me in charge of that and a top-down mandate wasn't on the table. So I started small: three curious engineers, a few internal demos that showed real time saved on things like PR review, and informal lunch sessions with no attendance requirement. I wrote up the wins as they happened, things like a two-hour reduction on a specific review cycle, and put reusable prompt templates into a shared repo anyone could pull from. Within three months about 70% of engineers on both teams were using the workflow, and I'd become the person people asked when they wanted to get started. It was slower than an announcement from management would have been, but it stuck because people chose it themselves, and I don't think a decree would have gotten the same result.

## Story 4: Slowing down after the acquisition

My first instinct at Benevity was to start fixing things that looked wrong to me from the outside, and that instinct was mostly wrong. The team was ten times the size of what I'd worked in, the codebase was Java-based microservices instead of the Node stack I knew, and the processes had reasons behind them I didn't understand yet. I spent the first month just mapping the existing architecture and talking to the platform and infra teams before I proposed anything, which felt slow at the time. That patience paid off: I ended up contributing to three major cross-team architecture decisions and became a liaison for the Europe-facing product surface, which wouldn't have happened if I'd shown up with opinions in week one. The lesson stuck with me because it's the opposite of my default mode, and it's part of why I'd approach a new storage stack the same way, by mapping how the current system actually behaves under real GPU workloads before proposing changes to it.
