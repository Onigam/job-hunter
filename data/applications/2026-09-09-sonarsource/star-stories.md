# Behavioral Interview Prep, SonarSource, Staff Software Engineer (Java), Identity Squad

Five stories picked for this role: architecture trade-offs under pressure, cross-team influence without formal authority, mentoring, hypergrowth scaling, and navigating a very different codebase after the Benevity acquisition. All in STAR+R, written the way I'd actually say them out loud.

## 1. The monolith vs. microservices call at Alaya

We hit a point at Alaya where the monolith was starting to show real scaling cracks, and the team split into two camps: rewrite everything as microservices, or keep pushing the monolith further. Investors wanted new features, not a quarter of internal plumbing work, so I couldn't just pick the technically purest answer and expect buy-in. I ran a two-week spike comparing both approaches with actual load tests instead of opinions, then walked the CTO and investors through the numbers. What I proposed was a strangler fig migration: pull out the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest alone, and set clear criteria for when we'd extract more. We shipped the first extracted module in six weeks, and the busiest endpoints got about 40% faster. The rest of the monolith stayed stable all the way through the acquisition, no big rewrite required. Looking back, the hardest part wasn't the engineering, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the disciplined choice. I'd push for the load-test data even earlier next time, since that's what actually ended the debate.

## 2. Landing in a much bigger, unfamiliar codebase after the acquisition

When Benevity acquired Alaya in 2021, I went from a five-person team where I knew every line of code to a Java and microservices org roughly ten times the size, with its own culture and its own way of deciding things. My instinct early on was to start pointing out things that looked wrong to me, which is exactly the wrong move in someone else's house. I spent the first month just mapping the existing architecture and asking questions before I proposed anything, and I made a point of building relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing any technical opinion. That patience is what let me become the architecture liaison for our Europe-facing product surface and contribute to three cross-team architecture decisions that actually stuck. If I'd led with opinions in week one the way I nearly did, I don't think any of those decisions would have landed the same way. Slowing down first turned out to be the fast path.

## 3. Getting AI tooling adopted across teams that didn't report to me

I wanted engineers across two teams to start using AI-assisted development, but I had no management authority over either team and no mandate to make anyone change how they worked. Instead of pushing a top-down rollout, I found three curious engineers, built small internal demos that showed concrete time saved on real tasks, and ran informal lunch sessions with no agenda beyond "here's what this looks like." I wrote up prompt templates and committed them to a shared repo so people could take what was useful and ignore the rest. Within about three months, roughly 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted code review or generation regularly, and I'd become the person people asked when they got stuck with it. It was slower than a mandate would have been, but it stuck because people adopted it on their own terms and then told their teammates why. I'd probably still choose the slow version even knowing the timeline going in.

## 4. Building Alaya's platform from a blank page

In 2016 I cofounded Alaya with nothing built yet and the business already signing its first enterprise clients, so the pressure to ship was immediate. I picked a pragmatic stack, Node.js, React, PostgreSQL, that wouldn't box us in but also wouldn't force premature complexity, and got a working demo out in the first three months. From there it was multi-tenant architecture, an API layer, impact reporting, all layered in as the client base grew, alongside hiring and onboarding the first three engineers and setting up code review and CI/CD from scratch. We got to over 50 enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity's acquisition in 2021 was, in a real sense, a vote of confidence in those early technical calls. The thing I'd do differently is invest in observability sooner. We didn't have proper APM until fairly late, and retrofitting multi-tenancy cost more than designing for it from day one would have.

## 5. Making a hard architecture decision stick without being anyone's boss

At Benevity, I was a Senior Staff IC with no direct reports, but I needed a handful of engineers on two teams to change how they built things, which meant influencing without any of the usual pull a manager has. I leaned on small early wins instead of a big announcement, showed people the actual time saved rather than arguing for it abstractly, and made the resources easy to find and reuse. What made it durable wasn't the initial adoption, it was that the engineers who picked it up started explaining it to their own teammates without me in the room. That's the part a top-down decree can't buy. I'd still bet on the mandate-free approach for anything that needs to survive after I stop pushing it.
