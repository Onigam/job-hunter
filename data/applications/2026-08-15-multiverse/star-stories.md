# STAR Stories - Multiverse, Staff AI Engineer (AI Transformation)

Picked for a role about rebuilding an organization's internals with AI: driving adoption without a mandate, building integration layers and API contracts from scratch, making cost and architecture tradeoffs with real data, and setting standards that survive contact with a much bigger org.

## 1. Getting two teams to adopt AI-assisted workflows with no mandate

At Benevity I was a Senior Staff engineer with no direct reports, and I wanted two teams that didn't report to me to actually use AI-assisted development instead of treating it as a novelty. I didn't have the authority to decree it, so I didn't try. I started with three curious engineers, built a few internal demos that showed real time saved, and ran informal lunch sessions with no agenda beyond "here's what this looks like." From there I wrote reusable prompt templates, put them in a shared repo, and documented concrete wins, like a two-hour saving on a single PR review cycle. Within three months about 70% of engineers across both teams were using it regularly, and I'd become the person people came to for AI tooling questions across the org. It was slower than a top-down rollout, but it stuck, because people adopted it on their own terms and told their peers it worked rather than being told to use it. That's the same shape of problem as getting a whole company to treat agent tooling as normal: the standards that survive are the ones people choose, not the ones handed down.

## 2. Building the platform and its integration layer from zero

I co-founded Alaya in 2016 with a blank page, no customers, and a business team already out signing enterprise clients before we had much of a product. My job was to architect and build the whole thing, alone at first. I picked a pragmatic stack, Node.js, React, Postgres, that could scale without turning into a science project, had a working demo inside three months, and kept expanding: multi-tenant architecture, an API layer other systems could actually plug into, impact reporting, campaign tooling. Once we had traction I hired and onboarded the first three engineers and put in CI/CD and a real code review culture before either felt urgent. The platform ended up running for fifty-plus enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity, a Canadian unicorn, acquired the company in 2021. What I'd change: I'd design the API and data contracts for multi-tenancy from day one instead of retrofitting them later, which is exactly the kind of shared-contract thinking a fresh integration layer needs to get right upfront.

## 3. A cost and architecture tradeoff decided with data, not opinions

At Alaya our monolith started showing scaling cracks right when investors wanted new features, not a rewrite. The team was split between a full microservices rewrite and something more modest, and it landed on me to make a call that wouldn't blow up either the system or the roadmap. I ran a two-week spike comparing both approaches with actual load tests, brought the numbers to the CTO and the board instead of opinions, and proposed a strangler-fig migration: extract the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest alone, and define clear criteria for when to extract more. We shipped the first extracted module in six weeks and saw close to a 40% performance gain on the busiest endpoints, and the rest of the monolith stayed stable through the acquisition. The hardest part wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the disciplined choice. I'd want to run that same kind of spike on any cost tradeoff involving model routing or caching before committing to an architecture, since the debate is always shorter once there's a number attached to it.

## 4. Setting standards inside a much bigger, already-structured org

When Benevity acquired Alaya in 2021, I went from owning a small stack solo to sitting on a team ten times the size, with a different language, Java instead of Node, and processes I hadn't written. I spent the first month mostly listening, mapping how the existing system actually worked before proposing to touch anything, and built relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing technical opinions. From there I helped drive the migration of Alaya's core capabilities into Benevity's platform while still shipping new features, and became a cross-team architecture liaison for the Europe-facing product surface, contributing to three major architecture decisions along the way. My first instinct had actually been to move fast and start fixing things that weren't broken in the new org, and dialing that back mattered more than any specific technical contribution I made that quarter. Setting a standard that a bigger org will actually keep means understanding what's already working before you propose to replace it.
