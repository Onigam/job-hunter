# STAR Stories: Plutus, Founding Engineer

Prepared for behavioral rounds. Picked for founding-engineer signal at a sub-ten-person, SEC-registered fintech: building the whole stack alone, owning production incidents, making calls under real financial and regulatory stakes, and driving change without formal authority, since Plutus is asking one or two engineers to take a platform from tens of millions to a billion in AUM.

## Story 1: Building Alaya from zero to acquisition

This is the one I'd lead with. In 2016 I co-founded Alaya, an employee purpose and impact platform, and there was nothing yet, no customers, no product, not even a settled stack. My job was to architect and build the whole thing myself first, then grow a team around it while the business was closing its first enterprise deals. I picked Node.js, React, and PostgreSQL because they let me move fast without boxing myself into something I'd regret in year two, and I got the core product to a first demo inside three months. From there it was a steady expansion: multi-tenant architecture, an API layer, reporting, and eventually hiring the first three engineers and setting up CI/CD and a code review culture that didn't exist before I wrote it down. We ended up with 50+ enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity, a Canadian unicorn valued over a billion dollars, acquired us in 2021. Looking back, I'd have added real observability much earlier; we bolted it on late and paid for it in debugging time. For a company handling other people's money from day one, that's exactly the kind of lesson I'd rather apply upfront than relearn.

## Story 2: A hard architecture call under investor pressure

At Alaya, our monolith started showing real scaling cracks right as investors were pushing hard for new features, not a rewrite. The team was split between a full microservices rewrite and just living with what we had, and I needed to make a call that wouldn't blow up either the roadmap or the system. I ran a two-week spike comparing both approaches with actual load tests instead of opinions, then brought the CTO and investors concrete numbers. What we landed on was a strangler fig migration: pull out the two highest-traffic modules first and leave the rest of the monolith alone, with clear criteria for when to extract the next piece. The first module shipped in six weeks and cut latency on our busiest endpoints by roughly 40%. The rest of the monolith held up fine all the way through the acquisition, no big-bang rewrite required. The hard part wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the disciplined choice. A team racing toward a billion in AUM by year end is going to face this same fork more than once, and I'd rather bring data to that conversation than instinct.

## Story 3: Owning production and on-call at Benevity's scale

After the acquisition, I joined Benevity as Senior Staff on a team roughly ten times the size of Alaya, running services I hadn't built, with an on-call rotation that actually paged me at 3am more than once. Early on I inherited an incident where a batch job silently corrupted a subset of reporting data overnight, the kind of thing that's invisible until a customer notices numbers don't add up. I traced it back to a race condition in a retry path nobody had load tested past normal volume, wrote a fix, and then spent the following week building alerting that would have caught it before a customer did. That habit, treating a near miss as a gap in the safety net rather than a one-off, is what I'd bring to a platform moving real client money, where a quiet data bug is a much bigger problem than a loud one.

## Story 4: Driving AI tooling adoption with no authority to mandate it

Still at Benevity, no direct reports, I wanted two teams that didn't answer to me to actually change how they worked day to day. Rather than push a mandate nobody asked for, I started with three engineers who were already curious, built small internal demos that showed real time saved on their own tickets, and ran informal lunch sessions with no agenda beyond "here's what this looks like." I wrote up reusable prompt templates and committed them to a shared repo so people could just take what worked. Within about three months, something like 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted review and generation regularly, and I became the person people pinged when they wanted to get better at it. It was slower than a top-down rollout would have been, but it stuck, because people adopted it because it helped them. At a two or three person engineering team, that same instinct, build the thing that proves itself rather than argue for it, is most of what "founding engineer" means in practice.

## Story 5: Retrofitting multi-tenancy under a live enterprise contract

One of Alaya's early clients wanted to onboard three subsidiaries under one contract, and our data model at the time assumed one organization per account, baked into most of the schema. I had maybe six weeks before the client's internal launch date, and no room to freeze the product for a clean rewrite. I mapped every place tenancy was implicit rather than explicit, built a migration path that let existing single-tenant accounts keep working unchanged, and shipped the multi-tenant version behind a flag so I could roll it out account by account and roll back the moment something looked wrong. It went out two days before the deadline, with zero downtime for existing customers. I'd underestimated how much of the codebase quietly assumed single-tenancy, and if I were starting Plutus's data model today, knowing multiple account types and custody relationships are coming, I'd design the tenancy boundary in from the first migration instead of retrofitting it under a deadline again.
