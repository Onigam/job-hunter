# STAR Stories: Manager, ML Solutions Architecture (Token Factory), Nebius

Prepared for a role that mixes people management, delivery accountability, and technical review of ML/LLM work. Expect questions on managing without a long track record as a formal manager, calling things infeasible with evidence, and building operational systems a team actually uses.

---

## 1. Hiring and onboarding a founding engineering team ("Tell me about your management experience")

When Alaya was still just me and a blank page in 2016, I knew fairly quickly that the thing that would break first wasn't the product, it was the fact that nobody else knew how any of it worked. I hired our first three engineers over the following year, and I was deliberate about not just handing them a codebase and a Slack invite. I set up a review culture from day one, wrote the first architecture docs so decisions weren't just in my head, and built CI/CD before we had enough traffic to strictly need it, because I didn't want quality to depend on me personally reviewing everything forever. What made this stick wasn't the tooling, it was that I treated onboarding as a real design problem rather than a formality: pairing on the first few tickets, being explicit about what "done" meant, and correcting fast when someone was stuck instead of waiting for a retro. The platform they helped build reached 50-plus enterprise clients and held up well enough that Benevity didn't need to gut it during acquisition due diligence. I don't have years of formal performance-review cycles behind me, and I'd say that plainly in an interview, but I do know what it takes to get a small team producing work you can stand behind without you in the room.

## 2. Calling a technical approach infeasible with evidence, not opinion ("Tell me about pushing back on scope or a deadline")

Our monolith at Alaya was starting to crack under enterprise load right as investors wanted new features, not an infrastructure quarter. The easy thing to do would have been to just say yes to both, or to unilaterally announce a rewrite because I thought it was right. Instead I ran a two-week spike, built actual load-test comparisons between a full microservices rewrite and a narrower alternative, and took real numbers into the room with the CTO and investors rather than a strong opinion. The data supported a strangler-fig migration: extract only the two highest-traffic modules, leave the rest alone, and set explicit criteria for when we'd touch more. We shipped the first extraction in six weeks and saw roughly a 40% latency improvement on the busiest endpoints, and the untouched part of the monolith stayed boring and stable straight through acquisition. The harder part honestly wasn't the technical call, it was convincing a room that not rewriting everything was the disciplined choice rather than the timid one. If I did it again, I'd build that load-test comparison before the debate even started, so there was less room for it to become opinion against opinion.

## 3. Getting a rollout adopted without any authority to mandate it ("How do you drive change across teams you don't own?")

At Benevity I was a Senior Staff IC with no direct reports, and I wanted two teams that didn't answer to me to start using AI-assisted development day to day. Announcing a policy wasn't an option I had, so I didn't try to force it. I found three engineers who were genuinely curious, built small demos that showed concrete time saved on real pull requests, and ran informal sessions people could skip without consequence. I wrote up specific wins, like a case that cut two hours off a review cycle, and put reusable prompt templates in a shared repo rather than a mandate document. It was slower than a top-down rollout would have been. About 70% of engineers across both teams were using the workflow within three months anyway, and it stuck because nobody had to be told twice to keep doing it. Looking back, the slowness was the point. A rollout people choose survives the first six months; one they're told to adopt usually doesn't survive the first reorg.

## 4. Owning documentation that a team actually uses, not just files that exist ("Tell me about building operational systems")

At Alaya I wrote the first architecture docs mostly for myself, so I wouldn't have to hold every decision in my head once we had more than one engineer. What turned them from personal notes into something the team relied on was tying them to onboarding directly: a new hire's first week meant working through those docs to make a small, real change, not reading them passively. I kept them updated as part of the definition of done for anything that changed a core decision, not as a cleanup task scheduled for later. That habit carried into how I approached the cross-team architecture work at Benevity too, where documentation that nobody opened after week one was worse than no documentation, since it created false confidence. The lesson I'd bring into a Solutions Architecture team is that a runbook only counts if the next engineer reaches for it under pressure instead of pinging someone directly, and that's a habit you build by making the doc genuinely faster than the alternative, not by asking nicely.

## 5. A production trade-off I'd handle differently now ("Tell me about a mistake or a gap in your experience")

Observability at Alaya is the honest answer here. We didn't add proper monitoring until year two, treating it as a nice-to-have while we were racing toward the next enterprise client, and a couple of the harder production issues that year took roughly twice as long to diagnose as they should have because we were reading logs by hand instead of looking at a dashboard. Nobody forced that trade-off on me, I made it, prioritizing visible feature work over infrastructure that wouldn't show up in a demo. For a role like this one, where reviewing benchmarking and configuration work before it reaches a customer is part of the job, that's exactly the kind of gap I'd want to close early rather than let it compound. I'd rather be upfront that formal team management with performance reviews is a newer muscle for me than the technical and founding work is, and that I'd lean on the same evidence-first habit to build it fast.
