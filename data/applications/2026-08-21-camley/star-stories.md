# STAR+R Stories: Camley, Founding Engineer (Full-Stack AI)

Picked for a founding-engineer, agent-infrastructure role: zero-to-one building, ownership under ambiguity, judgment calls on architecture, and driving adoption of AI-assisted workflows without formal authority.

---

## Building Alaya from a blank page to an acquisition

In 2016 I co-founded Alaya, a purpose and impact platform for employee engagement, and there was nothing to work from, no customers, no product, not even a settled idea of what the first version should do. My job was to architect and build the entire platform myself at first, then grow a small team, all while the business was out signing its first enterprise clients on the strength of a promise more than a product. I picked a pragmatic stack, Node, React, PostgreSQL, that could carry weight without forcing us into premature complexity, and pushed to get a working demo in front of clients within three months rather than polishing something nobody had validated yet. From there it was iteration: multi-tenant architecture, an API layer, impact reporting, campaign tooling, and eventually hiring the first three engineers and setting up the CI/CD and review culture that let the codebase survive more than one person touching it. We got to fifty-plus enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity, a Canadian unicorn, acquired the company in 2021, which was about as clear a signal as you get that the early technical bets held up. If I did it again I'd invest in observability much earlier; we bolted on proper monitoring later than we should have, and I'd design for multi-tenancy from day zero instead of retrofitting it once it started to hurt.

## A technical trade-off under real pressure

At Alaya, the original monolith started showing scaling cracks right around the time investors wanted new features, not a rewrite, so a full microservices split was off the table even though a few engineers were pushing hard for it. My task was to find a path that respected both the scaling problem and the delivery pressure, and to make the case with something more convincing than opinion. I ran a two-week spike with prototype load tests comparing a modular-monolith approach against a partial extraction, then brought the numbers, not a slide deck of arguments, to the CTO and investors. What we landed on was a strangler-fig migration: pull out the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest alone, and define clear criteria for when the next module earned extraction. The first module shipped in six weeks and performance improved roughly 40% on the busiest endpoints, and the remaining monolith stayed stable straight through the acquisition, no big-bang rewrite required. The hard part honestly wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that restraint was the right call; the data did most of that convincing, and next time I'd run the early benchmarks sooner so the debate itself takes less time.

## Driving AI-tooling adoption with no mandate

After the Benevity acquisition I was a senior staff IC with no direct reports, but I wanted two teams that didn't report to me to actually change how they worked and bring AI tooling into their daily development loop. Ordering that from the top wasn't an option I had, so I started small: three engineers who were already curious, a handful of internal demos, and informal lunch-and-learn sessions with no mandate behind them, just working examples. I built reusable prompt templates and committed them to a shared repo, and I documented concrete wins as they happened, one story was a two-hour reduction on a single PR review cycle, because specific numbers travel further than a pitch. Within three months, about 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted review and generation on their own initiative, and I ended up the informal go-to person for AI tooling across the org. It was slower than a top-down rollout would have been, but it stuck, because people who adopt something on their own terms end up defending it to their peers in a way a mandate never produces. That's the same instinct I'd bring to getting a team comfortable trusting an agent's output on a live customer workflow: show, don't decree.

## Making the case for restraint after an acquisition

Joining Benevity meant landing on a team ten times the size of Alaya, a different codebase in Java and microservices, and a different set of norms I hadn't earned the standing to challenge yet. My task was to integrate what Alaya had built into Benevity's platform while keeping the product moving, without coming across as the acquired guy who thinks he knows better. I spent the first month mapping the existing architecture before proposing anything, and built relationships with the platform and infra teams before I pushed a single technical opinion in a design review. From there I became a cross-team architecture liaison for the Europe-facing product surface and contributed to three major architecture decisions that stuck. The features migrated cleanly and the relationships held, which mattered more long-term than any single technical win. My first instinct, in month one, was to move fast and fix things that weren't actually broken in their context, and slowing down to listen first is what made the rest of it work; it's a mistake I only had to make once.
