# STAR+R Stories, Senior AI Engineer, Jua.ai

Prepared for a startup building a physics foundation model from petabytes of Earth data. Stories chosen for ambiguity, scaling infrastructure under real load, and driving change without formal authority, all things that show up early at a company still figuring out how its platform holds together as the models and the data grow.

---

## Story 1: Building a platform from a blank page

In 2016 I co-founded Alaya, an employee purpose and impact platform, with no product and no customers yet. My job was to architect and build the whole thing from zero, alone at first, while the business was already signing its first enterprise clients, so there wasn't much room to get the foundations wrong. I picked a pragmatic stack, Node.js, React, PostgreSQL, that could scale without over-engineering something nobody had validated yet, and got the core features to a first demo inside three months. From there I expanded iteratively: multi-tenant architecture, an API layer, impact reporting, and eventually postal campaigns, while hiring and onboarding the first three engineers and setting up CI/CD and a real code review culture. The platform ended up running production for 50-plus enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity, a Canadian unicorn valued north of a billion dollars, acquired the company in 2021. If I did it again I'd invest in observability much earlier; we bolted on proper APM late, and I underestimated how expensive it would be to retrofit multi-tenancy instead of designing for it from day zero.

## Story 2: A hard architecture call under investor pressure

At Alaya, our monolith started showing scaling cracks right as investors were pushing hard for new features, not a rewrite. The team was split between a full microservices rewrite and a more modular monolith approach, and I needed a decision that balanced long-term scalability against short-term delivery without just picking a side on instinct. I ran a two-week technical spike comparing both approaches with prototype load tests, then presented the trade-offs to the CTO and investors using the numbers instead of opinions. What I proposed and led was a strangler-fig migration: extract the two highest-traffic modules first, leave everything else monolithic, and define clear criteria for when to pull out more. The first extracted module shipped in six weeks, performance on the busiest endpoints improved around 40%, and the rest of the platform stayed stable straight through the acquisition, no big-bang rewrite required. The hardest part honestly wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the disciplined choice; next time I'd front-load more benchmarking so that argument takes less time to win.

## Story 3: Driving adoption with no mandate

At Benevity I was a senior IC with no direct reports, but I wanted two teams that didn't report to me to actually change how they worked and bring AI tooling into their daily development. Mandating it wasn't an option, so I started with three engineers who were already curious, built small internal demos that showed concrete time savings, and ran informal lunch-and-learn sessions instead of top-down training. I put together reusable prompt templates, committed them to a shared repo, and wrote up results as they came in, things like a two-hour reduction on a single PR review cycle. Within three months, about 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted review and generation regularly, and I'd become the informal AI tooling lead for the org without ever having the title. It was slower than a mandate would have been, but it stuck, because people who adopt something on their own terms end up advocating for it themselves, which isn't something you can get from a policy memo.

## Story 4: Learning an unfamiliar system before touching it

When Benevity acquired Alaya in 2021, I landed on an engineering team roughly ten times the size of what I'd worked in, running a different language, a Java microservices codebase instead of the Node monolith I knew, with its own culture and its own processes. My task was to fold Alaya's capabilities into that platform while still shipping new features, without losing the product velocity we'd built as a five-person startup team. I spent the first month just mapping the existing architecture before I proposed a single change, and I made a point of building relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing any technical opinions their way. That patience paid off: the Alaya features migrated into Benevity's platform successfully, I contributed to three major cross-team architecture decisions, and I became the architecture liaison for the Europe-facing product surface. I did move too fast early on trying to "fix" things that weren't actually broken in the new org, and slowing down to listen first is what turned that around and made the rest of the integration land well.
