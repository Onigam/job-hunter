# STAR Stories: SoSafe, Staff Engineer (IT Automation & AI-native Transformation)

Picked for a role that's really two jobs at once: architecting an automation and agentic-workflow toolchain, and getting the rest of the org to actually use it. These lean on the parts of my background that involve driving adoption without formal authority, building infrastructure from a blank page, and making an infra decision hold up under real pressure.

## 1. Driving AI-native adoption with no formal authority

As a senior staff IC at Benevity, I had no direct reports and no mandate, but I wanted two teams that didn't report to me to change how they worked and fold AI-assisted development into their daily flow. Instead of pushing a rollout nobody asked for, I started with three curious engineers, built small internal demos that showed concrete time saved on real pull requests, and ran informal lunch sessions with no attendance requirement. I wrote up results as they came in, things like a two-hour PR review cycle cut down concretely, and committed reusable prompt templates to a shared repo anyone could pull from without asking me first. Within three months, about 70% of engineers across both teams were using the workflow on their own, and I became the informal lead on AI tooling for the org without ever holding the title. It was slower than a top-down decree would have been, but it stuck, because people who opted in on their own terms ended up advocating for it themselves. That's the same bet I'd make on the cultural half of this role: demos and repeatable wins beat a mandate almost every time.

## 2. Building infrastructure and CI/CD from zero

In 2016 I co-founded Alaya with a blank page, no customers, and no product. My job was to architect and build the entire platform alone at first, then grow a small engineering team, while the business closed its first enterprise clients in parallel. I picked a pragmatic stack that could scale without over-engineering for a scale we didn't have yet, and got the core features to a working demo inside three months. Once I had headcount, I set up CI/CD, a code review culture, and architecture documentation from scratch, so the practices were baked in before the team was large enough to argue about them. The platform ended up running 50-plus enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity acquired the company in 2021. If I did it again I'd invest in observability and infrastructure-as-code even earlier. We treated infra as something to formalize later, and formalizing it after the fact cost more than building it in from day one would have, a lesson that maps directly onto pushing an org toward an all-encompassing *-as-code model.

## 3. Making a hard architecture call under delivery pressure

At Alaya, our monolith started showing scaling cracks right as investors were pushing hard for new features, not a rewrite. My task was to make a defensible call that balanced long-term scalability against short-term delivery pressure, without a full rewrite eating the runway. I ran a two-week spike comparing a microservices rewrite against a modular monolith, backed it with prototype load tests, and brought the CTO and investors numbers instead of opinions. We landed on a strangler-fig approach: extract the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest monolithic, and define clear criteria for when to pull out the next piece. The first extracted module shipped in six weeks and cut latency on the busiest endpoints by around 40%, and the remaining monolith stayed stable all the way through the acquisition with no big-bang rewrite ever needed. The hard part wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the right call, and data got me there faster than argument did.

## 4. Cross-team architecture liaison after an acquisition

After Benevity acquired Alaya in 2021, I landed on a team roughly ten times the size of what I'd worked in, with a different codebase in Java and microservices and a different set of norms entirely. My task was to integrate Alaya's capabilities into that platform while still shipping new features, without losing the velocity we'd built as a four-person team. I spent the first month mapping the existing architecture before proposing anything, and built relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing technical opinions on them. Once I had that footing, I became the architecture liaison for the Europe-facing product surface and contributed to three major cross-team architecture decisions. Early on I tried fixing things that weren't actually broken, just unfamiliar, and slowing down to listen first is what turned that around, something I'd apply again working across whichever teams own IT ops here.
