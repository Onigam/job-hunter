# STAR Stories - Remy (Founding Backend/Infra Engineer)

Picked for a founding infra role: building from nothing, making an infra trade-off under pressure, and driving change without formal authority.

## 1. Building Alaya's platform from zero

In 2016 I co-founded Alaya, an employee purpose and impact platform, and started from a genuinely blank page: no customers, no product, no code. My job was to architect and build the whole thing myself while the business was closing its first enterprise clients, so there wasn't room for a slow ramp-up. I picked Node.js, React and Postgres because they let me move fast without locking us into anything exotic, and I had a working demo within three months. From there I kept expanding the platform piece by piece: multi-tenant architecture, an API layer, impact reporting, and eventually postal campaign features, while also hiring and onboarding the first three engineers and setting up CI/CD and a real code review culture. The platform ended up serving more than fifty enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity, a Canadian company valued north of a billion dollars, acquired us in 2021. Looking back, I'd have invested in observability much earlier since we bolted on proper APM late, and I underestimated how expensive it would be to retrofit multi-tenancy rather than designing for it from day one. That's exactly the kind of lesson I'd bring into a founding infra role: get the unglamorous foundations right before the product forces your hand.

## 2. Choosing a modular monolith over a rewrite

At Alaya, our monolith was starting to show real scaling cracks, and the team split into two camps: rewrite everything as microservices, or keep pushing forward with a modular monolith. Investors wanted new features, not a rewrite, so I had to make a call that held up technically without stalling the roadmap. I ran a two-week spike comparing both approaches with actual load tests rather than opinions, then presented the trade-offs to the CTO and investors with real numbers attached. What I proposed was a strangler-fig migration: pull out only the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest of the monolith alone, and define clear criteria for when we'd extract more. The first extracted module shipped in six weeks and cut latency on our busiest endpoints by around 40%, and the remaining monolith stayed stable all the way through the acquisition, so we never needed the big rewrite everyone was originally afraid of. The hard part wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the disciplined choice. Data cut the debate short faster than argument would have, and I'd push for even earlier benchmarking next time to skip some of that back and forth.

## 3. Driving AI-tooling adoption with no direct reports

After Benevity acquired Alaya, I joined as a Senior Staff IC on a team roughly ten times bigger than what I'd worked with before, with no direct reports of my own. I wanted to get AI-assisted development practices into the workflow of two teams that didn't answer to me, which meant influence had to do the work that authority usually does. I started small: three curious engineers who wanted to try it, some internal tooling demos that showed concrete time saved, and informal lunch-and-learn sessions with no mandate attached. I also built reusable prompt templates and committed them to a shared repo so people could adopt them on their own terms rather than because someone told them to. Within three months, about 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted review and generation, and I'd become the informal go-to person for AI tooling across the org. It was slower than a top-down rollout would have been, but it stuck, because the engineers who picked it up became the ones advocating for it internally. That's the model I'd use again in a small founding team: show, don't mandate.

## 4. Integrating into Benevity's stack without breaking velocity

Joining Benevity right after the acquisition meant landing on a team ten times larger, working in Java and microservices instead of the Node stack I'd built Alaya on, with different processes end to end. My task was to fold Alaya's capabilities into Benevity's platform while still shipping new features, without losing the speed we'd had as a small team. I spent the first month just mapping their existing architecture before I proposed a single change, and made a point of building relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing any technical opinions. That groundwork let me drive AI-augmented workflows, things like Copilot and shared prompt patterns, across the wider engineering org, and I ended up as the cross-team architecture liaison for the Europe-facing product surface. We migrated the core Alaya features successfully and I contributed to three major cross-team architecture calls in the process. My honest reflection is that I moved too fast early on trying to fix things that weren't actually broken in the new org; slowing down and listening first is what made the rest of it work, and it's a habit I'd carry into any new team, founding or otherwise.
