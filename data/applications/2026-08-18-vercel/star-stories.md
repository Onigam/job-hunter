# STAR Stories: Member of the Technical Staff, Internal Agent, Vercel

Picked for a role that's really three things stacked together: agent architecture, cross-system plumbing (UI, APIs, databases, infra all in one surface), and driving adoption of something new inside an org that didn't ask for it. These four lean into that shape.

## Building Alaya from zero to acquisition

In 2016 I co-founded Alaya, a purpose and impact platform for employee engagement, with nothing behind it, no customers, no product, just a blank page and a first enterprise client we'd already signed before we'd built anything for them. My job was to architect and build the whole thing, alone at first, while the business kept selling. I picked a stack that could scale without over-engineering it on day one, Node.js, React, Postgres, and pushed to get a working demo in the first three months. From there it was iteration: multi-tenant architecture, an API layer other teams and eventually customers plugged into, impact reporting, and along the way hiring the first three engineers and setting up the review culture we ran on for years. We got to production with roughly 50 enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and in 2021 Benevity, a Canadian unicorn, acquired the company. Looking back, I'd invest in observability a lot earlier, we bolted on proper APM late, and I underestimated how expensive retrofitting multi-tenancy would be. Building primitives that other teams can build on top of without breaking things is exactly the muscle this Alaya period built, and it's the same muscle the V role needs.

## Driving AI-assisted workflows without a mandate

At Benevity I was a Senior Staff IC with no direct reports, but I wanted two teams that didn't report to me to actually adopt AI-assisted development instead of treating it as a novelty. There was no mandate behind this, so I had to make people want it. I started with three curious engineers, built small internal demos that showed real time saved rather than talking about the idea abstractly, and ran informal lunch sessions with no obligation to attend. I put together reusable prompt templates in a shared repo and wrote up concrete results, one story was saving about two hours on a single PR review cycle. Within three months roughly 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted review or generation regularly, and I became the informal lead for it across the org without ever holding the title. What stuck with me is that the slower, no-mandate approach produced advocates who pushed the practice on their own terms, something a top-down rollout would never have gotten me. Getting an internal agent adopted inside an engineering org that already has strong opinions about its own tools is the same problem, just with a product this time instead of a habit.

## A hard architecture call under investor pressure

At Alaya, our monolith was starting to crack under load, and the team was split between a full microservices rewrite and a more modest modular monolith, while investors wanted new features shipped, not months spent on a rewrite. I had to make a call that held up technically without stalling the business. I ran a two-week spike comparing both approaches with actual load tests, then took the CTO and investors through the trade-offs with numbers instead of opinions. What I proposed was a strangler fig migration: pull out the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest of the monolith alone, and set clear criteria for when we'd extract the next piece. The first module shipped in six weeks and cut latency by about 40% on our busiest endpoints, and the rest of the monolith stayed stable straight through the acquisition, no big rewrite ever needed. The hardest part honestly wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the right call. Deciding how much of an agent's architecture to build up front versus let evolve is the same kind of judgment call, and I'd rather make it with a spike and real numbers than a strong opinion.

## Integrating after the acquisition

The acquisition wasn't the end of the hard part, it was the start of a different one. I joined Benevity as a Senior Staff Engineer on a team roughly ten times the size of Alaya's, a different codebase in Java and microservices, and a culture I didn't know yet. My task was to fold Alaya's capabilities into their platform while still shipping new features, without losing the speed that made Alaya work in the first place. I spent the first month just reading the architecture and talking to people before I suggested a single change, which felt slow at the time but paid off, because by the time I did push opinions, the platform and infra teams already trusted that I understood what I was proposing to touch. We migrated the core Alaya features in successfully, and I ended up part of three major cross-team architecture calls. Working across UI, APIs, and infra boundaries without owning any single one of them outright, and getting other teams to trust what you're building there, is close to daily life for this role, not an edge case.

## Why this matters for the V role specifically

Building V means touching product surfaces (Slack, web), the reasoning layer underneath, and the eval and observability systems that prove any of it actually works, while other teams start contributing capabilities into something you don't fully control anymore. I've built the platform that other teams plug into, driven adoption of an AI practice nobody asked for, and made the call on how much architecture to build up front versus defer. That combination, not a generic comfort with ambiguity, is what I'd bring to this one.
