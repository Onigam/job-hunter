# STAR Stories: Bjak, Machine Learning Platform Engineer

Prepared for a platform-infrastructure role supporting A1, Bjak's AI assistant. Picked for relevance to building infra from scratch, making production trade-offs under pressure, driving AI tooling adoption without formal authority, and working inside an unfamiliar codebase after a structural change.

---

## Story 1: Building a platform from zero (Alaya)

Back in 2016 I co-founded Alaya, a purpose and impact platform for employee engagement, and there was nothing to point to yet: no customers, no product, just a blank page and a deadline to sign the first enterprise clients. I ended up owning the architecture end to end, alone at first and then with a small team I hired and trained as we went. I picked Node.js, React, and PostgreSQL specifically because it let us move fast without boxing us in, and we had a working demo inside three months. From there it was constant expansion: multi-tenancy, an API layer, reporting, and eventually a CI/CD setup and a code review culture that didn't exist on day one. By the time Benevity, a Canadian unicorn valued north of a billion dollars, acquired us in 2021, we had 50-plus enterprise clients across Switzerland and France running on that platform. If I did it again, I'd bring in observability much earlier; we bolted on proper monitoring late, and retrofitting multi-tenancy afterward cost us more than designing for it from day zero would have.

## Story 2: A hard infrastructure trade-off under pressure (Alaya)

At Alaya, our monolith started showing real scaling cracks right as investors were pushing hard for new features, not a rewrite. The team was split between a full microservices rewrite and something more modest, and I needed a decision that would hold up technically and survive a room full of people with opinions. I ran a two-week spike comparing both approaches with actual load tests, not slideware, and brought the numbers to the CTO and investors instead of arguing from instinct. What I proposed was a strangler-fig migration: pull out the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest alone, and set clear criteria for when to extract more later. The first module shipped in six weeks, the busiest endpoints got roughly 40% faster, and the monolith stayed stable all the way through the acquisition, no big-bang rewrite required. The hard part wasn't the technical call, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the disciplined choice. Data got me there faster than argument did, and next time I'd run the benchmarks even earlier to shorten that debate.

## Story 3: Driving AI tooling adoption with no direct reports (Benevity)

At Benevity I was a Senior Staff IC with no one reporting to me, and I wanted two teams to change how they worked day to day by folding AI-assisted development into their normal flow. Mandating it wasn't an option, so I started small: three engineers who were already curious became the first adopters, and I built internal demos that showed concrete time saved rather than talking in the abstract. From there it was informal lunch-and-learn sessions with no mandate attached, plus a shared repo of prompt templates people could actually reuse instead of reinventing. One story that stuck was an engineer who saved two hours on a single PR review cycle using a pattern from that repo. Within three months, about 70% of engineers across both teams had it in their workflow, and I'd become the person people asked about AI tooling without ever holding a title for it. It was slower than a top-down rollout would have been, but it stuck because people adopted it on their own terms and then argued for it to their peers, which I couldn't have manufactured with a decree.

## Story 4: Navigating a post-acquisition integration (Benevity)

Joining Benevity after the acquisition put me on a team roughly ten times the size of Alaya, working in Java and microservices instead of the Node stack I'd built for five years. My job was folding Alaya's capabilities into their platform while still shipping new features, without losing the speed we'd had as a small team. I spent the first month mostly listening, mapping their existing architecture before proposing anything, and building relationships with the platform and infra teams before I pushed a single technical opinion. That patience paid off: I ended up contributing to three major cross-team architecture decisions and became a go-to liaison for the Europe-facing side of the product. Early on I moved faster than the org was ready for, trying to fix things that weren't actually broken, just unfamiliar to me. Slowing down and understanding the system on its own terms first, rather than mine, is what made the rest of it work.
