# STAR Stories: Cloro (Founding Engineer)

## 1. Building Alaya from zero to acquisition

Back in 2016 I cofounded Alaya, a purpose and impact platform for employee engagement, and we started with literally nothing: no customers, no code, no product. My job was to architect and build the whole thing myself at first, then grow a small team around it, all while the business was already signing its first enterprise clients, so there wasn't much room to get it wrong slowly. I picked a pragmatic stack, Node.js, React, PostgreSQL, that could scale without turning into an over-engineered mess before we even had users. I got the core features shipped in the first three months so we could run a real demo, then kept expanding from there: multi-tenant architecture, an API layer, impact reporting, postal campaign tooling. Along the way I hired and onboarded our first three engineers and set up the basics of CI/CD and code review that let us move fast without breaking things constantly. We ended up with 50-plus enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and in 2021 Benevity, a Canadian unicorn valued north of a billion dollars, acquired us. Looking back, I'd have invested in proper observability much earlier than we did, and I underestimated how expensive it would be to retrofit multi-tenancy after the fact. If I did it again, I'd design for that from day zero instead of bolting it on later.

## 2. A hard architecture call under investor pressure

At Alaya, our monolith started showing real scaling cracks, and the team split into two camps: rewrite everything as microservices, or keep it as a modular monolith. Investors, meanwhile, wanted new features, not months of internal rewrites. I had to make a call that balanced long-term scalability against short-term delivery, and defend it to people who weren't engineers. So I ran a two-week technical spike, built prototypes for both approaches, and load-tested them instead of arguing from opinion. I brought the results to the CTO and investors as numbers, not preferences, and proposed a strangler-fig migration: pull out the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest alone, and define clear criteria for when to extract the next piece. We shipped the first extracted module in six weeks, and the busiest endpoints got about 40% faster. The rest of the monolith stayed stable straight through the acquisition, no big-bang rewrite required. The hard part wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the disciplined choice. Data made that argument for me. Next time I'd try to get the benchmarks in front of people even earlier, just to shorten the debate.

## 3. Driving adoption with no formal authority

After Benevity acquired us, I was a Senior Staff IC with no direct reports, but I wanted to get AI-assisted development tools into the daily workflow of two engineering teams that didn't answer to me. Nobody was going to mandate this from the top, so I had to make people want it. I started with three curious engineers, built small internal demos that showed concrete time savings rather than talking in the abstract, and ran informal lunch-and-learn sessions with zero mandate attached. I put together reusable prompt templates and committed them to a shared repo so the good habits could spread on their own. Within three months, about 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted review and code generation regularly, and I'd become the person people went to for that kind of tooling question. It was slower than a top-down rollout would have been, but it stuck, because people adopted it because it actually helped them, not because someone told them to. That's a lesson I keep coming back to: influence without authority works better when you let the results do the persuading.

## 4. Navigating a much bigger, much different org

Joining Benevity after the acquisition meant landing on a team roughly ten times the size of Alaya, with a different codebase (Java, microservices instead of our monolith), different culture, different everything. My task was to fold Alaya's capabilities into Benevity's platform while still shipping new features, without losing the speed we'd built our reputation on. I spent the first month just mapping their existing architecture before proposing a single change, and made a point of building relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing any technical opinions on them. From there I helped drive adoption of AI-augmented workflows across the org and ended up as a cross-team architecture liaison for the Europe-facing product surface. We migrated the core Alaya features successfully, I contributed to three major cross-team architecture decisions, and became a go-to person for AI tooling across two teams. Early on I moved too fast trying to fix things in the new org that genuinely weren't broken, just different. Slowing down and listening first turned out to make me far more effective than showing up with answers.
