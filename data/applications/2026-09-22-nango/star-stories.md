# STAR Stories: Staff Engineer, Platform & Infrastructure, Nango

Picked for a role that's explicitly about owning the cloud layer end to end on a small team, leading BYOC toward something productized, and making reliability a feature customers can trust rather than a promise. These lean toward standing up infrastructure from nothing, making a scaling call under real pressure, operating inside a platform much bigger than the one I started with, and driving change on teams that don't report to me, since that's close to what a first platform hire does day to day.

## Building Alaya's infrastructure from zero

In 2016 I co-founded Alaya, an employee purpose and impact platform, with nothing built and an enterprise client already signed. My job was to architect and run the infrastructure alone at first, on AWS, with Docker and Postgres underneath a Node.js backend, while the business kept selling around me. I got a working demo out in the first three months, then spent the next few years building out a multi-tenant data layer, an API surface other services could build on, and the operational habits, CI/CD, code review, basic monitoring, that let a growing team ship without stepping on each other. We got to production serving over 50 enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and the infrastructure held through Benevity's acquisition of the company in 2021 without a rewrite. If I did it again I'd invest in observability much earlier, we bolted on proper monitoring late, and I'd design the multi-tenancy in from day zero instead of retrofitting it under load. That's a lesson that matters more when you're the only person who can see the whole system.

## A load-tested call instead of a rewrite

By year three, Alaya's monolith was starting to crack under load, and the team was split between a full microservices rewrite and a smaller, modular approach, while investors wanted features shipped, not months spent rebuilding infrastructure. I needed a call that would hold up technically without stalling the business. I ran a two-week spike comparing both approaches against real load tests, then walked the CTO and investors through the numbers instead of arguing opinions. What I proposed was a strangler fig migration: pull the two highest-traffic modules out first, leave the rest of the monolith alone, and define clear criteria for extracting anything further. The first module shipped in six weeks and cut latency on our busiest endpoints by roughly 40%, and the system stayed stable straight through the acquisition with no big rewrite ever needed. The hard part wasn't the engineering, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the right call, and the load-test data did most of that convincing. Next time I'd front-load even more benchmarking so the argument takes less time to win. It's the same discipline I'd want behind an SLO before calling it meaningful.

## Learning someone else's platform fast

Joining Benevity after the acquisition meant landing on a team roughly ten times the size of Alaya's, running infrastructure I hadn't touched before, on conventions I didn't set. The task was to fold Alaya's systems into that platform while still shipping new work, without losing the operational speed that had made Alaya function with a small team. I spent the first month reading the architecture and talking to the platform and infra people before proposing a single change, which felt slow at the time but meant that once I did push an opinion, people already trusted I understood what I was touching. The migration went in cleanly, and I ended up part of three major cross-team architecture decisions afterward, including ones that touched how the org handled security review for anything customer-facing. Looking back, I moved too fast early trying to fix things that weren't actually broken in the new org, and slowing down to listen first is what turned that around. That's close to the posture I'd want walking into infrastructure a dozen other engineers already depend on.

## Getting two teams to adopt a new practice without a mandate

At Benevity I was a Senior Staff IC with no direct reports, but I wanted two teams outside my own to actually change how they worked, in this case adopting AI-assisted development, rather than treat it as a novelty. There was no mandate behind it, so I had to make people want it. I started with three curious engineers, built small internal demos that showed real time saved instead of pitching the idea in the abstract, and ran informal sessions nobody was obligated to attend. I wrote up concrete outcomes, one story was saving about two hours on a single PR review cycle, and put reusable templates in a shared repo anyone could pull from. Within three months roughly 70% of engineers across both teams were using it regularly, and I became the informal lead for it without ever holding the title. The slower, no-mandate approach produced people who advocated for the practice on their own terms, which a top-down rollout never would have gotten me. A fourteen-person engineering team doesn't have room for top-down mandates either, and stepping up without waiting for permission is most of what the role seems to ask for.
