# STAR Stories: Cosuno, Principal Full Stack Developer (TypeScript)

Picked for this role because Cosuno is looking for someone who can reason across the full stack, own features end to end, and mentor other engineers on architecture. These four cover building from zero, a hard technical trade-off, integrating into a much bigger org, and influencing without formal authority.

## 1. Building Alaya from zero (founding engineer, ambiguity)

In 2016 I co-founded Alaya, a purpose and impact platform for employee engagement, and started with a blank page: no customers, no product, no team. My job was to architect and build the whole thing, alone at first, while the business was already trying to sign its first enterprise clients. I picked a pragmatic stack, Node.js, React and Postgres, that could scale without forcing early over-engineering, and got the core features to a working demo in about three months. From there I kept expanding: multi-tenant architecture, an API layer, impact reporting, postal campaigns, and eventually hiring and onboarding the first three engineers along with CI/CD and a real code review culture. The platform ended up serving 50-plus enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity, a Canadian unicorn valued at over a billion dollars, acquired the company in 2021. If I did it again I'd invest in observability much earlier since we only added proper APM late, and I'd design for multi-tenancy from day zero instead of retrofitting it.

## 2. The strangler-fig migration (architecture under pressure)

At Alaya, our monolith started showing scaling cracks right as investors were pushing hard for new features rather than a rewrite. The team was split between a full microservices rewrite and a more modest modular monolith, and I needed a decision that held up technically without stalling the roadmap. I ran a two-week spike comparing both approaches with actual load tests, then presented the trade-offs to the CTO and investors using those numbers instead of opinions. Rather than a big-bang rewrite, I proposed and led a strangler-fig migration: pull out the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest of the monolith alone, and define clear criteria for when to extract more. We shipped the first extracted module in six weeks, and performance on the busiest endpoints improved by roughly 40%. The rest of the monolith stayed stable straight through the acquisition, so no rewrite was ever needed. The hardest part honestly wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the right call; having real data made that argument much shorter, and next time I'd run the benchmarking even earlier to cut the debate down further.

## 3. Integrating into a much bigger engineering org (cross-team collaboration)

When Benevity acquired Alaya in 2021, I joined as Senior Staff Engineer on a team roughly ten times the size of what I'd worked in before, with a different culture and a Java microservices codebase I didn't know. My task was to fold Alaya's capabilities into Benevity's platform while still shipping new features, without losing the product speed we'd built up. I spent the first month just mapping the existing architecture before proposing anything, and made a point of building relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing my own technical opinions. That groundwork let me become a cross-team architecture liaison for the Europe-facing product surface and contribute to three major architecture decisions, while also migrating the core Alaya features over successfully. My early instinct was to move fast and "fix" things that weren't actually broken in the new org; slowing down and listening first is what actually made me effective, and it's a lesson I'd apply directly to joining a smaller, established team like Cosuno's.

## 4. Driving AI-assisted engineering practices without authority (mentoring, influence)

At Benevity I was a Senior Staff IC with no direct reports, but I wanted two teams that didn't report to me to actually adopt AI-assisted development instead of treating it as a novelty. I started small, with three curious engineers who wanted to try it, built internal demos that showed concrete time savings, and ran informal lunch-and-learn sessions with no mandate behind them, just working examples. I put together reusable prompt templates in a shared repo and documented wins as they happened, like a specific case where AI tooling saved about two hours on a single PR review cycle. Within three months, around 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted review and generation, and I ended up as the informal AI tooling lead for the org. It was slower than a top-down mandate would have been, but it stuck better, because engineers who opted in on their own terms ended up advocating for it to their peers, which is exactly the kind of "raise the technical bar" mentoring this Cosuno role is asking for.
