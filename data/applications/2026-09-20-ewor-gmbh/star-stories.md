# STAR+R Stories, Fintech Founding Engineer, EWOR GmbH

Picked for this role because it's a 0-to-1 build, reporting directly to a department founder, with an explicit goal of positioning the venture for institutional backing later. That maps closely to founding Alaya, the architecture call under pressure at Alaya, and driving adoption without formal authority at Benevity, so those are the three I'd lead with. I've added a fourth on convincing non-technical stakeholders to back a platform investment, since "position the department for growth and institutional backing" is really an investor-facing problem as much as a technical one.

## Story 1: Building Alaya from a blank page to acquisition

In 2016 I co-founded Alaya, an employee purpose and impact platform, and there was nothing to work from. No customers, no product, just a business that was already out talking to enterprise clients before the platform existed to back it up. My job was to architect and build the whole thing, alone at first, while sales kept moving. I picked Node.js, React, and PostgreSQL specifically because they'd let us move fast without boxing us into a rewrite later, and I pushed to get a working demo in front of the first prospects within three months. From there it was constant expansion: multi-tenant architecture, an API layer, impact reporting, and eventually onboarding the first three engineers along with the CI/CD and code review habits that let more than one person touch the codebase safely. Five years in, Alaya was running in production for more than 50 enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity, a Canadian company valued north of a billion dollars, acquired us. That's the clearest signal I have that the early calls held up under real growth. If I'm honest, I'd have put proper observability in much earlier than I did, and I underestimated how expensive it would be to retrofit multi-tenancy after the fact rather than designing for it from day zero. That's a mistake I wouldn't repeat starting a fintech build from scratch, where the cost of getting the data model wrong compounds even faster.

## Story 2: Choosing not to rewrite, under investor pressure

At Alaya, the monolith started showing real scaling cracks around the same time investors were pushing hard for new features, not a rewrite. The team was split on whether to go full microservices or stay monolithic, and neither camp had much more than opinions to argue from. I ran a two-week spike, built prototypes of both approaches, and load-tested them so the conversation with the CTO and investors could run on numbers instead of preference. What I proposed was a strangler fig migration: pull out the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest of the monolith alone, and set explicit criteria for when a module earned the right to be extracted next. We shipped the first extraction in six weeks, and the busiest endpoints got about 40% faster. No big-bang rewrite, and the remaining monolith held up fine all the way through the acquisition. The hard part wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that doing less was the right call, and the data is what closed that argument faster than my opinion ever could have. For a founding role where I'd be architecting a fintech platform under similar pressure to ship features while investors watch the runway, that's exactly the muscle I'd be using again.

## Story 3: Getting two teams to adopt something with no mandate

After Benevity acquired Alaya, I ended up a Senior Staff IC on a team ten times the size of anything I'd worked on, with no direct reports and no authority to tell anyone how to work. I wanted two teams that didn't report to me to start using AI-assisted development practices, and a top-down push wasn't an option I had. So I started small: three engineers who were already curious, a few internal demos that showed real time saved rather than a pitch deck, and informal lunch sessions with no attendance requirement. I built reusable prompt templates and put them in a shared repo so the win was easy to copy, and I documented concrete cases, like shaving two hours off a single PR review cycle, so the argument was never abstract. Within three months, about 70% of engineers across both teams had picked it up on their own, and I'd become the person people came to for AI tooling questions across the org. It was slower than a mandate would have been, but it stuck, because people adopted it because it worked for them, not because someone told them to. Reporting to a department founder with maximum autonomy sounds like the same shape of problem: getting a small team to move fast and actually believe in the direction, not just comply with it.

## Story 4: Making the case for platform investment to non-technical stakeholders

Early at Alaya, I had to convince the CTO and, indirectly, the board that time spent on multi-tenant architecture and CI/CD was worth delaying a couple of feature requests for. Nobody outside engineering cared about the architecture itself, they cared about not losing the next enterprise deal to a platform that couldn't onboard a new client without a code change. So I framed the ask entirely around that: how many weeks a proper onboarding flow would save on every future client, and what a security incident from a shared-tenant bug would cost us with a client like the ones already in the pipeline. That got the investment approved without much resistance, because it was never really a technical pitch, it was a risk and speed pitch dressed in technical clothes. For a role that explicitly includes positioning a department for institutional backing, I think that's the actual skill being asked for: being able to explain why the architecture choices you're making now are the reason the thing will still work when someone with real money starts asking hard questions about it.
