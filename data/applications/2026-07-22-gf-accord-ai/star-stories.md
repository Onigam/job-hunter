# STAR Stories, GF-ACCORD AI (CTO and Head of IT, AI)

Picked for a Head of IT / CTO role at an early-stage, pre-funded AI company. The through-line: building technical infrastructure from nothing, making trust-critical architecture calls, and driving adoption of AI practices without a mandate. All should apply directly to standing up GF-ACCORD's stack and team from scratch.

---

## 1. Building Alaya from zero to acquisition

In 2016 I co-founded Alaya, a purpose and impact platform for employee engagement, and there was nothing to inherit: no product, no customers, no infrastructure. I had to architect and build the whole thing myself at first, while the business side was already out signing enterprise clients, so the pressure to ship something real was immediate rather than theoretical. I picked a pragmatic stack, Node.js, React, PostgreSQL, that could scale without forcing us into premature complexity, and got a working demo out inside three months. From there it was constant expansion: multi-tenant architecture, an API layer, impact reporting, campaign tooling, plus hiring and onboarding the first three engineers and putting real CI/CD and code review habits in place before the team got big enough that bad habits would stick. The platform ended up running production for 50-plus enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and in 2021 Benevity, a Canadian company valued north of a billion dollars, acquired it, which was about as direct a validation of those early technical calls as you can get. If I did it again I'd invest in observability much earlier; we bolted on proper APM late, and retrofitting multi-tenancy cost more than designing for it from day zero would have.

## 2. Making a hard architecture decision under pressure

At Alaya, the monolith we'd built started showing real scaling cracks, and the team split into two camps: rewrite everything as microservices, or keep it as a modular monolith. Investors, meanwhile, wanted new features shipped, not months spent on plumbing nobody outside engineering would see. My job was to land on a decision that held up technically without blowing the delivery calendar. I ran a two-week spike comparing both approaches with actual load tests rather than opinions, then took the results to the CTO and investors as numbers, not a pitch. What came out of that was a strangler-fig migration: extract the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest alone, and define concrete criteria for when the next module earns extraction. The first extracted module shipped in six weeks and cut latency on our busiest endpoints by around 40%, and the rest of the monolith stayed stable straight through the acquisition, so the big-bang rewrite never had to happen. The hard part wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was in fact the disciplined choice. Data made that argument for me, and next time I'd front-load even more of the benchmarking to shorten the debate.

## 3. Leading AI tooling adoption without formal authority

At Benevity I was a Senior Staff IC with no direct reports, and I wanted two teams that didn't answer to me to actually change how they worked by bringing AI tooling into their daily workflow. Mandating that wasn't an option, so I started small: three engineers who were already curious became the first users, and I built internal demos that showed concrete time saved rather than making abstract claims about productivity. From there it was informal lunch-and-learn sessions, no mandate attached, just people showing other people what worked, plus a shared repo of reusable prompt templates and documented wins, like the time a PR review cycle got two hours shorter. Within three months, about 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted review and generation, and I'd become the person people asked when they wanted to get AI tooling working for their own team. It was slower than a top-down rollout would have been, but stickier, because engineers who opted in on their own terms ended up selling it internally better than I ever could have from a deck. That's the same bet a company like GF-ACCORD is making at the product level: getting people to trust an AI system enough to actually rely on its output, one demonstrated result at a time.

## 4. Integrating into a much larger, more structured organization

When Benevity acquired Alaya in 2021, I went from a five-person team where I made most of the calls to a Senior Staff Engineer seat on an org roughly ten times the size, with a different codebase, Java and microservices instead of what we'd built, and processes built for scale rather than speed. My task was to fold Alaya's capabilities into that platform while still shipping new features, without losing the velocity that had gotten us acquired in the first place. I spent the first month just mapping the existing architecture before I proposed a single change, and built relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing any technical opinion their way. That groundwork let me drive adoption of AI-assisted engineering practices org-wide and become an architecture liaison for the Europe-facing product surface, and I ended up contributing to three major cross-team architecture decisions in that first year. Where I got it wrong early on was moving too fast trying to fix things in the new org that weren't actually broken, they just looked unfamiliar. Slowing down and listening first is what made the rest of it work, and it's the instinct I'd bring to reporting into a board and investors who need to trust the technical story before they trust the product.
