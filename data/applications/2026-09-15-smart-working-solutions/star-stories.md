# STAR Stories: Smart Working Solutions (Founding Engineer, Full-Stack Generalist)

Prepared for a solo founding-engineer role on an AI-native PSA platform. Stories chosen for high ambiguity, end-to-end ownership, and working directly with founders rather than a structured eng org.

---

## 1. Building Alaya from a blank page to acquisition

This is the one I'd lead with for a founding-engineer conversation, since it's the closest match to what's being asked here. In 2016 I co-founded Alaya, an employee purpose and impact platform, with no customers and no product yet, just an idea the business side was already starting to sell. My job was to architect and build the whole thing from zero, alone at first, while the company signed its first enterprise clients in parallel. I picked a pragmatic stack, Node.js, React, PostgreSQL, that could scale without forcing me to over-engineer for problems we didn't have yet, and pushed to get a working demo in front of the first prospects within three months. From there it was constant iteration: multi-tenant architecture, an API layer, impact reporting, postal campaigns, whatever the next enterprise deal actually needed. Once revenue justified it, I hired and onboarded the first three engineers and put in place CI/CD and a real code review culture before the codebase got big enough that skipping it would've hurt. The platform ended up running production for more than fifty enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity, a Canadian unicorn, acquired the company in 2021, which was the clearest validation I could've asked for on those early calls. If I'm honest, I'd have invested in observability much earlier; we bolted on proper monitoring later than we should have, and I underestimated how expensive it would be to retrofit multi-tenancy instead of designing for it from day one.

## 2. Choosing not to rewrite the monolith

At Alaya we hit the point every growing product hits, where the monolith starts showing scaling cracks and half the team wants a full microservices rewrite while investors want new features, not a multi-month infrastructure detour. I was the one who had to make a call that would hold up technically and be defensible to people who weren't engineers. Instead of arguing from opinion, I ran a two-week spike comparing both approaches with actual load tests, then brought the CTO and investors numbers instead of a preference. What I proposed was a strangler-fig migration: pull out the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest of the monolith alone, and set clear criteria for when the next module would earn the same treatment. We shipped the first extracted module in six weeks, the busiest endpoints got about 40% faster, and the remaining monolith stayed stable all the way through the acquisition process without ever needing the big rewrite. The hard part wasn't the technical work, it was convincing a room that "don't rewrite everything" was the ambitious answer, not the timid one. Data got me there faster than argument did, and next time I'd start the benchmarking earlier just to shorten that debate.

## 3. Getting two teams to adopt AI tooling with no authority to make them

After the Benevity acquisition I joined as a Senior Staff IC on a team about ten times the size of Alaya, different codebase, different culture, no direct reports, and I wanted two teams that didn't answer to me to change how they worked day to day by folding AI-assisted development into their workflow. Issuing a mandate wasn't an option and wouldn't have worked anyway, so I started small: three curious engineers, a few internal demos that showed concrete time saved rather than a slide about the future of engineering, and informal lunch sessions with no attendance requirement. I built reusable prompt templates, put them in a shared repo, and wrote up specific wins as they happened, like a PR review cycle that dropped by two hours. Within three months about 70% of engineers on both teams were using AI-assisted review and generation, and I'd become the person people asked when they wanted to get started. It was slower than a top-down rollout would have been, but it stuck, because the engineers who adopted it did so on their own terms and then went and told their peers it was worth it. I couldn't have gotten the same result by decree, and I try to remember that whenever I'm tempted to move faster by skipping the buy-in step.

## 4. Learning to listen before touching someone else's architecture

The month right after the Benevity acquisition taught me something I still use. I'd spent five years being the person who made the calls at Alaya, and I walked into Benevity with opinions about their Java microservices setup that I hadn't earned the standing to voice yet. My task was to integrate Alaya's capabilities into their platform without losing the product velocity we'd built, and my first instinct was to start proposing fixes for things that struck me as inefficient. I caught myself moving too fast within the first few weeks and switched approach: spent a full month just mapping their existing architecture and building relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing any technical opinion at all. That patience is what let me become a cross-team architecture liaison for the Europe-facing surface and contribute meaningfully to three major architecture decisions afterward, instead of being the outsider nobody wanted to loop in. Slowing down felt counterproductive in the moment, but it's the reason people actually listened once I did have something to say. Going into a solo founding role, that instinct, understand the existing system and the founders' constraints before reaching for the keyboard, matters even more, since there's no team to catch me if I get it wrong.
