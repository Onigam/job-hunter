# STAR Stories: Staff DevSecOps Engineer, ID Quantique

## 1. Setting up CI/CD and delivery standards from nothing

Best for: internal developer platforms, software factory ownership, bootstrapping a pipeline where none existed.

When I co-founded Alaya in 2016 there was no pipeline to inherit and no process to fall back on. I picked Node.js, React and PostgreSQL because they let me move fast without building infrastructure the company didn't need yet, and spent the first three months getting to a working demo on my own. Once the first three engineers came on board, my job stopped being about writing features and became about giving them something solid to build inside. I set up CI/CD, wrote the first code review guidelines, and kept architecture docs current instead of letting decisions live only in my head. That groundwork is a big part of why a team that size shipped to fifty-plus enterprise clients across Switzerland and France without the codebase turning into a liability. What I got wrong was waiting too long on proper observability, we ran on ad hoc logging well past the point where real instrumentation would have caught incidents in minutes instead of hours. If I were standing up a software factory again, monitoring and audit trails would go in on day one, not after the first outage makes the case for me.

## 2. Choosing a phased migration over a rewrite, under delivery pressure

Best for: release engineering trade-offs, defending a technical decision with data, managing risk in a live system.

A few years into Alaya, our monolith started buckling under real production load, and the team split between a full microservices rewrite and just absorbing the pain a while longer. Investors wanted new features shipped, not a multi-month infrastructure project with nothing visible to show for it, so whatever I proposed had to survive scrutiny without stalling the roadmap. I ran a two-week spike comparing both paths with actual load tests, then brought the CTO and investors real numbers instead of an opinion. The plan that came out of it was a strangler-fig extraction: pull the two highest-traffic modules out first, leave everything else alone, and set explicit criteria for extracting more later. The first module shipped in six weeks, and latency on the busiest endpoints improved by roughly 40%. The rest of the monolith held stable straight through the acquisition, and we never did the big rewrite people had braced for. The hard part wasn't the engineering, it was convincing a room that the less dramatic option was the responsible one. I'd front-load the benchmarking earlier next time so that argument doesn't eat as much calendar time.

## 3. Driving AI-assisted and agentic tooling adoption with no mandate

Best for: agentic automation for pipeline operations, influence without authority, mentoring across teams you don't manage.

At Benevity I wanted two teams that had no obligation to listen to me to start using AI-assisted development practices, and there was no manager telling them to. Issuing a directive wasn't an option, and it wouldn't have stuck even if it were, so I started with three engineers who were already curious. I built small internal demos showing concrete time saved, ran informal sessions with nothing mandatory about them, and wrote reusable prompt templates that I committed to a shared repo so people could take what worked and ignore the rest. I documented specific outcomes too, like one engineer cutting a couple of hours off a single PR review cycle, because a vague claim that something helps convinces nobody. Within about three months, something like 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted review and generation on their own, and I'd become the person people came to with tooling questions without anyone appointing me. It was slower than a top-down rollout, but the engineers who adopted it on their own terms ended up defending it internally in a way a mandate never produces. That's the pattern I'd bring to rolling out agentic automation against a delivery pipeline: prove the time saved before asking anyone to trust it near production.

## 4. Earning the standing to set standards across a much larger org

Best for: cross-team engineering standards, working in unfamiliar codebases, operating without formal authority after an acquisition.

After Benevity acquired Alaya in 2021, I landed on a team roughly ten times larger than anything I'd worked in, a different codebase, different processes, and no direct reports. The task was folding Alaya's capabilities into Benevity's platform without slowing new feature work or stepping on toes I didn't yet understand. I spent the first month mostly listening, mapping the existing architecture before proposing anything, and building relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing a single technical opinion their way. That patience paid off. I became the cross-team architecture liaison for the Europe-facing product surface, and other teams started looping me in before changes touching a Tier 1 service went out. Early on I moved too fast trying to fix things that were unfamiliar rather than actually broken, and that cost some credibility I had to earn back. The lesson that stuck is that standards only hold when the people already doing the work trust you understand what they're protecting, and that trust comes before the rules, not after.
