# STAR Stories: VP of Engineering @ Qdrant

## 1. Building a platform from zero to acquisition

Best for: "how do you build an engineering org from nothing" / founding-team scaling questions.

In 2016 I co-founded Alaya, an employee purpose and impact platform, and I was the first and only engineer for a while. There was no product yet, just a pitch and a couple of early conversations with prospective enterprise clients. My job was to pick a stack that wouldn't fall over once we had real customers, and to get something demoable fast enough that sales could start closing. I went with Node.js, React and PostgreSQL because I could move quickly without betting the company on infrastructure we didn't need yet. The first three months went into getting to a working demo, then I spent the next couple of years building out multi-tenancy, an API layer, impact reporting and campaign tooling as the client list grew. Somewhere in there I hired and onboarded our first three engineers and put in place code review and basic architecture documentation, mostly because I was tired of being the only person who understood how anything worked. By the time Benevity acquired us in 2021, we had over fifty enterprise clients across Switzerland and France running on that platform, which told me the early calls, stack choice included, had mostly been right. If I'm honest, I'd invest in observability a lot earlier next time. We didn't have real APM until fairly late, and multi-tenancy retrofitted onto code that wasn't built for it cost more than it should have.

## 2. Choosing a strangler-fig migration over a rewrite

Best for: technical trade-off questions, how to balance engineering excellence with delivery pressure.

By our third or fourth year at Alaya, the original monolith was starting to show real strain under load, and the team was split between rewriting it as microservices and just living with it a while longer. Investors wanted new features, not a multi-month infrastructure project with no visible output. I needed a call that would hold up technically without stalling the roadmap, so I ran a two-week spike comparing both paths with actual load tests instead of opinions, then took the results to the CTO and investors. What I proposed was a strangler-fig approach: pull out the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest of the monolith alone, and set clear criteria for when we'd extract anything else. The first module shipped in six weeks and performance on our busiest endpoints improved by roughly 40%. The rest of the monolith stayed stable straight through the acquisition, and we never did the big-bang rewrite everyone was scared of. The harder part honestly wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the responsible choice. Data made that argument for me, though next time I'd front-load the benchmarking so the debate doesn't drag as long.

## 3. Driving adoption without formal authority

Best for: "how do you lead engineers who don't report to you" / culture and rigor questions.

After Benevity acquired Alaya, I ended up as a Senior Staff IC on a team roughly ten times the size I was used to, no direct reports, and I wanted to get AI-assisted development practices adopted across two teams that had zero reason to listen to me. Ordering people to use new tooling wasn't an option and wouldn't have worked anyway, so I started with three engineers who were already curious, built small internal demos that showed concrete time saved, and ran informal lunch sessions with no mandate attached. I wrote up reusable prompt templates and committed them to a shared repo, and documented specific wins, like one engineer saving a couple of hours on a single PR review cycle. Within about three months, something like 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted review and generation regularly, and I'd become the person people went to for AI tooling questions across the org without anyone appointing me to that. It was slower than a top-down rollout would have been, but the engineers who picked it up on their own terms ended up advocating for it internally in a way a mandate never would have produced.

## 4. Integrating into a much larger org after acquisition

Best for: cross-functional collaboration, navigating scale and process change.

Joining Benevity after the acquisition meant a ten-times-larger team, a different language, Java instead of what I'd used at Alaya, and processes I hadn't designed. My task was to get Alaya's capabilities folded into Benevity's platform without slowing down on new features, and without stepping on toes I didn't yet understand the shape of. I spent the first month mostly listening, mapping out the existing architecture before I proposed anything, and building relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing any technical opinions their way. That patience paid off. I ended up contributing to three major cross-team architecture decisions and became the informal architecture liaison for the Europe-facing product surface. Early on I did move too fast trying to fix things that weren't actually broken, just unfamiliar, and that cost me some credibility I had to rebuild. Slowing down and listening first turned out to matter more than being right quickly.
