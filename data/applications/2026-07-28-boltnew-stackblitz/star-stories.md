# STAR+R Stories: Senior Applied AI Engineer, Bolt.new (StackBlitz)

Picked for relevance to this role: ownership of ambiguous technical problems, data-driven trade-offs under pressure, and driving adoption of AI-assisted engineering without formal authority. Sourced from `profile/star-bank.md`.

## Building Alaya from zero to acquisition

When I co-founded Alaya in 2016, we had nothing: no product, no customers, just an idea about employee purpose and impact. I was the one who had to architect and build the whole platform, first completely alone, then slowly growing a small team while the business chased its first enterprise contracts. I picked Node.js, React and PostgreSQL, a stack I could actually move fast in without over-engineering it early, and got a working demo out within three months. From there I kept expanding: multi-tenant architecture, an API layer, impact reporting, postal campaign tooling. I also hired and onboarded our first three engineers and put in place CI/CD and a real code review culture, because a platform only I understood wasn't going to scale past me. We ended up with more than 50 enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity, a Canadian company valued north of a billion dollars, acquired us in 2021. Looking back, I'd have invested in observability much earlier, we bolted on proper APM too late, and I underestimated how expensive it would be to retrofit multi-tenancy after the fact. If I did it again, I'd design for that from day zero instead of treating it as a later problem.

## Making a hard architecture call under pressure

At Alaya, our monolith started showing real scaling cracks right around the time investors wanted new features, not a rewrite. The team was split between a full microservices rewrite and something more modest, and I needed to make a call that would hold up technically without stalling the roadmap for months. Instead of arguing from opinion, I ran a two-week spike comparing both approaches with actual prototype load tests, then brought the trade-offs to the CTO and investors as numbers rather than a pitch. What I proposed was a strangler fig migration: pull the two highest-traffic modules out first and leave the rest of the monolith alone, with clear criteria for when we'd extract anything further. The first module shipped in six weeks, and performance on our busiest endpoints improved by around 40%. The rest of the system stayed stable all the way through the acquisition, no big-bang rewrite needed. The hardest part honestly wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the disciplined choice. Data made that argument for me, and next time I'd front-load even more benchmarking so that debate doesn't eat weeks before it even starts.

## Driving AI-assisted engineering adoption without a mandate

At Benevity I was a senior staff IC with no direct reports, but I wanted two teams that didn't report to me to actually change how they worked day to day by adding AI tooling into their workflow. Mandating that from the top wasn't an option and probably wouldn't have stuck anyway, so I started small, with three curious engineers willing to try it first. I built internal demos that showed concrete time saved, ran informal lunch and learn sessions with no official backing behind them, just working examples, and wrote reusable prompt templates that I committed to a shared repo so people didn't have to start from scratch. One engineer told me a template had saved him roughly two hours on a single PR review cycle, and stories like that spread faster than any announcement would have. Within about three months, something like 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted code review and generation regularly. I ended up as the informal go-to person for AI tooling across the org, without ever holding a formal title for it. It was slower than a top-down rollout would have been, but it stuck, because people who adopt something on their own terms end up defending it to their peers, which isn't something you can order into existence.

## Integrating into a much larger org after acquisition

Right after Benevity acquired Alaya in 2021, I landed on a team about ten times the size of what I was used to: different codebase in Java and microservices, different culture, different process for basically everything. My job was to fold Alaya's capabilities into Benevity's platform while still shipping new features, without losing the speed we'd built as a five-person startup. I spent the first month just mapping their existing architecture before proposing a single change, and I made a point of building relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing any technical opinion. From there I helped drive AI-assisted workflows across the org and became a cross-team architecture liaison for the Europe-facing product surface. We got the core Alaya features migrated, I contributed to three major cross-team architecture decisions, and became a reference point for AI tooling adoption on two teams. My one regret is that early on I tried to fix things that genuinely weren't broken in the new org, just because they looked different from what I was used to. Slowing down and listening first, before making calls, is what actually made me effective there.
