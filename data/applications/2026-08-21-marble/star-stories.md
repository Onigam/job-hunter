# STAR Stories: Marble, Co-Founder / CTO, Physical AI for Process Industries

A note before these: my depth is platform engineering, distributed systems, and AI-augmented workflows, not physics-informed ML, industrial control systems, or OPC UA/SCADA specifically. These stories are picked to speak to what actually transfers into a cofounder role: building something from nothing, making defensible calls under real pressure, ramping into an unfamiliar domain fast, and getting people to change how they work without a mandate to force it.

## Story 1: Building Alaya from nothing

In 2016 I cofounded Alaya with literally a blank page: no customers, no product, no infrastructure. My job was to architect and build the whole platform, alone at first and then with a small team, while the business side was already out signing enterprise clients who expected something to exist. I picked a deliberately boring stack, Node.js, React, PostgreSQL, because the goal was a first demo in three months, not something clever. From there we layered in multi-tenancy, an API layer, and reporting as real usage demanded it, and I hired and onboarded the first three engineers along with the CI/CD and review practices we'd need once it wasn't just me anymore. Five years later the platform had over fifty enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity, a Canadian company worth over a billion dollars, acquired it in 2021. Looking back, I underestimated how expensive it would be to retrofit multi-tenancy after the fact; starting a company again from zero, I'd design for it on day one instead of bolting it on later, which is exactly the kind of architectural call a process-industry platform needs to get right early too.

## Story 2: The monolith vs. rewrite call under investor pressure

At Alaya our monolith started buckling under load, and the team split into two camps: rip it out for microservices, or keep patching what we had. Investors wanted new features, not a quarter spent on plumbing, so I couldn't just pick a side and hope it worked out. I ran a two-week spike, building small prototypes of both approaches and load-testing them against real traffic instead of arguing from opinion. What came out of it was a middle path, a strangler fig migration extracting only the two highest-traffic modules and leaving the rest alone until it actually needed attention. We shipped the first extraction in six weeks with about a 40% latency improvement on the busiest endpoints, and the rest of the system stayed stable through the acquisition. The hardest part wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the right call, and having load-test numbers instead of opinions is what closed that argument. Physics-informed models will bring the same kind of pressure to ship fast and skip the validation step, and I'd run the same playbook.

## Story 3: Ramping into a completely different system after the acquisition

After Benevity acquired Alaya in 2021, I joined a team roughly ten times the size of what I'd worked in, running Java-based microservices instead of the Node stack I knew, with processes that had reasons behind them I hadn't seen yet. My first instinct was to start fixing things that looked wrong from the outside, and that instinct was mostly wrong. I spent the first month just mapping the existing architecture and talking to the platform and infra teams before proposing anything, which felt slow at the time. That patience paid off: I ended up contributing to three major cross-team architecture decisions and became a liaison for the Europe-facing product surface, which wouldn't have happened if I'd shown up with opinions in week one. Sensor data, plant operators, and industrial safety constraints are a different domain than anything I've touched, but the approach is the same one that worked here, map how the system actually behaves before changing it.

## Story 4: Earning adoption without a mandate

At Benevity I was a senior staff engineer with no direct reports, and I wanted two teams that didn't report to me to actually adopt AI-assisted development practices. A top-down mandate wasn't on the table, so I started small: three curious engineers, a few internal demos showing real time saved on things like PR review, and informal lunch sessions with no attendance requirement. I wrote up the wins as they happened, things like a two-hour reduction on a specific review cycle, and put reusable prompt templates into a shared repo anyone could pull from. Within three months about 70% of engineers on both teams were using the workflow, and I'd become the person people asked when they wanted to get started. As a cofounder I'd be building both a technical platform and an engineering culture from scratch, and this is the closest thing I've done to that: getting people to change how they work because it's clearly better, not because someone told them to.
