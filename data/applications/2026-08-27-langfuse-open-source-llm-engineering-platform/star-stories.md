# STAR Stories, Langfuse (Backend / Product Engineer)

Picked for likely themes at Langfuse right now: building from zero, technical trade-offs around scale and observability, integrating after an acquisition, and driving adoption without formal authority. All four map fairly directly onto a small, fast-moving engineering org that just joined a bigger company.

## Building Alaya from zero

In 2016 I co-founded Alaya, a purpose and impact platform for employee engagement. There was no product yet, no customers, just a blank page and a founding team betting on an idea. My job was to architect and build the whole thing, alone at first and then with a small team, while the business side was already out signing its first enterprise clients, so there wasn't much room to wait for a clean design before shipping something real. I picked a pragmatic stack, Node.js, React, Postgres, that could get us to a first demo fast without locking us into something we'd regret later, and had the core features working within three months. From there I kept expanding it: multi-tenant architecture, an API layer, impact reporting, postal campaign tooling, and along the way I hired and onboarded our first three engineers and put in place code review and basic architecture docs. The platform ended up running production for over fifty enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity, a Canadian company valued north of a billion dollars, acquired us in 2021. Looking back, I would have invested in observability much earlier than I did; we bolted on proper monitoring late, and retrofitting multi-tenancy cost more than it should have because I didn't design for it from day zero.

## A hard architecture call under investor pressure

A couple of years into Alaya, our monolith started showing real scaling cracks, and the team split into two camps: rewrite everything as microservices, or keep it as a modular monolith. Investors, meanwhile, wanted new features, not a multi-month rewrite with nothing to show for it. I needed a decision I could actually defend, not just an opinion. So I ran a two-week technical spike comparing both approaches with real prototype load tests, then took the results to the CTO and investors as numbers rather than as a pitch. What I proposed was a strangler-fig migration: pull out the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest of the monolith alone, and set clear criteria for when we'd extract anything else. The first module shipped in six weeks, latency on the busiest endpoints improved by around 40%, and the rest of the monolith stayed stable straight through the acquisition, no big-bang rewrite required. The hard part honestly wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the right call. Data did most of that convincing for me. If I did it again I'd push for the early benchmarking sooner, just to cut the debate down.

## Integrating after an acquisition

When Benevity acquired Alaya in 2021, I landed on a team roughly ten times the size of what I'd worked with before, with a different codebase in Java and microservices, different culture, different everything. The task was to fold Alaya's capabilities into their platform while still shipping new features, without losing the pace we'd built up as a small team. I spent the first month just mapping their existing architecture before I proposed a single change, and made a point of building relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing any technical opinion on them. That patience paid off: I ended up contributing to three major cross-team architecture decisions and became a kind of architecture liaison for the Europe-facing side of the product. I also started driving adoption of AI-assisted workflows across the org during this period, which turned into its own thing. My honest reflection is that I moved too fast early on trying to fix things that weren't actually broken in the new org, just different from what I was used to. Slowing down and listening first is what actually made me effective there, and it's a lesson that would transfer directly to joining a team at Langfuse right after the ClickHouse deal.

## Driving adoption without formal authority

At Benevity I was a senior IC with no direct reports, but I wanted to get two teams that didn't report to me to actually adopt AI-assisted development practices, not just nod along in a meeting. Mandating it wasn't an option and probably wouldn't have worked anyway, so I started small: three curious engineers, some internal demos that showed concrete time saved rather than a slide about the future of AI, and informal lunch sessions with no agenda beyond "here's something useful." I built out reusable prompt templates and committed them to a shared repo so people could just take them rather than start from scratch, and wrote up results like a two-hour PR review cycle that got cut down to something much shorter. Within about three months, something like 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted review or generation day to day. I ended up as the informal go-to person for AI tooling across the org, without ever having the title for it. The approach was slower than a top-down decree would have been, but it stuck, because people who chose it themselves ended up advocating for it to others in a way I couldn't have manufactured.
