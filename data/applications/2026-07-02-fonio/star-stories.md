## Building Alaya from a blank page to an acquisition

In 2016 I co-founded Alaya, a purpose and impact platform for employee engagement, and for the first year I was the only engineer on it. There was no product, no customers yet, just a business that was starting to sign its first enterprise clients while I was still deciding what the platform should even look like. My job was to architect and build the whole thing from zero and keep shipping fast enough that sales could keep making promises we could actually deliver on. I picked a pragmatic stack, Node.js, React, and PostgreSQL, that wouldn't force a rewrite in a year, and got a working demo out in the first three months. From there I expanded the platform module by module: multi-tenant architecture, an API layer, impact reporting, postal campaigns, and once the team grew I hired the first three engineers and set up CI/CD and a review culture so I wasn't the only person who understood how anything worked. Five years later the platform was running production traffic for over 50 enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity, a Canadian unicorn valued north of a billion dollars, acquired the company. If I did it again I'd invest in observability much earlier since we only added proper APM once things were already big, and I'd design the multi-tenancy model from day one instead of retrofitting it later, because that retrofit cost us more than the feature itself.

## A hard architecture call with investors pushing the other way

At Alaya, our monolith started showing real scaling cracks around the same time investors were pushing hard for new features, not for us to disappear into a rewrite. The team was split between a full microservices rewrite and a more modest modular monolith, and I needed a defensible answer, not a gut call, because whatever I picked would eat months of runway either way. I ran a two week technical spike with prototype load tests on both approaches and brought the CTO and investors real numbers instead of an opinion. Rather than pitching an all-or-nothing rewrite, I proposed a strangler fig migration: pull out the two highest-traffic modules first and leave the rest of the monolith alone until we had a concrete reason to touch it. The first extracted module shipped in six weeks and performance on our busiest endpoints improved by roughly 40%, and the rest of the monolith stayed stable all the way through the acquisition, so we never needed the big rewrite everyone was afraid of. What stuck with me is that data shortened the argument more than any amount of talking would have, and next time I'd run that kind of benchmark earlier just to cut the debate down before it starts.

## Getting engineers to change how they worked without being their manager

At Benevity I was a Senior Staff IC with no direct reports, but I wanted two teams that didn't report to me to actually adopt AI-assisted development instead of treating it as a novelty. Ordering people to use new tooling was never an option I had, so the task was closer to persuasion than management: prove it was worth their time, on their terms. I started small, with three engineers who were already curious, built internal demos that showed real time saved rather than a slide about potential, and ran informal lunch sessions with no mandate attached, just people showing other people what worked. I put together reusable prompt templates and committed them to a shared repo so the good habits didn't live only in my head, and I wrote up concrete results, like a specific PR review that dropped from a full afternoon to about two hours. Within three months, something like 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted review and generation daily, and I ended up the informal go-to person for AI tooling across the org without ever having the title for it. The slower, no-mandate approach turned out to be the sticky one, since people who opt in on their own terms end up defending the change to their peers in a way a top-down decree never gets you.

## Slowing down before pushing change into someone else's codebase

After Benevity acquired Alaya in 2021, I landed on a team roughly ten times the size of what I'd worked in, with a different culture, a Java and microservices codebase I didn't know, and processes that had nothing to do with how we'd operated before. The obvious trap was showing up with opinions about what was "wrong" and trying to fix it fast, and for the first couple of weeks I nearly fell into exactly that. Instead I spent the first month just mapping the existing architecture and building relationships with the platform and infra teams before I proposed anything, which felt slow at the time but meant people actually listened when I did speak up. From there I helped migrate Alaya's core capabilities into Benevity's platform, contributed to three major cross-team architecture decisions, and became the informal architecture liaison for the Europe-facing product surface. Looking back, the version of me that wanted to move fast in month one would have burned trust I needed for the next two years, and the lesson generalizes well beyond that acquisition: listen before you touch anything you didn't build.
