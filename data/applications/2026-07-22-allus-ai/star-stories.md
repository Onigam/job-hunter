# STAR Stories: Allus AI, Founding AI Agent Engineer

## 1. Building Alaya from zero to acquisition

Best for: "Tell me about building something from scratch," founding engineer questions, how I handle ambiguity when there's no playbook.

In 2016 I co-founded Alaya, a purpose and impact platform for employee engagement. There was no product yet, no customers, just a blank page and a business that was already talking to its first enterprise prospects. My job was to architect and build the whole platform, alone at first and then with a small team, while sales kept promising things engineering hadn't built. I picked Node.js, React, and PostgreSQL because they let me move fast without locking myself into infrastructure I didn't need yet, and I pushed to get a working demo in front of the first client within three months. From there it was constant iteration: multi-tenant architecture, an API layer, impact reporting, postal campaigns for clients who wanted physical rewards alongside digital ones. Once the team grew past me, I hired the first three engineers and put in place CI/CD, code review norms, and lightweight architecture docs so decisions didn't live only in my head. The platform ended up serving 50+ enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity, a Canadian company valued north of a billion dollars, acquired us in 2021. Looking back, I would have invested in observability much earlier, we bolted on proper monitoring later than we should have, and I underestimated how expensive it would be to retrofit multi-tenancy instead of designing for it from day one. That's a mistake I don't plan to repeat at a company building agent infrastructure from scratch.

## 2. Driving AI-assisted engineering adoption without a mandate

Best for: agent tooling questions, influence without authority, how I get engineers to actually use new AI workflows.

At Benevity I was a senior staff IC with no direct reports, but I wanted two teams that didn't report to me to start using AI-assisted development in a real way, not just as a novelty someone tried once. Rather than push a top-down policy, I started with three engineers who were already curious and built small internal demos that showed concrete time saved on things like PR review and boilerplate generation. I ran informal lunch sessions, no mandate attached, just showing what worked, and put together prompt templates that I committed to a shared repo so people could take them and adapt them rather than starting from nothing. I also wrote up results as they came in, one story was a two-hour PR review cycle that dropped to twenty minutes once the reviewer had a good template to start from. Within three months, about 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted review and generation regularly, and I'd become the person people asked when they wanted to get started with agent tooling. The slower, opt-in approach took longer than a top-down rollout would have, but the engineers who adopted it became its advocates internally, which is something a decree from above never would have produced. That's the same pattern I'd want to build into evaluation and feedback tooling for a team like Allus, where the people using the system daily need to trust it before it becomes load-bearing.

## 3. A hard architecture call under investor pressure

Best for: technical trade-off questions, production reliability, how I weigh rewrite risk against delivery pressure.

At Alaya our monolith started showing real scaling cracks, and the team was split between a full microservices rewrite and a more modest modular monolith approach, right as investors were pushing hard for new features instead of infrastructure work. I ran a two-week technical spike comparing both paths with actual prototype load tests rather than opinions, then took the results to the CTO and investors with numbers instead of a pitch. What we landed on was a strangler fig migration: pull out the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest of the monolith alone, and define clear criteria for when the next module would qualify for extraction. The first extracted module shipped in six weeks and performance on the busiest endpoints improved by roughly 40%, and the remaining monolith stayed stable all the way through the acquisition, so we never needed the big-bang rewrite everyone was afraid of. The hardest part honestly wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the disciplined choice rather than the timid one. If I did it again I'd push for more early benchmarking data up front, since that's what actually cut the internal debate short, not the argument itself.

## 4. Integrating into a much larger org after acquisition

Best for: navigating change, cross-functional collaboration, working inside someone else's system without breaking it.

After Benevity acquired Alaya in 2021, I joined a team roughly ten times the size of what I'd been running, with a different codebase in Java and microservices, different processes, and a culture I hadn't shaped. My task was to get Alaya's capabilities integrated into Benevity's platform while still shipping new features, without losing the velocity that had gotten us acquired in the first place. I spent the first month mostly listening, mapping out the existing architecture before I proposed any changes, and built relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing technical opinions their way. That groundwork let me become a cross-team architecture liaison for the Europe-facing product surface and contribute to three major cross-team architecture decisions, on top of driving the AI tooling adoption that became story two. I moved too fast early on, trying to fix things in the new org that weren't actually broken, and slowing down to listen first is what made the rest of the integration work. For a founding role where I'd be the one setting the early patterns, that same instinct, understand before you change something, is what keeps a small team's infrastructure from becoming something the next hire has to unwind.
