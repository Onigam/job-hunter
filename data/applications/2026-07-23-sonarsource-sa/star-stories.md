# STAR Stories: Senior Software Engineer (Java) - Remediation Agent, SonarSource

## 1. Moving a research prototype into a production Java codebase (Benevity integration)

When Benevity acquired Alaya in 2021, I went from a codebase I'd built from scratch to one that was Java, microservices, and about ten times larger, with conventions I hadn't set and didn't fully understand yet. My task was to integrate Alaya's capabilities into that platform without losing the delivery speed we'd had as a small team, and without breaking things I didn't yet know the shape of. I spent the first month reading, not shipping: mapping the existing services, sitting in on architecture reviews, figuring out where the sharp edges were before I touched anything. Once I had that picture, I built relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing my own opinions, and I ended up becoming the architecture liaison for the Europe-facing product surface. We migrated the core Alaya features in, and I contributed to three cross-team architecture decisions in the process. Looking back, I moved too fast early on trying to fix things that weren't actually broken in the new org, just unfamiliar. Slowing down and listening first is the lesson I carry into any new codebase now, and it's exactly the instinct I'd bring to inheriting research prototypes and turning them into services other teams depend on.

## 2. A technical trade-off under real delivery pressure (strangler fig migration)

At Alaya, our monolith was starting to show real scaling cracks, and the team was split between a full microservices rewrite and something more conservative, while investors wanted new features, not a quarter spent on infrastructure. I needed a decision I could defend with data, not opinion, so I ran a two-week spike comparing both approaches with actual load tests rather than architecture diagrams. I took those numbers to the CTO and investors and proposed a strangler fig migration instead of a rewrite: extract the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest monolithic, and define clear criteria for when to pull out the next piece. We shipped the first extracted module in six weeks, performance on the busiest endpoints improved by roughly 40%, and the remaining monolith stayed stable straight through the acquisition. The hard part wasn't the technical design, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the right call; the load test numbers did most of that work for me. If I did it again, I'd push for that benchmarking even earlier, since it's what actually shortened the debate. That same instinct, measure before you argue, is how I'd want to evaluate something like routing logic between QuickFixes and LLM-based fixes: watch what the data says before committing to an architecture.

## 3. Driving adoption without formal authority (AI tooling rollout)

At Benevity I was a senior IC with no direct reports, but I wanted two engineering teams that didn't report to me to actually change how they worked and bring AI tooling into their daily workflow. Rather than pushing a mandate, I started with three curious engineers, built small internal demos that showed concrete time saved, and ran informal lunch sessions with no agenda beyond "here's what this looks like in practice." I wrote up reusable prompt templates and committed them to a shared repo so people could take them without asking me first, and I documented specific wins, like the time we shaved two hours off a single PR review cycle. Within three months, about 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted review and generation, and I became the informal go-to person for it across the org. It was slower than a top-down rollout would have been, but it stuck, because people adopted it on their own terms and then advocated for it themselves. That's the same approach I'd use to mentor through design reviews on something like a remediation engine: show, don't mandate, and let the engineers who get it become the ones who spread it.

## 4. Building something from a blank page under real ambiguity (Alaya, 2016)

In 2016 I cofounded Alaya with nothing: no customers, no product, no team yet. My job was to architect and build the whole platform, alone at first, while the business was closing its first enterprise clients on the promise that the product would exist. I picked a pragmatic stack that could scale without over-engineering for a scale we didn't have yet, got the core features to a working demo within three months, and then expanded iteratively into multi-tenant architecture, an API layer, and reporting as real usage told us what mattered. As the team grew, I hired and onboarded the first three engineers and put in CI/CD and a code review culture before we needed it, not after. We reached production with more than 50 enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity's acquisition in 2021 was, in a fairly direct way, a validation of the calls made in that first year. The thing I'd change is observability: we bolted on proper APM late, and I underestimated how expensive retrofitting multi-tenancy would be, so now I design for both from day zero. That's the same bias I'd bring to sandboxed execution for untrusted AI output, build the safety and observability boundary in from the start, not after the first incident forces it.
