# STAR Stories: Lead ML/AI Platform Engineer @ SavvyMoney

Prepared for behavioral rounds. Four stories, picked for technical leadership, AI tooling adoption without formal authority, architecture trade-offs under pressure, and cross-team integration in a larger, more regulated org, all things this role will ask about directly.

---

## 1. Setting technical direction from a blank page (founding engineer, Alaya)

In 2016 I co-founded Alaya, an employee purpose and impact platform, and there was nothing to build on yet: no customers, no product, no team. I had to architect and build the whole thing myself first, then grow a team around it while the business was closing its first enterprise clients, so the pressure to ship and the pressure to build something that wouldn't fall over a year later were both real from day one. I picked a pragmatic stack, Node.js, React, PostgreSQL, that could scale without over-engineering for problems we didn't have yet, and got the core features to a working demo in about three months. From there I kept expanding the platform iteratively: multi-tenant architecture, an API layer, impact reporting, postal campaigns, and once we had traction I hired and onboarded the first three engineers and set up CI/CD, code review norms, and architecture docs so the decisions in my head became decisions the team could actually work from. The platform reached production with 50+ enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity, a Canadian unicorn valued north of a billion dollars, acquired the company in 2021, which told me the early technical calls had held up under real growth. If I did it again I'd invest in observability much earlier, we bolted on proper APM late, and I'd design for multi-tenancy from day zero instead of retrofitting it, since that retrofit cost us more than it should have.

## 2. Driving AI tooling adoption across teams that didn't report to me (Benevity)

At Benevity I was a Senior Staff IC with no direct reports, and I wanted two teams that had no obligation to listen to me to actually change how they worked by adding AI-assisted development into their daily flow. Mandating it wasn't an option, so the task became: change behavior through influence, not authority. I started small, three engineers who were genuinely curious, and built internal demos that showed concrete time saved rather than talking about the idea in the abstract. From there I ran informal lunch-and-learn sessions with no mandate attached, just demos and questions, and built a shared repo of reusable prompt templates so the value compounded instead of living in individual heads. I also documented specific wins, like a case where AI-assisted review cut a couple of hours off a PR cycle, because a concrete story travels better than a slide. Within three months, about 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted review and generation, and I ended up as the informal AI tooling lead for the org without anyone appointing me to that. It was slower than a top-down rollout would have been, but it stuck, because people who adopt something on their own terms end up defending it to their peers in a way a mandate never produces. That's the same pattern I'd want to run for GenAI and agentic tooling adoption here, since a regulated fintech is exactly the kind of place where a heavy-handed rollout backfires.

## 3. Choosing a strangler-fig migration over a full rewrite under investor pressure (Alaya)

Alaya's monolith started showing real scaling cracks, and the team was split between a full microservices rewrite and a more modular approach, while investors were pushing hard for new features, not a multi-month rewrite with no visible output. I needed a technical decision I could actually defend with data, not just an opinion I happened to hold more strongly than everyone else's. I ran a two-week spike comparing both approaches with prototype load tests, then took the trade-offs to the CTO and investors as numbers, not as a pitch. Out of that I proposed and led a strangler-fig migration: extract the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest of the monolith alone, and define clear criteria for when the next module would be worth extracting. We shipped the first extracted module in six weeks, performance on the busiest endpoints improved by roughly 40%, and the remaining monolith stayed stable all the way through the acquisition, so we never needed the big-bang rewrite people were originally arguing for. The hardest part honestly wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the disciplined choice rather than the timid one, and having the benchmark data ready up front would have cut that debate shorter than it ended up being.

## 4. Integrating into a much larger, more constrained org after an acquisition (Benevity)

After Benevity acquired Alaya in 2021 I joined a team roughly ten times the size of what I'd been working in, with a different culture, a Java-based microservices codebase instead of the one I'd built, and processes that existed for reasons I didn't yet understand. My task was to get Alaya's capabilities integrated into Benevity's platform while still shipping new features, without losing the product velocity that had gotten us acquired in the first place. I spent the first month mapping the existing architecture before proposing a single change, and I deliberately built relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing any technical opinions on them, since I'd seen what happens when a founder-turned-employee shows up assuming their old playbook still applies. From there I became a cross-team architecture liaison for the Europe-facing product surface and contributed to three major cross-team architecture decisions, on top of continuing to push AI-augmented workflow adoption in parallel. The Alaya features made it into Benevity's platform successfully, and the credibility I built by listening first is what let the later technical opinions land. Early on I did try to "fix" a few things in the new org that weren't actually broken, they just looked unfamiliar, and slowing down to understand why something existed before changing it made me noticeably more effective for the rest of the integration.
