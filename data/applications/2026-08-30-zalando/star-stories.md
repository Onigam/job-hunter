# STAR Stories: Zalando, Principal Software Engineer (Zalando Payments)

Selected for: agent-augmented delivery, principal-level technical leadership, cross-department technical liaison, mentoring, production readiness.

---

## 1. Driving AI-tooling adoption across two teams without a mandate

This is probably the closest match to what they're calling "agent-augmented delivery." At Benevity I was a Senior Staff IC with no direct reports, and I wanted two engineering teams that didn't report to me to actually start using AI-assisted development instead of treating it as a novelty. I knew a top-down decree would get resistance, so I started small: three engineers who were already curious, and I built internal demos that showed concrete time saved on real PRs rather than talking in the abstract. We ran informal lunch sessions, no mandate attached, and I put together a shared repo of prompt templates that people could actually reuse instead of reinventing every time. Within three months, about 70% of engineers across both teams had it in their regular workflow, and I'd become the person people went to when they wanted to get this stuff working properly. Looking back, the slower, opt-in approach is what made it stick. If I'd pushed it top-down it probably would have been abandoned the moment I stopped watching. That's roughly the playbook I'd bring to standing up agent-augmented workflows at Zalando Payments, minus the assumption that people need to be told to use it.

## 2. Building Alaya from a blank page to acquisition

In 2016 I co-founded Alaya, an employee purpose and impact platform, and there was nothing to build on: no customers, no product, no team. My job was to architect the whole thing while the business was closing its first enterprise deals, so there wasn't room for a long runway before shipping. I picked a stack that could scale without over-engineering for problems we didn't have yet, Node.js, React, PostgreSQL, and got the core features to a working demo in about three months. From there it was iterative: multi-tenant architecture, an API layer, impact reporting, and eventually hiring the first three engineers and setting up the CI/CD and review culture that let the team grow past just me. We got to 50+ enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity, a Canadian unicorn valued north of a billion dollars, acquired us in 2021. If I'm honest, the thing I'd change is investing in observability much earlier; we bolted on proper APM late and paid for it in debugging time we didn't have to lose.

## 3. A hard architecture call under investor pressure

By year three at Alaya, our monolith was starting to crack under load, and the team was split between a full microservices rewrite and just patching things as they broke. Investors wanted new features, not a rewrite, so I needed a decision I could actually defend with data rather than opinion. I ran a two-week spike comparing both approaches with real load tests, then presented the trade-offs to the CTO and investors in numbers instead of arguments. What we landed on was a strangler-fig migration: pull out the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest of the monolith alone, and set clear criteria for when to extract more. The first module shipped in six weeks and performance on our busiest endpoints improved by around 40%. The rest of the monolith stayed stable straight through the acquisition, no big-bang rewrite required. The hard part wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the disciplined choice. I'd do more upfront benchmarking next time just to shorten that debate.

## 4. Acting as the technical bridge after the acquisition

When Benevity acquired Alaya in 2021, I went from a five-person team to an engineering org roughly ten times the size, with a different codebase (Java-based microservices instead of what we'd built), different processes, different unwritten rules. My task was to get Alaya's capabilities integrated into their platform while keeping up the delivery speed we were used to, without steamrolling a culture I didn't understand yet. I spent the first month just mapping their existing architecture before I proposed anything, and made a point of building relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing technical opinions on them. That earned me enough trust to become a de facto architecture liaison for the Europe-facing product surface, and I ended up contributing to three major cross-team architecture decisions in that first year. My early instinct was to move fast and fix things that, in hindsight, weren't actually broken in the new org; slowing down and listening first is what made the integration work.
