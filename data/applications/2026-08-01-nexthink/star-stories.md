# STAR Stories: Nexthink, Staff Software Engineer (AI-native automation)

Picked for this role because Flow is a greenfield rebuild toward AI-agent orchestration at large scale, and these four cover building something from nothing, making a hard distributed-systems call under pressure, working inside a much bigger Java codebase, and driving AI tooling adoption without formal authority.

---

## 1. Building Alaya from zero to acquisition

I co-founded Alaya in 2016 as a purpose and impact platform for employee engagement, and when I say we started from nothing, I mean an actual blank page: no customers yet, no product, no team beyond me and my co-founder. My job was to architect and build the whole platform while the business was closing its first enterprise clients, so there wasn't room for a slow ramp. I picked a pragmatic stack, Node.js, React, PostgreSQL, that could scale without turning into an over-engineered mess before we even had users, and I pushed to get a working demo in front of prospects within three months. From there it was iterative: multi-tenant architecture, an API layer, impact reporting, postal campaign tooling, and along the way I hired and onboarded our first three engineers and set up CI/CD and a real code review culture. We ended up with over fifty enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity, a Canadian company valued north of a billion dollars, acquired us in 2021. Looking back, I'd invest in observability much earlier than we did; our APM setup came late and cost us some painful debugging sessions. I also underestimated how expensive it would be to retrofit multi-tenancy after the fact. If I did it again, I'd design for that isolation from day zero rather than treating it as a phase-two problem.

## 2. A hard architecture call under investor pressure

At Alaya, our monolith started showing real scaling cracks right around the time investors were pushing hard for new features, not a rewrite. The team was split between a full microservices rewrite and something more conservative, and I needed to make a call that wouldn't blow up either the roadmap or the system. So I ran a two-week spike comparing both approaches with actual prototype load tests instead of arguing from opinion, then took the results to the CTO and investors with numbers they could poke holes in. What I proposed was a strangler-fig migration: pull out the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest of the monolith alone, and define clear criteria for when the next module would get extracted. We shipped the first extracted piece in about six weeks, and performance on the busiest endpoints improved roughly 40 percent. The rest of the monolith stayed stable all the way through the acquisition, so we never needed the big-bang rewrite people were originally arguing for. The hard part honestly wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the disciplined choice. Having real benchmarks in hand cut that debate short, and next time I'd push for that kind of early data even sooner, before the debate has a chance to calcify into camps.

## 3. Landing in a Java codebase ten times the size

When Benevity acquired Alaya in 2021, I joined as Senior Staff Engineer on a team roughly ten times the size of what I was used to, working in Java microservices instead of the Node.js stack I'd built Alaya on. The task wasn't just learning new code, it was integrating Alaya's capabilities into Benevity's platform while keeping up the product velocity we'd had as a five-person team, inside an org with very different culture and process. I spent the first month mostly reading and mapping the existing architecture before I proposed anything, and I made a point of building relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing technical opinions their way. From there I became a cross-team architecture liaison for the Europe-facing product surface and helped drive three major cross-team architecture decisions, while also pushing AI-assisted engineering practices, things like GitHub Copilot workflows and reusable prompt patterns, across the wider org. The migration of core Alaya features into Benevity's platform landed successfully, and I became the go-to person for AI tooling questions across two teams. My honest reflection is that I moved too fast early on, trying to fix things in the new codebase that weren't actually broken, just unfamiliar. Slowing down and listening first is what made the rest of it work, and it's a lesson I'd apply immediately walking into a Java-heavy, larger-scale environment like this one.

## 4. Driving AI tooling adoption with no direct reports

At Benevity I was a Senior Staff IC with no direct reports, but I wanted to change how two teams that didn't report to me actually worked day to day by getting AI tooling into their normal workflow. Mandating it wasn't an option and probably wouldn't have stuck anyway, so I started small: three engineers who were already curious became my first adopters. I built internal demos that showed concrete time savings rather than abstract pitches, ran informal lunch-and-learn sessions with zero mandate attached, and put together reusable prompt templates in a shared repo so people could just grab something that worked. I also documented specific wins, one story I kept coming back to was a two-hour reduction on a single PR review cycle, because a concrete number travels further than a general claim. Within three months, about 70 percent of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted code review and generation regularly, and I ended up as the informal AI tooling lead for the org without ever having the title. It was slower than a top-down rollout would have been, but it stuck, because people who adopt something on their own terms end up advocating for it themselves. That's the same instinct I'd bring to a role built around getting AI agents to actually orchestrate real work: earn trust in the workflow before asking anyone to hand over control.
