# STAR Stories: Zero21 (Superpower), Founding Engineer Full Stack

Picked for a founding-engineer-at-an-early-stage-health-startup interview: building from nothing, making architecture calls that hold up under growth, working without a safety net of senior engineers around you, and adapting to a much bigger org later. Superpower is post-Series A with a small team, so the ground-zero and ambiguity stories carry the most weight here.

## Building Alaya from zero to acquisition

In 2016 I co-founded Alaya, an employee purpose and impact platform, with a blank page and no customers yet. My job was to architect and build the entire thing myself at first, then grow a team, while the business was out signing its first enterprise clients, so there wasn't room for a six-month runway before shipping something real. I picked Node.js, React, and PostgreSQL specifically because I could move fast on it without over-engineering for scale I didn't have yet, and I had the first demoable version ready in about three months. From there I expanded iteratively: multi-tenant architecture, an API layer that outside integrators could actually build against, impact reporting, and eventually hired and onboarded the first three engineers, setting up CI/CD and a review culture along the way. The platform ended up serving fifty-plus enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity, a Canadian unicorn valued north of a billion dollars, acquired the company in 2021, which felt like the clearest possible validation of the early technical bets. If I did it again I'd invest in observability much earlier, since we didn't have proper APM until things were already big enough that debugging without it hurt, and I'd design for multi-tenancy from day zero instead of retrofitting it once the second enterprise client showed up.

## A hard architecture call under investor pressure

At Alaya we had a monolith that was starting to crack under load, and the team was split between a full microservices rewrite and something more modest, right as investors were pushing hard for new features instead of infrastructure work. I needed a decision that held up technically without stalling the roadmap, so I ran a two-week spike comparing both approaches with actual load tests instead of arguing from opinion, then brought the CTO and investors real numbers. What I proposed was a strangler-fig migration: pull out the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest of the monolith alone, and define clear criteria for when the next module would earn the same treatment. The first extracted module shipped in six weeks and cut latency on the busiest endpoints by around 40%, and the remaining monolith stayed stable all the way through the acquisition, so we never needed the big-bang rewrite everyone was afraid of. The hard part wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the disciplined choice; next time I'd start the benchmarking earlier so that argument doesn't eat as much calendar time.

## Landing in a much bigger org after the acquisition

When Benevity acquired Alaya in 2021, I went from a five-person team to an engineering org ten times the size, with a different language, different processes, and a codebase I hadn't written a line of. The task wasn't just technical integration, it was figuring out how to keep shipping at the pace we had before without torching relationships with teams who'd never worked with an acquired founder. I spent the first month mapping their existing architecture before I proposed anything, then built relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing any opinions on how things should change. Once there was trust, I drove adoption of AI-assisted development workflows across two teams and became the informal architecture liaison for the Europe-facing side of the product. Alaya's core capabilities made it into Benevity's platform, and I ended up contributing to three cross-team architecture decisions that outlived the integration project itself. I moved too fast early on trying to fix things that weren't actually broken in the new org; slowing down and listening first is what actually made the second half of that year work.

## Getting a team to adopt AI tooling without any authority to mandate it

At Benevity I was a senior IC with no direct reports, but I wanted two teams that didn't report to me to start using AI-assisted development seriously, at a point when most people were still skeptical it would hold up on real production code. I couldn't order anyone to change how they worked, so the task was closer to a sales problem: get enough people to try it that the results spoke for themselves. I started with three curious engineers, built small internal demos that showed concrete time saved on real tasks, and ran informal lunch sessions with no mandate attached, just a working example each time. I also committed a set of reusable prompt templates to a shared repo so the win wasn't locked in my head, and wrote up specific results, like a PR review cycle that dropped from most of a day to about two hours. Within three months, around 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted review or generation regularly, and I became the person people asked when they wanted to get started. It was slower than a top-down rollout would have been, but the adoption stuck because people chose it themselves, which is the same instinct I'd bring to introducing new tooling on a founding team where nobody wants to be told how to work.
