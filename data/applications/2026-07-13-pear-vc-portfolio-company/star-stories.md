# STAR+R Stories, Founding Engineer, Kato (Pear VC)

Prepared for a founding engineer interview loop where the panel will care most about: can this person own ambiguous technical scope alone, make defensible architecture calls under pressure, and operate without a big team backing them up.

## 1. Building Alaya from a blank page

This is the one I'd lead with for a founding engineer conversation, because it's the closest thing I have to what Kato needs from its first hire. In 2016 I co-founded Alaya, a purpose and impact platform for employee engagement, and we started with nothing: no product, no customers, just a pitch deck and a signed first client waiting on something to actually use. My job was to architect and build the whole platform myself before we could hire anyone. I picked Node.js, React and PostgreSQL specifically because they'd let me move fast without boxing us into a corner later, built the core features in about three months to get to a first demo, and then kept expanding into multi-tenancy, an API layer and reporting as the client base grew. Once we had traction I hired and onboarded our first three engineers and put in the CI/CD and code review habits that let the codebase survive more than one person touching it. The platform ended up serving 50-plus enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity, a Canadian unicorn, acquired us in 2021, which is about as clear a signal as you get that the early technical bets were sound. If I did it again I'd invest in observability much earlier. We didn't have real APM until things were already big enough that debugging blind had gotten painful, and I'd also design for multi-tenancy from day one instead of retrofitting it once the second enterprise client showed up.

## 2. Choosing not to rewrite under pressure

At Alaya, our monolith started showing real scaling cracks right around the time investors wanted new features, not a rewrite. The team was split between a full microservices rewrite and something more incremental, and I was the one who had to make a call that would hold up technically and also survive an investor conversation. I ran a two-week spike comparing both approaches with actual load tests instead of arguing from opinion, then presented the trade-offs to the CTO and investors with numbers on the table. What I proposed was a strangler-fig migration: pull out the two highest-traffic modules first and leave the rest of the monolith alone, with clear criteria for when to extract the next piece. We shipped the first extracted module in six weeks and saw about a 40% performance improvement on the busiest endpoints, and the remaining monolith stayed stable all the way through the acquisition, no big-bang rewrite required. The hard part wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that doing less was the right call. Data got us there faster than debate did, and next time I'd push for that early benchmarking sooner just to cut the argument short.

## 3. Leading two teams that didn't report to me

After Benevity acquired us, I was a senior staff engineer with no direct reports, but I wanted two teams that had nothing to do with my org chart to actually change how they worked and start using AI-assisted development. I couldn't mandate anything, so I started small: three engineers who were already curious, a few internal demos that showed real time saved rather than a slide about the future of AI, and informal lunch sessions with no attendance requirement. I built reusable prompt templates, put them in a shared repo, and wrote up concrete results, like the time a PR review cycle got two hours shorter because of how we'd set up the review prompts. Within three months about 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted review and generation on their own, and I ended up as the informal lead for AI tooling across the org without ever having the authority to assign anyone that work. It took longer than a top-down rollout would have, but the people who adopted it kept advocating for it themselves afterward, which a mandate never gets you.

## 4. Mapping the terrain before changing it

When Benevity's acquisition of Alaya closed, I went from a ten-person company to a team ten times that size overnight, with a different codebase, in Java, a different culture, and processes I didn't understand yet. My instinct was to start fixing things I thought looked wrong, and I caught myself doing that in the first couple of weeks before it cost me anything real. I stopped, spent the better part of a month actually mapping their existing architecture, and made a point of building relationships with the platform and infra teams before I pushed a single technical opinion. That patience is what let me become a cross-team architecture liaison for the Europe-facing product surface and contribute to three major architecture decisions that actually stuck, instead of getting written off as the acquired guy with opinions about their Java services. The lesson stuck with me: moving fast to "fix" a system you don't understand yet usually just costs you credibility you'll want later. Slower and listening first turned out to be the faster path.
