# STAR+R Stories: Nexthink, Principal/Staff Software Engineer

Picked for this role because the posting is about setting technical direction on an AI-driven platform meant to serve a huge, already-live customer base, not building a greenfield toy. These five cover ambiguity, technical trade-offs under pressure, cross-team influence without authority, scaling under load, and stakeholder persuasion, which is the mix Staff and Principal loops usually probe for.

## 1. Building Alaya from a blank page to acquisition

When I co-founded Alaya in 2016, there was no product, no customers, and no team yet, just an idea about employee engagement software. I had to architect and build the whole platform myself at first, then bring people in, while the business was already out signing its first enterprise clients, so the ground kept shifting under the design. I picked Node.js, React, and PostgreSQL specifically because they'd let me move fast without locking in a rigid architecture too early, and I got the core features working in about three months so we'd have something real to demo. From there I kept expanding it: multi-tenancy, an API layer, impact reporting, and eventually postal campaign tooling, while hiring and onboarding the first three engineers and setting up CI/CD and a code review culture along the way. The platform ended up running for 50-plus enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity, a Canadian company valued north of a billion dollars, acquired us in 2021, which told me the early technical bets had actually held up. If I did it again I'd invest in observability much sooner, we bolted on proper APM late, and I'd design for multi-tenancy from day zero instead of retrofitting it once it started to hurt.

## 2. The strangler-fig migration instead of the rewrite everyone wanted

Alaya's monolith started showing real scaling cracks just as investors were pushing hard for new features rather than a pause to fix infrastructure. The team was split between doing a full microservices rewrite and something more conservative, and I needed to land on a decision I could actually defend, not just one that felt safer. So I spent two weeks running a technical spike with load tests on both approaches, then brought the CTO and investors concrete numbers instead of opinions about which way to go. What I proposed was a strangler-fig migration: pull out only the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest of the monolith alone, and set clear criteria for when we'd extract the next piece. The first extracted module shipped in six weeks, the busiest endpoints got about 40% faster, and the remaining monolith stayed stable straight through the acquisition, so we never needed the big-bang rewrite people were afraid of. The hard part honestly wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the disciplined choice; with more benchmarking up front I think I could have shortened that debate.

## 3. Getting AI-assisted engineering adopted across teams that didn't report to me

After Benevity acquired Alaya, I joined as a Senior Staff IC on a team about ten times the size of what I was used to, with no direct reports and no formal mandate over anyone. I wanted engineers across two teams to actually change how they worked and bring AI tooling into their daily flow, but ordering that from the outside wasn't an option and probably wouldn't have stuck anyway. I started small, with three engineers who were already curious, built internal demos that showed real time saved rather than talking in the abstract, and ran informal lunch-and-learn sessions with no mandate attached. I also put together reusable prompt templates and committed them to a shared repo, and wrote up concrete results, like a PR review cycle that dropped by two hours, so people could see the payoff for themselves. Within three months, around 70% of engineers on both teams were using AI-assisted review and generation regularly, and I ended up the informal go-to person for AI tooling across the org. It was slower than a top-down rollout would have been, but the adoption held because people chose it, and they were the ones telling their peers to try it, which I couldn't have manufactured with a directive.

## 4. Mapping the org before trying to change anything

Joining Benevity's platform team, I was dropped into a much larger codebase built in Java with a microservices setup very different from anything at Alaya, and a different culture around decision-making entirely. My instinct early on was to start proposing fixes for things that looked wrong to me, but that would have burned trust fast on a team that didn't know me yet. Instead I spent the first month just mapping the existing architecture and understanding why things were built the way they were before I said much of anything, and I built relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing any technical opinions their way. That patience paid off: I contributed to three major cross-team architecture decisions afterward and became the architecture liaison for the Europe-facing product surface. Looking back, I moved too fast in the first couple of weeks trying to "fix" things that weren't actually broken, just unfamiliar to me, and slowing down to listen first is what made the rest of it work.

## 5. Making the case for platform investment against feature pressure

Throughout Alaya's growth, there was constant tension between the team's push for new customer-facing features and the platform work that would keep the whole thing from falling over later, and investors weren't naturally sympathetic to time spent on the latter. Rather than arguing the case on principle, I treated it like any other technical decision and brought data: load test results, projected failure points, and a phased plan that let feature work continue in parallel with the platform fixes instead of stopping for them. That framing, showing the cost of doing nothing next to the cost of the fix, is what got buy-in for the strangler-fig migration described above, and it's the same approach I'd bring to arguing for platform investment on a system serving millions of live endpoints. The lesson that stuck is that technical credibility with non-technical stakeholders comes from specific numbers, not conviction, and I try to have the numbers ready before I walk into that kind of conversation now.
