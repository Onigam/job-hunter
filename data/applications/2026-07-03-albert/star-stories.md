# STAR Stories: Albert (Principal / Distinguished Engineer, path to CTO/co-founder)

Picked for questions about founding-level ownership, 0 to 1 building, technical judgment under pressure, and operating without a mandate. All stories drawn from `profile/star-bank.md`, reframed for a role where the expectation is founder-level accountability rather than pure IC execution.

## 1. Building Alaya from a blank page to acquisition

Someone asked me once what my job title was in the first six months at Alaya, and the honest answer was "whatever needed doing." In 2016 we had no product, no customers, and no team, just a decision to build an employee purpose and impact platform and the belief that enterprise clients would pay for it once it existed. I picked Node.js, React, and PostgreSQL because I could move fast without over-engineering for scale I didn't have yet, and I had a working demo inside three months, which is what let the founders start closing early conversations. From there I kept expanding the platform myself for a while: multi-tenant architecture, an API layer, impact reporting, and eventually the postal campaign features clients kept asking for. Once we had traction I hired the first three engineers and set up the basics that let a team actually function, CI/CD, code review, some architecture docs, so the next hires wouldn't inherit a mess. We got to 50+ enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and in 2021 Benevity, a Canadian unicorn, acquired the company, which was about as direct a validation of those early technical calls as I could have asked for. If I'm honest, I underestimated how expensive it would be to retrofit multi-tenancy after the fact, and I put observability in far too late. Both are things I'd design for from day zero next time, and both are exactly the kind of debt that shows up first in a 0 to 1 product like the ones Albert is still building.

## 2. The strangler fig decision nobody wanted to make

At Alaya, our monolith started cracking under load right as investors were pushing hard for new features, not a rewrite. The team was split between a full microservices rewrite and just living with the pain, and neither option was actually defensible without data. So I ran a two-week spike, built prototypes of both approaches, and put them under load tests instead of arguing from opinion. What came out of that wasn't either extreme: I proposed pulling out the two highest-traffic modules first and leaving the rest of the monolith alone, with clear criteria for when we'd extract the next piece. We shipped the first extraction in six weeks and saw about a 40% performance gain on the busiest endpoints, and the remaining monolith held up fine all the way through the acquisition. No big-bang rewrite ever happened. The hard part wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that doing less was the right call, and the load test numbers did most of that convincing for me. Next time I'd try to get that benchmark data even earlier, because the debate itself cost us more time than the actual migration did.

## 3. Driving AI adoption across teams that didn't report to me

After the Benevity acquisition I had no direct reports and no mandate, just a conviction that AI-assisted development was going to change how the org worked, on two teams that had zero reason to listen to me. Rather than pitch it top down, I found three curious engineers, built small internal demos that showed real time saved on actual PRs, and ran informal lunch sessions with no agenda beyond "here's what this looks like." I wrote up the wins as they happened, things like a two-hour review cycle collapsing to twenty minutes, and put reusable prompt templates in a shared repo so people could just take them rather than start from scratch. Three months in, about 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted review and generation daily, and I'd become the person people asked when they wanted to get better at it. It was slower than a mandate would have been, but it stuck, because the engineers who adopted it did so on their own terms and then defended it to their peers themselves. That's the version of influence I'd want to bring into a role where I'm supposed to set technical direction without always having formal authority to back it.

## 4. Mapping the org before touching anything

Joining Benevity after the acquisition meant walking into a team ten times the size of Alaya, a different language (Java instead of Node), different microservices conventions, and a culture I didn't understand yet. My instinct was to start fixing things I thought were broken, and that instinct was wrong. I caught myself early enough, spent the first month just mapping the existing architecture and talking to the platform and infra teams before I proposed a single change, and built relationships before I built opinions. That patience is what let Alaya's capabilities actually get folded into the platform without a turf war, and it's why I ended up as the architecture liaison for the Europe-facing product surface and contributed to three major cross-team decisions afterward. The reflection I keep coming back to is that moving fast on trust matters more than moving fast on code, especially the first ninety days somewhere new, and that's doubly true walking into a company at the scale Albert operates at.

## 5. Deciding what to own versus what to buy

One judgment call that came up constantly at Alaya, and again at Benevity, was when to build something ourselves versus reach for a managed service. Early on at Alaya I chose to write our own lightweight API layer rather than adopt a heavier framework, mostly because I could tune it to exactly what our multi-tenant reporting needed and I didn't want to fight someone else's abstractions while we were still figuring out the product. That paid off when reporting became one of the features clients cared about most. At Benevity the calculus flipped: the org already had strong platform conventions, so my job was recognizing where reinventing something would just create maintenance debt nobody asked for, and pushing back on my own instinct to rebuild things I didn't fully understand yet. The result in both cases was the same underlying skill, knowing which battles are worth the engineering investment and which aren't, and I think that's exactly the kind of judgment a role with real ownership over 0 to 1 products has to get right more often than it gets to ask permission for.
