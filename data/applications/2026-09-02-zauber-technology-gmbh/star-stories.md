# STAR Stories, Zauber Technology (Founding Engineer)

Picked for a founding-engineer role: building from zero, making calls under pressure with no playbook, driving AI tooling adoption without authority, and working close to customers.

## 1. Building Alaya from a blank page

In 2016 I cofounded Alaya, a purpose and impact platform for employee engagement, and we started with nothing: no product, no customers, just a signed first enterprise deal and a deadline. My job was to architect and build the whole thing, alone at first, while the business side was already selling. I picked a pragmatic stack, Node.js, React, Postgres, that could grow without over-engineering it on day one, and pushed to get a working demo in front of the first client within three months. From there it was constant expansion: multi-tenant architecture, an API layer, impact reporting, and eventually a small team I hired and onboarded myself, setting up CI/CD and a review culture as we went. By the time Benevity acquired us in 2021, we had over 50 enterprise clients across Switzerland and France running on the platform I'd built from that blank page. If I did it again I'd invest in observability much earlier, we bolted on proper APM late, and I'd design for multi-tenancy from day zero instead of retrofitting it under load.

## 2. A hard architecture call under investor pressure

At Alaya, our monolith started showing real scaling cracks right as we were closing bigger enterprise deals, and the team split between a full microservices rewrite and something more modest. Investors wanted new features shipped, not months spent on a rewrite, so I had to make a call that held up under both engineering and business scrutiny. I ran a two-week spike comparing both approaches with actual load tests, then took the numbers, not opinions, to the CTO and investors. What I proposed was a strangler-fig migration: pull out the two highest-traffic modules first and leave the rest of the monolith alone, with clear criteria for when to extract more. The first extraction shipped in six weeks and cut latency on the busiest endpoints by about 40%, and the remaining monolith stayed stable straight through the acquisition. No big-bang rewrite ever happened. The hard part wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the disciplined choice. Next time I'd front-load more benchmarking so that argument takes less time to win.

## 3. Driving AI tooling adoption with no mandate

After joining Benevity as a Senior Staff IC, I wanted two teams that didn't report to me to start using AI-assisted development, and I had no authority to make that happen, only the case for it. I started small: three engineers who were curious, a few internal demos showing real time saved on actual PRs, and informal lunch-and-learn sessions with no top-down push behind them. I built out reusable prompt templates and committed them to a shared repo so the habit had something concrete to latch onto, and documented wins as they came, like a PR review cycle that dropped by two hours. Within three months, about 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted review and generation in their daily work, and I'd become the informal go-to person for AI tooling across the org. It was slower than a mandate would have been, but it stuck, because people who opt in on their own terms end up defending the practice to their peers in a way a decree never gets you.

## 4. Rebuilding trust after an acquisition

When Benevity acquired Alaya in 2021, I went from a ten-person startup I'd helped build to a team ten times that size, a different codebase in Java and microservices, and a culture I didn't know yet. The task was integrating Alaya's capabilities into their platform without losing the speed we'd built our reputation on, and without steamrolling people who'd been there longer than the acquisition. I spent the first month just mapping their existing architecture before I proposed anything, and made a point of building relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing technical opinions on them. That patience is what let me drive adoption of AI-augmented workflows later and become an architecture liaison for the Europe-facing product surface. We migrated the core Alaya features over cleanly and I ended up contributing to three major cross-team architecture decisions in the following year. My first instinct was to move fast and fix things that, from the inside, weren't actually broken. Slowing down and listening first is what made the rest of it work, and it's the same instinct I'd bring to walking into an existing customer relationship at a founding-engineer stage company.
