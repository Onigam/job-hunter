# STAR Stories - EWOR, AI Co-Founder / CTO

Picked for a founder-selection process rather than a hiring manager: stories that show building from zero under real ambiguity, making technical calls without a safety net, leading people who don't report to me, and staying coachable inside a much bigger structure once I no longer control it.

## 1. Building the platform from zero, before there was a company to speak of

I co-founded Alaya in 2016 with a blank page, no customers, and a business team already out signing enterprise clients before we had much of a product to show them. My job was to architect and build the whole thing, alone at first. I picked a pragmatic stack, Node.js, React, Postgres, that could scale without turning into a science project, had a working demo inside three months, and kept expanding from there: multi-tenant architecture, an API layer, impact reporting, campaign tooling. Once we had traction I hired and onboarded the first three engineers and put in CI/CD and a real code review culture before either felt urgent. The platform ended up running for fifty-plus enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity, a Canadian unicorn valued north of a billion dollars, acquired the company in 2021. What I'd change: I'd design the data model for multi-tenancy from day one instead of retrofitting it later, since the cost of that decision compounds faster than almost anything else you build early.

## 2. A cost and architecture tradeoff decided with data, not conviction

At Alaya our monolith started showing scaling cracks right when investors wanted new features, not a rewrite. The team was split between a full microservices rewrite and something more modest, and it landed on me to make a call that wouldn't blow up either the system or the roadmap. I ran a two-week spike comparing both approaches with actual load tests, brought the numbers to the CTO and the board instead of opinions, and proposed a strangler-fig migration: extract the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest alone, and set clear criteria for when to extract more. We shipped the first extracted module in six weeks and saw close to a 40% performance gain on the busiest endpoints, and the rest of the monolith stayed stable all the way through the acquisition. The hard part wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the disciplined choice. Next time I'd run that spike earlier, before the debate had a chance to turn into a standoff.

## 3. Getting two teams to adopt something with no authority to require it

At Benevity I was a Senior Staff engineer with no direct reports, and I wanted two teams that didn't report to me to actually use AI-assisted development instead of treating it as a novelty. I didn't have the authority to decree it, so I didn't try. I started with three curious engineers, built a few internal demos that showed real time saved, and ran informal lunch sessions with no agenda beyond "here's what this looks like." From there I wrote reusable prompt templates, put them in a shared repo, and wrote up concrete wins, like a two-hour saving on a single PR review cycle. Within three months about 70% of engineers across both teams were using it regularly, and I'd become the person people came to with AI tooling questions across the org. It was slower than a mandate would have been, but it stuck, because people adopted it on their own terms and told their peers it worked. Building a company mostly means getting people who don't have to listen to you to want to anyway, and that's the muscle this exercised.

## 4. Staying coachable once I no longer owned the whole decision

When Benevity acquired Alaya in 2021, I went from owning a small stack solo to sitting on a team ten times the size, with a different language, Java instead of Node, and processes I hadn't written and didn't fully agree with at first. My instinct was to start fixing things that looked broken from the outside. I sat on that instinct instead, spent the first month mapping how the existing system actually worked, and built relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing any technical opinion. From there I helped migrate Alaya's core capabilities into Benevity's platform while still shipping new features, and became a cross-team architecture liaison for the Europe-facing product surface, contributing to three major architecture decisions along the way. The founder reflex is to trust your own read of a system; the harder skill, and the one I had to build under pressure, is knowing when someone else's read is better than yours and taking the coaching instead of defending the call.
