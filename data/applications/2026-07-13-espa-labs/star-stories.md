# STAR Stories: Espa Labs (Founding Software Engineer)

_Picked for: founding-engineer ambiguity, agent/orchestration building, systems design under pressure, and driving adoption without formal authority. All sourced from `profile/star-bank.md`._

## Story 1: Building Alaya from zero to acquisition

This is the one I'd lead with for a founding engineer conversation. Back in 2016 I co-founded Alaya, an employee purpose and impact platform, and we started with literally nothing: no product, no customers, just a idea the two of us believed in. My job was to architect and build the whole thing from scratch, alone at first, while the business side was already out signing enterprise clients who expected something to exist. I picked a stack that was pragmatic rather than impressive, Node.js, React, PostgreSQL, because at that stage speed to a real demo mattered more than architectural purity. We had core features live inside three months, then I spent the next few years layering in multi-tenancy, an API layer, and impact reporting, while also hiring and onboarding the first three engineers and putting in place the code review habits and architecture docs that let more people touch the codebase without breaking it. By the time Benevity acquired us in 2021, we had fifty-plus enterprise clients across Switzerland and France running on it, which is about as direct a validation of those early technical calls as you can get. If I'm honest, the thing I'd change is investing in observability sooner, we bolted on proper APM late, and multi-tenancy would have been cheaper if I'd designed for it from day zero instead of retrofitting it.

## Story 2: Driving AI tooling adoption with no direct reports

This maps closely to the orchestration and agent-building side of your role. At Benevity I was a Senior Staff IC with no direct reports, but I wanted two engineering teams that didn't report to me to actually start using AI-assisted workflows in their daily work, not just talk about it in a Slack channel. Rather than pushing a mandate, I started with three engineers who were already curious, built small internal demos that showed concrete time saved, and ran informal lunch-and-learn sessions with no obligation attached. I also put together reusable prompt templates and committed them to a shared repo so the wins didn't stay locked in my head. Within three months, about seventy percent of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted code review and generation regularly, and I'd become the person people pinged when they wanted to get more out of the tooling. It was slower than just announcing a policy, but it stuck, because people adopted it on their own terms and then went and told their teammates why it was worth it. I don't think a top-down decree would have gotten anywhere close to that adoption rate.

## Story 3: A hard architecture call under investor pressure

Good example of systems-design thinking that isn't just theoretical. At Alaya, our monolith started showing real scaling cracks right as investors were pushing hard for new features, not a rewrite. The team was split between a full microservices rewrite and something more modular but still monolithic, and neither camp had much more than opinions to back their side. I ran a two-week spike with load tests on prototypes of both approaches, then brought the CTO and investors concrete numbers instead of a philosophical argument. What we landed on was a strangler fig migration: pull out the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest of the monolith alone, and define clear criteria for when the next module was worth extracting. The first extracted module shipped in six weeks and cut latency by around 40% on our busiest endpoints, and the rest of the monolith held up fine all the way through the acquisition, no big-bang rewrite required. The hardest part honestly wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the disciplined choice. Next time I'd push for early benchmarking sooner just to shorten that debate.

## Story 4: Integrating into a codebase ten times the size of what I'd built

Relevant for a role where I'd be handing off pieces of the system to a growing team over time. When Benevity acquired Alaya in 2021, I joined a team about ten times larger than what I was used to, with a different culture, a Java-based microservices codebase instead of what I'd built, and processes I hadn't designed. Instead of showing up with opinions, I spent the first month just mapping how the existing architecture actually worked, and I made a point of building relationships with the platform and infra teams before I pushed for any technical changes. From there I helped drive the migration of Alaya's core capabilities into Benevity's platform while we kept shipping new features in parallel, and I ended up as a cross-team architecture liaison for the Europe-facing product surface, contributing to three major architecture decisions along the way. Early on I moved too fast trying to fix things that weren't actually broken in the new org, just different from what I knew. Slowing down and listening first is what turned that around, and it's a lesson I still apply anytime I join something already in motion.
