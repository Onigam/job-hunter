# STAR Stories: Pango, Founding Full-Stack Engineer

## 1. Building Alaya from a blank page

I co-founded Alaya in 2016 as an employee purpose and impact platform, and when we started there was nothing: no customers, no product, just an idea and a whiteboard. My job was to architect and build the whole thing myself first, then bring on a team while the business was already signing its first enterprise clients, so there wasn't much room to get the foundation wrong. I picked a pragmatic stack, Node.js, React, and PostgreSQL, that could carry us to production fast without locking us into decisions we'd regret later. The first three months went into getting a working demo in front of prospects, and from there I kept expanding: multi-tenant architecture, an API layer, impact reporting, postal campaign tooling. I hired and onboarded our first three engineers and put in place CI/CD and a code review culture before it became urgent. We got to 50-plus enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity, a Canadian unicorn, acquired us in 2021. Looking back, I'd have invested in real observability much earlier; we bolted on proper APM later than we should have, and retrofitting multi-tenancy cost us more than designing for it from day zero would have.

## 2. Choosing not to rewrite everything

At Alaya, our monolith started showing real scaling cracks, and the team split into two camps: rewrite everything as microservices, or keep it as a modular monolith. Investors wanted new features shipped, not a multi-month rewrite, so I had to make a call that could hold up under both technical and business scrutiny. I ran a two-week spike comparing both approaches with actual load tests rather than opinions, then walked the CTO and investors through the numbers. What came out of that was a strangler-fig plan: extract only the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest alone, and set clear criteria for when to peel off more. The first extracted module shipped in six weeks and performance on our busiest endpoints improved by roughly 40%. The rest of the monolith stayed stable straight through the acquisition, no big-bang rewrite required. The hard part honestly wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that doing less was the right call; having the load-test data made that argument for me, and next time I'd run those benchmarks even earlier to cut the debate short.

## 3. Driving AI tooling adoption with no mandate

After Benevity acquired us, I was a Senior Staff IC on a team roughly ten times the size of Alaya, with no direct reports and no authority to tell anyone how to work. I wanted to get AI-assisted development into the daily workflow of two engineering teams that didn't answer to me, so I started small: three curious engineers, some internal demos showing concrete time saved, and informal lunch sessions instead of any kind of top-down rollout. I built reusable prompt templates and committed them to a shared repo, and documented wins as they happened, things like shaving two hours off a PR review cycle. Within three months, about 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted review and generation on their own. I ended up becoming the informal lead for AI tooling across the org, which wasn't the goal going in. It was slower than a mandate would have been, but it stuck, because the engineers who adopted it did so on their own terms and then sold it to their peers themselves. I couldn't have gotten that outcome with a decree.

## 4. Getting a new codebase before touching it

Joining Benevity meant a completely different world from Alaya: a team ten times larger, a Java microservices codebase instead of our Node stack, and a culture I didn't understand yet. My task was to integrate what we'd built at Alaya into Benevity's platform while keeping shipping new features, without losing the speed we'd had as a small team. I spent the first month just mapping the existing architecture before proposing a single change, and I built relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing any technical opinions on them. That groundwork is what let me become a cross-team architecture liaison for the Europe-facing product surface later on. We migrated the core Alaya features into Benevity's platform and I contributed to three major cross-team architecture decisions in the following year. My early instinct was to move fast and start fixing things that, from Benevity's perspective, weren't actually broken; slowing down and listening first is what turned that instinct around, and it's the same discipline I'd bring to walking into Pango's existing carrier and WMS integrations rather than assuming they need to be rebuilt.
