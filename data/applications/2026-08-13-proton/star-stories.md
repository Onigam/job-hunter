# STAR Stories - Proton, Site Reliability Engineer (Application Edge)

Picked for an infra/SRE conversation: a real scaling decision under load, building a platform's reliability posture from nothing, working inside a much larger and more structured engineering org, and driving change across teams without formal authority.

## 1. A hard architecture call under investor pressure

At Alaya, our monolith started showing scaling cracks right around the time investors wanted new features, not a rewrite. The team was split between a full microservices rewrite and something more modest, and it fell to me to make a call that wouldn't blow up either the roadmap or the system. I ran a two-week spike comparing both approaches with actual load tests, brought the numbers, not opinions, to the CTO and the board, and proposed a strangler-fig migration instead: pull out the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest alone, and set clear criteria for when to extract more. We shipped the first extracted module in six weeks and saw a roughly 40% performance gain on our busiest endpoints, and the rest of the monolith stayed stable all the way through the acquisition. The hardest part honestly wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the disciplined choice. Data made that argument faster than I could have. Next time I'd do the early benchmarking sooner just to cut the debate short.

## 2. Building Alaya's platform, and its reliability posture, from zero

In 2016 I co-founded Alaya, a purpose and impact platform for employee engagement, with a blank page and no customers. My job was to architect and build the whole thing, alone at first, while the business side was already out signing early enterprise clients, so there wasn't much room to overthink the stack. I picked something pragmatic, Node.js, React, Postgres, that could scale without turning into a science project, got a working demo out inside three months, and kept expanding from there: multi-tenant architecture, an API layer, reporting, campaign tooling. Once we had traction I hired and onboarded our first three engineers and put in CI/CD and a real code review culture before either felt urgent. The platform ended up running for fifty-plus enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity, a Canadian unicorn, acquired us in 2021, which told me the early bets held up. If I did it again I'd add proper observability much earlier, we bolted on APM late, and that's the gap I'd want to close fastest on any infra team I join now.

## 3. Integrating into a much bigger, more structured org without losing momentum

When Benevity acquired Alaya in 2021 I went from owning a small stack solo to sitting on a team ten times the size, with a different language, Java instead of Node, a different architecture, and processes I hadn't written. I spent the first month mostly listening, mapping how the existing system actually worked before I proposed touching anything, and built relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing any technical opinions. From there I helped drive the migration of Alaya's core capabilities into Benevity's platform while still shipping new features, and ended up as a cross-team architecture liaison for the Europe-facing product surface, contributing to three major architecture decisions along the way. My first instinct had actually been to move fast and start fixing things that weren't broken in the new org, and dialing that back turned out to matter more than any specific technical contribution I made that quarter.

## 4. Leading a change nobody asked me to lead

Later, as a Senior Staff engineer at Benevity with no direct reports, I wanted two teams that didn't report to me to actually adopt AI-assisted development instead of treating it as a novelty. Rather than pushing a mandate I started small: three curious engineers, a few internal demos showing real time saved, informal lunch sessions with no agenda beyond "here's what this looks like." I built out reusable prompt templates, put them in a shared repo, and wrote up concrete results, like a documented two-hour saving on a single PR review cycle. Within three months about 70% of engineers across both teams were using it regularly, and I'd become the informal go-to on AI tooling for the org. It was slower than just decreeing it from the top, but it stuck, because people had adopted it on their own terms and were the ones telling their peers it worked. The same approach applies to any process change on a small, high-standards team, mandates rarely outperform a working demo.
