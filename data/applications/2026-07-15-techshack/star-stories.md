# STAR Stories: TechShack (Fractional CTO / Technical Advisor)

Picked for a fractional advisory context: influence without full-time presence, technical roadmap ownership, remote team oversight, and decisions that have to hold up when you're not in the room every day.

## Story 1: Building Alaya from zero to acquisition

In 2016 I cofounded Alaya, a purpose and impact platform for employee engagement, and we started with nothing, no customers, no product, just an idea we had to prove out fast. My job was to architect and build the whole thing from scratch, alone at first, while the business side was already signing enterprise clients who expected something real to show up. I picked a stack that was boring on purpose, Node.js, React, PostgreSQL, because we needed to move without painting ourselves into a corner, and I got a working core in about three months so we'd have something to demo. From there I kept expanding it in slices: multi-tenant architecture, an API layer, impact reporting, campaign tooling, and along the way I hired and onboarded our first three engineers and put in place code review habits and basic architecture docs so the decisions wouldn't just live in my head. We ended up with 50+ enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity, a Canadian company valued north of a billion dollars, acquired us in 2021, which felt like a fairly direct confirmation that the early technical bets had been the right ones. If I did it again I'd bring in proper observability much earlier, we added real APM later than we should have, and I'd design for multi-tenancy from day zero instead of retrofitting it once it started to hurt.

## Story 2: A hard architecture call under investor pressure

At Alaya, our monolith started showing real scaling cracks right around the time investors were pushing hard for new features, not a rewrite, and the team was split between a full microservices rewrite and something more modest. My task was to make a call that would hold up technically without blowing up the delivery pressure we were under. I ran a two-week spike comparing both paths with actual load tests instead of opinions, then took the numbers to the CTO and investors and proposed a strangler fig approach: pull out the two highest-traffic modules first and leave the rest of the monolith alone, with clear criteria for when we'd extract anything further. We shipped the first extracted module in six weeks and saw about a 40% performance gain on the busiest endpoints, and the rest of the system stayed stable straight through the acquisition, no big-bang rewrite required. The hardest part honestly wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the disciplined choice; the data did most of the convincing, and next time I'd front-load even more of that benchmarking to shorten the debate.

## Story 3: Post-acquisition integration at Benevity

Joining Benevity after the acquisition put me on a team about ten times the size of Alaya, with a different codebase, Java and microservices instead of what I'd built, and a different culture entirely. I needed to fold Alaya's capabilities into their platform while still shipping new features, without losing the velocity that had gotten us acquired in the first place. Instead of walking in with opinions, I spent the first month just mapping how their architecture actually worked, and I built relationships with the platform and infra teams before I pushed for any technical changes. That groundwork let me drive adoption of AI-assisted workflows, things like Copilot and shared prompt patterns, across the wider engineering org, and I became the informal architecture liaison for the Europe-facing side of the product. We migrated the core Alaya features successfully and I ended up contributing to three major cross-team architecture decisions. Looking back, I moved too fast early on trying to fix things that weren't actually broken in the new org, and slowing down to listen first is what made the rest of it work.

## Story 4: Leading change without formal authority

As a senior staff IC at Benevity I had no direct reports, but I still needed two teams that didn't report to me to change how they worked day to day and adopt AI-assisted development practices. I started small, with three engineers who were genuinely curious rather than trying to mandate anything from the top, and built internal demos that showed concrete time saved rather than just telling people it would help. We ran informal lunch sessions, no requirement to attend, and I put together reusable prompt templates in a shared repo so people could just take what worked. Within about three months, roughly 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted review and generation as part of their normal workflow, and I'd become the org's informal lead on that tooling without anyone appointing me to it. The approach was slower than a top-down decree would have been, but it stuck, because people who adopt something on their own terms end up defending it to others, which isn't something you can get from a mandate.
