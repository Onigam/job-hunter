# STAR Stories: On AG, Principal Engineer (AI & Cloud Infrastructure)

Picked for this role because it leans on platform building, mentoring engineers on distributed systems and MLOps/LLMOps judgment, and making architecture calls that hold up under pressure. Four stories, in first person.

## 1. Building a platform from a blank page (founding engineer, ambiguity)

In 2016 I co-founded Alaya, an employee purpose and impact platform, and there was nothing to start from: no customers, no product, just an idea we'd sold to a couple of early enterprise clients. My job was to architect and build the whole thing, alone at first and then with a small team, while the business was closing deals on features that didn't exist yet. I picked a stack that could scale without over-engineering it on day one, Node.js, React, PostgreSQL, and pushed to get a working demo out within three months. From there it was constant expansion: multi-tenant architecture, an API layer, impact reporting, and eventually onboarding the first three engineers with proper CI/CD and a code review culture instead of just shipping straight to production. The platform ended up running for 50+ enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity, a Canadian company valued north of a billion dollars, acquired us in 2021. Looking back, I'd invest in observability much earlier; we bolted on proper APM late and paid for it in debugging time. I'd also design for multi-tenancy from day zero rather than retrofitting it, since that retrofit cost us more than the original build.

## 2. Choosing not to rewrite the monolith (architecture under pressure)

At Alaya, our monolith started showing real scaling cracks right as investors were pushing hard for new features, not a rewrite. The team was split between a full microservices rewrite and something more conservative, and I needed a decision that was defensible on data rather than opinion. I ran a two-week technical spike comparing both approaches with actual load test prototypes, then took the results to the CTO and investors instead of arguing from intuition. What came out of that was a strangler fig migration: pull the two highest-traffic modules out first, leave the rest of the monolith alone, and define clear criteria for when to extract anything else. The first extracted module shipped in six weeks, the busiest endpoints got about 40% faster, and the remaining monolith stayed stable all the way through the acquisition. No big-bang rewrite ever happened. What I'd change is doing more of that benchmarking earlier; convincing people that not rewriting everything was the right call took longer than the technical work itself, and data was really the only thing that shortened the argument.

## 3. Getting AI tooling adopted without being anyone's manager

After the Benevity acquisition, I was a Senior Staff IC with no direct reports, but I wanted two teams that didn't report to me to actually change how they worked and start using AI-assisted development in their daily flow. Ordering that from the top wasn't an option, and I don't think it would have stuck even if it were. I started with three engineers who were already curious, built small internal demos that showed concrete time saved on real tasks, and ran informal lunch-and-learn sessions with no mandate attached, just people showing other people something useful. I also put together reusable prompt templates and committed them to a shared repo so the habit had something to attach to. Within three months, about 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted code review and generation, and I'd become the informal go-to person for that kind of tooling across the org. It was slower than a top-down rollout would have been, but it stuck because people adopted it on their own terms and then defended it to their peers, which I couldn't have manufactured with a policy.

## 4. Learning the org before trying to fix it

Joining Benevity after the acquisition meant going from founding engineer at a small company to one voice on a team roughly ten times the size, with a different codebase, Java and microservices instead of what I'd built, and a different set of processes I didn't yet understand. My instinct early on was to start proposing fixes for things that looked wrong to me, and that instinct was mostly premature. I spent the first month just mapping how the existing architecture actually worked and building relationships with the platform and infrastructure teams before I pushed any technical opinion. That groundwork is what let me help migrate Alaya's core capabilities into Benevity's platform, contribute to three cross-team architecture decisions, and become a liaison for the Europe-facing product surface. The result held up, but the bigger lesson was personal: I moved too fast trying to fix things that weren't actually broken in the new org, and slowing down to listen first is what made the later work land.
