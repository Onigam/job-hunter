Legal search has an unusual property: the cost of a wrong answer is asymmetric. A ranking mistake in e-commerce costs a sale; in legal research, it exposes a client to a missed precedent or a misread jurisdiction rule. That asymmetry makes the retrieval problem at Omnilex technically interesting rather than another semantic search exercise. The Swiss Federal Chancellery already using the product in production tells me legal-grade precision requirements are baked into the engineering constraints, not aspirational. That's the kind of hard requirement worth building for.

TypeScript/Node.js is the core of my career, from Alaya, the enterprise SaaS platform I co-founded, through the Benevity acquisition. At Benevity, I've been running LLM workloads in production pipelines for two years, which is where I developed the operational intuition these systems require: latency variance as context depth grows, the quiet failures that come with model version changes, and what it actually takes to build eval frameworks that catch regressions before users do. I've also worked with semantic search and embeddings in production, and the retrieval architecture I've thought most carefully about is one where precision and recall are operational contracts with downstream users, not just offline metrics.
