# STAR+R Stories, Principal Engineer, Core Platform (Hostaway)

Picked for this role because it's a monolith-to-microservices story with real scale, cross-team leadership without a management title, and mentoring across teams that don't report to you. All five map to something in the Hostaway job description.

## 1. The strangler-fig migration (architecture trade-off under pressure)

At Alaya we had a monolith that was starting to show real scaling cracks right as we were signing bigger enterprise clients, and at the same time investors wanted new features, not a rewrite. The team was split between two camps: some wanted a full microservices rewrite, others wanted to just keep patching the monolith. I got asked to make the call and defend it. Instead of picking a side in the abstract, I spent two weeks running a spike where I built small prototypes of both approaches and load-tested them against our real traffic patterns, then brought the CTO and investors actual numbers instead of opinions. What I proposed was a strangler-fig approach: extract only the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest of the monolith alone, and define clear criteria for when we'd extract more. We shipped the first extracted module in six weeks and saw about 40% improvement on the busiest endpoints, and the remaining monolith stayed boring and stable all the way through the Benevity acquisition, which is exactly what you want from infrastructure you're not actively working on. Looking back, the data was what actually won the argument, not the architecture diagram, so if I were doing it again I'd run the load-test comparison earlier, before the debate had time to turn into a values disagreement about rewrites versus patches.

## 2. Building Alaya's platform from a blank page (ownership and ambiguity)

In 2016 I co-founded Alaya with no customers and no product, just a plan to build an employee engagement platform, and I was the only engineer for the first stretch. There was no backlog to inherit and no existing system to reason about, so the task was really about deciding what mattered enough to build first while the business was closing its first enterprise deals in parallel. I picked a deliberately boring stack, Node, React, Postgres, that wouldn't need heroics to scale, got a working demo out in about three months, and then layered in multi-tenancy, an API layer, and reporting as real client needs showed up rather than guessing ahead of them. Once we had traction I hired the first three engineers and set up the basics, CI/CD, code review norms, some lightweight architecture docs, so the team could grow without everything running through me. We ended up at 50-plus enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity acquired us in 2021, which told me the early calls were roughly right. The thing I'd do differently is design for multi-tenancy from day zero instead of retrofitting it later; that retrofit was more expensive than it needed to be, and it's the kind of thing this role would need to get right up front given how many integrations Hostaway's platform already carries.

## 3. Mentoring across teams with no formal authority

After the acquisition I was a Senior Staff IC at Benevity with no direct reports, but I wanted to get AI-assisted development practices adopted across two engineering teams that had zero obligation to listen to me. Mandating anything was off the table, so the task became figuring out how to get people to actually want to change how they worked. I started small, found three engineers who were curious rather than skeptical, built quick demos that showed concrete time saved on real PRs, and ran informal lunch-and-learn sessions with no agenda beyond "here's what this looks like." I also put together reusable prompt templates and committed them to a shared repo so people could just take them rather than starting from scratch. Within three months about 70% of engineers on both teams were using AI-assisted review and generation day to day, and I'd become the person people pinged when they wanted to get better at it. It was slower than a top-down rollout would have been, but the adoption stuck because people chose it, and they ended up advocating for it to others in a way a mandate never would have produced. For a role that's explicitly about mentoring through code review and design collaboration, that bottom-up instinct is the one I'd bring in.

## 4. Listening before changing anything post-acquisition

Joining Benevity meant landing on a team roughly ten times the size of Alaya, with a different language, Java instead of Node, a different architecture, microservices instead of a monolith, and different processes than I was used to. My instinct after building something from scratch is to have opinions fast, and I noticed that instinct was actively working against me here. The task was to become useful and eventually influential on an unfamiliar codebase without alienating people who'd built it long before I showed up. I spent the first month just mapping the existing architecture and asking questions before proposing a single change, and I made a point of building relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing any technical opinions their way. That patience paid off: I ended up contributing to three major cross-team architecture decisions and became a go-to architecture liaison for the Europe-facing side of the product. The honest reflection here is that I moved too fast in my first few weeks trying to fix things that weren't actually broken, just unfamiliar, and slowing down to listen first is what made the rest of it work.

## 5. Convincing a team to hold off on a rewrite

This connects to the strangler-fig story but the harder part was social, not technical. Several engineers on the team genuinely believed a full rewrite was the only clean path forward, and I had to make the case for a slower, staged approach without dismissing their concerns as wrong. My task was to get buy-in, not just permission from the CTO, because a migration that half the team resents tends to quietly fail regardless of the architecture. I walked people through the load-test data directly rather than presenting conclusions, let them poke holes in it, and set explicit criteria up front for when we'd extract the next module so it didn't feel like an indefinite delay dressed up as caution. By the time we shipped the first module, most of the skeptics were the ones proposing which module to extract next. If I did it again I'd loop the skeptics into running part of the spike itself rather than just reviewing the results, since ownership of the data would have shortened the persuasion phase even further.
