# STAR Stories: Founding Engineer, Zenline AI

## 1. Building a product from zero in an ambiguous environment

I co-founded Alaya in 2016 with no product, no engineering team, and no platform to build on. We were selling into enterprise CSR and employee engagement budgets, so the first demos had to hold up in front of skeptical buyers almost immediately. My job was to turn a blank page into something real customers could use, without making technical choices that would collapse the moment the company started growing. I picked a pragmatic stack, Node.js, React, PostgreSQL, and AWS, then built the first end-to-end workflows myself so we could get in front of customers fast rather than polishing in isolation. As the product found traction, I introduced clearer service boundaries, CI/CD, code review habits, and ran the first engineering hiring loop, staying close enough to sales and customer conversations that the architecture kept getting shaped by real usage instead of theory. We went from zero to a production platform used by more than 50 enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and the company was acquired by Benevity in 2021, with the core technical decisions holding up through scale and due diligence. Looking back, I'd invest earlier in observability and multi-tenancy boundaries; both got noticeably more expensive to retrofit once the customer base had grown.

## 2. A technical decision made under real pressure

At Alaya, growth had started to expose cracks in the monolith, and the team split into two camps: rewrite into microservices, or leave the architecture alone and keep shipping features. Investors wanted the latter, and honestly they weren't wrong to push. I needed a call that reduced risk and improved performance without freezing delivery for months. I ran a short spike comparing both approaches against our actual bottlenecks rather than abstract scaling fears, then reframed the debate around cost, delivery risk, and timing instead of architectural purity. Instead of a rewrite, I proposed extracting the two highest-traffic modules first, with explicit criteria for what stayed monolithic and what moved out next. We shipped the first extraction in about six weeks and saw roughly a 40 percent performance gain on the busiest endpoints, and the rest of the system stayed stable straight through the acquisition. The reflection that stuck with me: the best architecture call wasn't the most elegant one, it was the one that solved the immediate pressure without setting up a second crisis six months later.

## 3. Adapting after a major organizational change

When Benevity acquired Alaya, I went from running a company I'd helped build to being one senior engineer inside a much larger org, with a Java-based microservices codebase I didn't know and processes I hadn't shaped. The temptation was to immediately start "fixing" things that felt wrong compared to how we'd done them at Alaya. Instead I spent the first month mapping the existing architecture and building relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing any opinions. Once I understood where the actual friction lived, I took on cross-team architecture work, connected product concerns across regions, and introduced AI-assisted engineering patterns where they solved a real problem rather than everywhere at once. Alaya's core capabilities got integrated into the broader platform, I contributed to several cross-team architecture decisions, and became a go-to person for practical AI tooling adoption. What I learned is that seniority in a new environment starts with listening; moving fast only helps once you're solving the right problem.

## 4. Driving adoption without formal authority

At Benevity I was a Senior Staff IC with no direct reports, but I wanted two teams that didn't report to me to actually change how they worked and start using AI tooling in day-to-day development. A mandate wasn't an option, so I started with three engineers who were already curious and built small demos tied to problems they already had, rather than abstract capability showcases. Lunch-and-learn sessions stayed short, concrete, and low pressure, and I committed reusable prompt templates to a shared repo so people could try things on their own terms. Within about three months, roughly 70 percent of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted code review or generation in some form, and I'd become the informal AI tooling lead for that part of the org. The tradeoff was speed: bottom-up adoption is slower to start than a top-down mandate, but it stuck, because engineers who opted in on their own advocated for it to their peers in a way a policy memo never could.
