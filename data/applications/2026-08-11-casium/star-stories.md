# STAR Stories - Casium, Founding AI Engineer

## 1. Building Alaya from a blank page to acquisition

Back in 2016 I co-founded Alaya, a purpose and impact platform for employee engagement, and we started with nothing: no customers, no product, just an idea we thought enterprises would pay for. I was the one who had to turn that into working software while the business was already trying to close its first enterprise clients, so there wasn't room for a slow ramp-up. I picked a pragmatic stack, Node.js, React, and Postgres, that could scale without forcing us into premature architecture, and pushed to get a working demo in front of prospects within the first three months. From there I kept expanding the platform iteratively: multi-tenancy, an API layer, impact reporting, postal campaign tooling, and along the way I hired and onboarded our first three engineers and put in place CI/CD and a real code review culture. By the time Benevity, a Canadian unicorn valued north of a billion dollars, acquired us in 2021, we had more than fifty enterprise clients live across Switzerland and France. Looking back, I'd invest in observability much earlier than we did; we bolted on proper APM late, and retrofitting multi-tenancy cost more than designing for it from day zero would have.

## 2. Driving AI-assisted workflow adoption without any formal mandate

At Benevity I was a Senior Staff IC with no direct reports, and I wanted two engineering teams that didn't report to me to actually change how they worked by folding AI tooling into their day-to-day development. Mandating it wasn't an option, so I had to make the case through results instead of authority. I started with three engineers who were already curious, built small internal demos that showed concrete time savings rather than talking about them abstractly, and ran informal lunch-and-learn sessions with no agenda beyond "here's what this looks like in practice." I also put together reusable prompt templates and committed them to a shared repo so people could just take what worked instead of reinventing it themselves. Within three months, about 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted code review and generation as part of their normal workflow, and I ended up becoming the informal go-to person for AI tooling across the org. The slower, mandate-free approach turned out to be the right call: engineers who adopted it because they wanted to were the ones who actually kept using it and told other people about it, something a top-down rollout would never have gotten me.

## 3. Choosing a strangler-fig migration over a full rewrite under investor pressure

At Alaya, our monolith started showing real scaling cracks, and the team was split between a full microservices rewrite and a more modest modular monolith approach, while investors were pushing hard for new features rather than a rewrite of any kind. I needed a decision I could actually defend with data, not just an engineering opinion, so I ran a two-week technical spike that load-tested both approaches side by side and brought the CTO and investors concrete numbers instead of a pitch. What came out of that was a strangler-fig migration: extract only the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest of the monolith alone, and define clear criteria for when we'd extract anything further. We shipped the first extracted module in six weeks, and the busiest endpoints got about 40% faster. The rest of the monolith stayed stable straight through the acquisition, so we never needed the big-bang rewrite everyone was originally arguing about. The hardest part honestly wasn't the technical work, it was convincing the room that not rewriting everything was the disciplined choice; having the benchmarks ready from the start would have cut that debate down by weeks.

## 4. Integrating into a much larger codebase after the Benevity acquisition

Right after Benevity acquired Alaya in 2021, I joined a team roughly ten times the size of what I was used to, with a different culture, a Java microservices codebase instead of what I'd built, and processes I hadn't had a hand in designing. My job was to get Alaya's capabilities folded into Benevity's platform while still shipping new features, without losing the product velocity that had gotten us acquired in the first place. Rather than walking in with opinions, I spent the first month just mapping how the existing architecture actually worked, and I made a point of building relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing for any technical changes. From there I helped drive adoption of AI-augmented workflows across the org and became the architecture liaison for the Europe-facing side of the product. We got the core Alaya features migrated in, I contributed to three major cross-team architecture calls, and became the person two different teams came to for AI tooling questions. My one regret is moving too fast early on trying to fix things in the new org that genuinely weren't broken; slowing down and listening first is what actually made me effective there.
