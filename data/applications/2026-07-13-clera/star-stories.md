# STAR Stories, Clera, Founding Engineer (Potential CTO)

Picked for this one because it's a second-technical-hire role with a CTO path: full-stack ownership from close to zero, judgment calls on AI architecture under time pressure, and working alongside a founder rather than inside an established team.

## 1. Building Alaya from zero to acquisition (founding engineer, ambiguity, full-stack ownership)

In 2016 I co-founded Alaya with a blank page, no customers, no product, nothing to inherit from anyone. My job was to architect and build the whole platform, alone at first, while the business side was already signing enterprise clients, so there wasn't room to spend months designing something nobody could use yet. I picked Node, React and Postgres, a stack that could scale without over-engineering for a size we didn't have, and got a working demo in three months. From there it was iterative: multi-tenant architecture, an API layer, impact reporting, and eventually hiring and onboarding the first three engineers while I set up CI/CD and a review culture. The platform reached production with fifty-plus enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity acquired the company in 2021, which felt like proof the early calls were right when nobody was around to check them. If I did it again I'd bring in real observability much sooner, and I'd design for multi-tenancy from day zero instead of retrofitting it later. Clera is asking for the same muscle at a different layer: nobody's checked the coaching-loop architecture against real users yet either.

## 2. The strangler-fig call instead of a rewrite (technical judgment under pressure)

At Alaya our monolith started cracking under load right as investors wanted new features, not a quarter spent on architecture. The team was split between a full microservices rewrite and just patching what we had, and my task was finding a path that didn't sacrifice either side. I ran a two-week spike with actual load tests instead of opinions, then took the numbers to the CTO and investors rather than a slide deck of preferences. What I proposed was narrower than either camp wanted: extract only the two highest-traffic modules, leave the rest monolithic, and set clear criteria for extracting more later. The first module shipped in six weeks, the busiest endpoints got roughly 40% faster, and the rest of the monolith held steady all the way through the acquisition. Convincing people that restraint was the harder, better choice took longer than the engineering itself did. A coaching model that reasons over biosignals in real time needs the same discipline: knowing when the model should think and when the pipeline underneath it just needs to be fast and boring.

## 3. Driving AI-tooling adoption without a mandate (honestly framed against the frontier-model requirement)

At Benevity I had no authority over two teams whose workflow I wanted to change, specifically getting them to use AI-assisted development instead of doing everything by hand. Rather than push a policy nobody asked for, I started with three curious engineers, built demos that showed real time saved on real tickets, and ran lunch-and-learns nobody was required to attend. I put together reusable prompt templates and committed them to a shared repo so the habit could spread on its own. Within three months about 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted review and generation without me asking twice. I'll say directly where this sits against what Clera needs: this is hands-on LLM integration and prompt-engineering work across a codebase, not a consumer-facing model making real-time coaching calls off live biosignals. It's the closest thing in my background to that muscle, and the gap between the two is real enough to name.

## 4. Mapping before proposing at a much larger company (working alongside an existing technical voice)

After Benevity acquired Alaya, I joined as Senior Staff Engineer on a team roughly ten times the size, a different codebase in Java plus microservices, and processes nobody had asked my opinion on. My task was folding Alaya's capabilities into their platform without losing the speed we'd built, while working with people who had their own way of doing things and no reason yet to trust mine. I spent the first month mapping the existing architecture before proposing a single change, and built relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing any technical opinion at all. That patience paid off: I ended up contributing to three major cross-team architecture decisions and became the liaison for the Europe-facing product surface. Early on I moved faster than the relationship could support, trying to fix things that weren't actually broken from where they sat, and slowing down to listen first is what turned that around. Coming in as the second technical voice at Clera, next to a founder who already has a strong view of the product, that's the instinct I'd lead with.
