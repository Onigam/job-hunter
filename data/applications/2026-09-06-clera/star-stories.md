# STAR Stories: Founding Engineer, Clera

Prepared for a role that's about full-stack ownership on a small team shipping daily against a single metric, not a broad architecture review. Expect questions on ambiguity, working without a spec, and technical trade-offs made under time pressure.

---

## 1. Building Alaya from a blank page ("Tell me about a time you built something from scratch")

In 2016 I co-founded Alaya with nothing, no customers, no product, just a bet that employee engagement platforms could be built better. I wrote the first version of the platform myself, a pragmatic Node.js and React stack chosen because it let me move fast without boxing myself into an architecture I'd regret in six months. The first three months were about getting to a demo the sales side could actually show a prospect, not about getting the data model perfect. Once we had our first enterprise clients signing, I hired the next three engineers and built out multi-tenancy, an API layer, and the reporting features that clients actually asked for, iterating in the order the business needed rather than the order that felt architecturally clean. That platform grew to 50-plus enterprise clients across Switzerland and France and was acquired by Benevity in 2021. Looking back, I'd have put in basic observability sooner, we were flying blind on a couple of production issues longer than we needed to, but the instinct to ship first and harden what mattered turned out right.

## 2. Choosing a strangler-fig migration over a rewrite ("Tell me about a technical trade-off under pressure")

Our monolith at Alaya started cracking under enterprise load right when investors wanted new features, not an infrastructure quarter. I could have just picked a side and argued for it, but instead I ran a two-week spike, built real load-test comparisons between a full microservices rewrite and a narrower extraction approach, and brought numbers into the room instead of opinions. The data pointed to pulling out only the two highest-traffic modules and leaving the rest alone, with clear criteria for when to touch more. We shipped the first extraction in six weeks and saw roughly a 40% latency improvement on the busiest endpoints, and the untouched part of the monolith stayed stable straight through acquisition diligence. The hardest part wasn't the technical call, it was convincing a room that not rewriting everything was the disciplined choice. If I did it again, I'd build that load-test comparison before the debate started, so there was less room for it to turn into opinion versus opinion.

## 3. Driving AI tooling adoption with no mandate ("How do you move fast without authority?")

At Benevity I was a Senior Staff IC with no direct reports, and I wanted two teams that didn't answer to me to actually use AI-assisted development day to day, not just nod along in a meeting. Announcing a policy wasn't something I could do, so instead I found three engineers who were genuinely curious, built small demos showing concrete time saved on real pull requests, and ran informal sessions people could skip without consequence. I wrote up specific wins, like a case that cut two hours off a review cycle, and put reusable prompt templates in a shared repo instead of a mandate document. It was slower than a top-down rollout, but about 70% of engineers across both teams were using the workflow within three months, and it stuck because nobody had to be told twice. That's roughly the loop I'd want to run at Clera: ship something small, measure it against a real number, let the result do the convincing.

## 4. A production gap I'd close earlier now ("Tell me about a mistake or a weak spot")

Observability at Alaya is the honest answer here. We treated monitoring as a nice-to-have through most of year one, prioritizing visible feature work because that's what showed up in demos to investors and clients. A couple of the harder production issues that year took roughly twice as long to diagnose as they should have, since we were reading logs by hand instead of looking at a dashboard. Nobody forced that call on me, I made it, and I'd make it differently now: basic tracing and error alerting from week one, even on a three-person team, because the cost of adding it later compounds fast once you have real users depending on the system. For a role like this one, where a small team owns the full stack and ships into production daily, that's exactly the kind of debt I'd want to name and fix early rather than let it quietly tax every incident.

## 5. Making the call on what not to build ("Tell me about scoping something down")

After the Benevity acquisition, I spent my first month mapping the existing architecture before proposing a single change, even though I had plenty of opinions about what looked wrong. The team I joined was ten times the size of Alaya, different codebase, different culture, and my instinct to move fast and start fixing things would have burned trust I hadn't earned yet. Instead I built relationships with the platform and infra teams first, then brought forward the changes that actually mattered once I understood why things were the way they were. That patience turned into three cross-team architecture decisions I ended up driving, and I became the person other teams pulled in for AI tooling questions. The lesson translates directly to a founding seat: moving fast matters, but on a team of three, wasting a week building the wrong thing costs more than a week spent making sure it's the right thing.
