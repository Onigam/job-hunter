# STAR Stories: Kangal AI, AI Founding Engineer

Selected for a founding-engineer role at a small, mission-driven AI startup. Prioritized: building from zero, hard technical calls under pressure, and driving change without formal authority.

---

## 1. Building Alaya from a blank page to acquisition

In 2016 I co-founded Alaya, a purpose and impact platform for employee engagement, and there was nothing to start from: no product, no customers, just an idea and a deadline to sign the first enterprise clients. My job was to architect and build the whole platform myself at first, then bring on a small team while the business kept moving. I picked a stack that could scale without turning into a science project, Node.js, React, and PostgreSQL, and pushed hard to get a working demo out inside three months. From there I built out the pieces that mattered as we grew: a multi-tenant architecture, an API layer, impact reporting, and eventually postal campaign tooling. I hired and onboarded our first three engineers myself and set up the basics I knew we'd need later, CI/CD, code review, some architecture notes so decisions didn't live only in my head. We ended up with over 50 enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity, a Canadian company valued north of a billion dollars, acquired us in 2021. Looking back, I waited too long on observability, we bolted on proper APM late, and I underestimated how expensive it would be to retrofit multi-tenancy after the fact. If I did it again I'd design for both from week one.

## 2. Choosing a strangler-fig migration over a rewrite under investor pressure

At Alaya, our monolith started showing real scaling cracks right as investors were pushing hard for new features, not a rewrite. The team was split between a full microservices rewrite and something more modest, and somebody had to make a call that would actually hold up. I ran a two-week technical spike comparing both paths with real load tests behind them, then took the results to the CTO and investors as numbers, not opinions. What I proposed was a strangler-fig approach: pull out the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest of the monolith alone, and set clear criteria for when we'd extract anything else. We shipped the first extracted module in six weeks and saw about a 40% performance gain on our busiest endpoints, and the rest of the monolith stayed stable straight through the acquisition. No big-bang rewrite, no six-month gap in feature delivery. The hard part honestly wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the disciplined choice. The data did most of the convincing for me. Next time I'd run the benchmarks even earlier so the debate itself takes less time.

## 3. Driving AI-tooling adoption across teams that didn't report to me

After Benevity acquired Alaya, I joined as a Senior Staff engineer with no direct reports, on a team ten times the size of what I was used to, running Java and microservices instead of what I'd built. I wanted engineers across two teams to actually adopt AI-assisted development, and nobody had given me a mandate to make that happen. So I started small: three curious engineers, a few internal demos that showed real time saved rather than a pitch deck, and informal lunch-and-learn sessions with no attendance requirement. I built reusable prompt templates and committed them to a shared repo so the value compounded instead of living in my head. Within three months, about 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted review and generation day to day, and I'd become the person people went to for this without ever being asked to take the role. It was slower than a top-down rollout would have been, but it stuck, because people who opt in on their own terms end up defending the thing to their peers in a way a mandate never gets you.

## 4. Integrating into a much larger org without breaking what worked

The month after the Benevity acquisition closed, I went from being the most senior technical voice at a small company to one of many at a much bigger one, with a different codebase and different habits around how decisions got made. My task was to fold Alaya's capabilities into Benevity's platform while still shipping new features, without losing the speed that got us acquired in the first place. I spent the first month just mapping their architecture before I proposed anything, and I built relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing any technical opinions on them. Once I had that context, I contributed to three major cross-team architecture decisions and became the informal liaison for the Europe-facing product surface. It worked, but not immediately, I moved too fast early on trying to fix things that weren't actually broken in the new org, and I had to consciously slow down and listen more before people trusted my read on their systems.
