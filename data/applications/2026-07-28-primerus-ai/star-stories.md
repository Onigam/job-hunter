# STAR Stories: Primerus AI (Technical Co-Founder / CTO)

Picked for a co-founder conversation: building from zero, making architecture calls under pressure, leading without formal authority, and navigating post-acquisition change. These map closely to what an early technical co-founder actually does day to day.

## 1. Building Alaya from a blank page

Back in 2016 I co-founded Alaya, a purpose and impact platform for employee engagement, and there was nothing to start from: no customers, no product, not even a settled idea of what the first version should do. My job was to architect and build the whole thing, alone at first, while the business side was already out signing enterprise clients who expected something to show up. I picked a stack, Node.js, React, Postgres, that let us move fast without painting ourselves into a corner, and pushed to get a working demo out in the first three months because that's what closed the early deals. From there it was constant expansion: multi-tenant architecture, an API layer, impact reporting, campaign tooling, and eventually hiring and onboarding the first three engineers along with the CI/CD and review habits that let more than one person touch the codebase safely. By the time Benevity acquired us in 2021, we had more than fifty enterprise clients across Switzerland and France running on it. If I did it again I'd put observability in from day one instead of bolting on proper APM two years in, and I'd design the multi-tenancy model up front rather than retrofitting it once it started to hurt.

## 2. The monolith-versus-rewrite call under investor pressure

At Alaya, the monolith we'd built was starting to crack under load, and the team split into two camps: rewrite it as microservices, or keep it as a modular monolith. Investors wanted new features, not a rewrite, so I couldn't just pick the technically satisfying answer, I had to pick one I could defend with numbers. I ran a two-week spike prototyping both approaches with load tests, then took the results, not opinions, to the CTO and investors. What came out of that was a strangler-fig plan: extract only the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest alone, and set clear criteria for when anything else would get pulled out. The first extracted module shipped in six weeks and cut latency on our busiest endpoints by roughly 40%, and the rest of the monolith stayed boring and stable straight through the acquisition. No big-bang rewrite, no six-month feature freeze. The hard part honestly wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the brave choice. Data made that argument for me, and next time I'd run the benchmarks even earlier so the debate doesn't eat weeks it doesn't need to.

## 3. Driving AI tooling adoption with no authority to mandate it

At Benevity I was a senior staff IC with no direct reports, and I wanted two teams that didn't answer to me to start using AI-assisted development practices. Ordering it wasn't an option, so I started small: three engineers who were already curious became the first users, and I built internal demos that showed concrete time saved rather than talking about potential. From there it was lunch-and-learn sessions with no mandate attached, a shared repo of prompt templates people could actually reuse, and documented wins, one story was saving about two hours on a single PR review cycle. Within three months, something like 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted review and generation, and I'd become the person people went to for AI tooling questions across the org without ever having the title for it. It was slower than a top-down rollout would have been, but it stuck, because people who adopt something on their own terms are the ones who go defend it to their peers afterward.

## 4. Landing inside a much bigger org after being acquired

When Benevity acquired Alaya in 2021, I went from a five-person engineering team to one roughly ten times the size, with a Java microservices codebase instead of the Node stack I knew and a set of processes I'd never worked inside. My task was to fold Alaya's capabilities into Benevity's platform while still shipping new features, without losing the speed we'd built our reputation on. I spent the first month just mapping how the existing architecture actually worked before I proposed changing anything, and built relationships with the platform and infra teams before I pushed a single technical opinion. That patience paid off: Alaya's core features migrated into the platform, I ended up contributing to three major cross-team architecture decisions, and became the liaison for the Europe-facing product surface. My first instinct had been to fix things that looked broken to outside eyes but weren't actually broken in context, and slowing down to listen first is what made the rest of it work.
