# STAR Stories: iudexnc, Co-Founder & CTO

## 1. Building Alaya from zero to acquisition

In 2016 I co-founded Alaya, a purpose and impact platform for employee engagement, with nothing but a blank page. No product, no customers, no team. I had to architect and build the whole platform myself at first, then grow a team around it, while the business was out signing its first enterprise clients in parallel. I picked a pragmatic stack, Node.js, React, PostgreSQL, that could scale without turning into a science project, and pushed to get core features live within three months so we had something to demo. From there it was iterative: multi-tenant architecture, an API layer, impact reporting, postal campaign tooling. I hired and onboarded the first three engineers and put in place CI/CD and a real code review culture before either was strictly necessary. The platform ended up serving over 50 enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity, a Canadian company valued north of a billion dollars, acquired us in 2021. Looking back, I'd have invested in observability much earlier since we bolted on proper APM late, and I underestimated how expensive it would be to retrofit multi-tenancy after the fact rather than designing for it from day one. That's the lesson I'd carry straight into a system that has to be multi-insurer from the start rather than single-tenant first.

## 2. A hard architecture call under investor pressure

At Alaya, the monolith started showing scaling cracks and the team split over whether to do a full microservices rewrite or stick with a modular monolith. Investors wanted new features, not months spent on a rewrite, so I had to land on a decision I could actually defend. I ran a two-week technical spike comparing both approaches with real load tests behind them, then brought the CTO and investors concrete numbers instead of opinions. What I proposed was a strangler fig migration: pull out the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest of the monolith alone, and set explicit criteria for when to extract anything further. The first extracted module shipped in six weeks and performance on the busiest endpoints improved by about 40%. The rest of the monolith stayed stable all the way through the acquisition, no big-bang rewrite required. The hard part wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the right call, and having the benchmark data ready cut that argument short. I'd do more of that early benchmarking next time so the debate never drags on as long as it did.

## 3. Post-acquisition integration at Benevity

After Benevity acquired Alaya in 2021, I joined as senior staff engineer on a team roughly ten times the size of what I'd worked with before, a different codebase in Java and microservices, and a different culture altogether. My job was to fold Alaya's capabilities into Benevity's platform without losing the product velocity we'd built up over five years. I spent the first month just mapping their existing architecture before proposing anything, and built relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing any technical opinions on them. Once that trust was there, I drove adoption of AI-augmented workflows, things like Copilot and shared prompt patterns, across the wider engineering org and became the cross-team architecture liaison for the Europe-facing product surface. The core Alaya features made it into Benevity's platform successfully, I contributed to three major cross-team architecture decisions, and became the go-to person on AI tooling for two engineering teams. My honest reflection is that I moved too fast early on trying to fix things that weren't actually broken in the new org, and slowing down to listen first is what made the rest of the integration work.

## 4. Leading without formal authority

As a senior staff IC at Benevity with no direct reports, I wanted to get AI-assisted development practices adopted across two teams that didn't report to me and had no obligation to listen. Rather than pushing a mandate, I started small: three engineers who were already curious became early adopters, and I built internal demos that showed concrete time saved rather than just talking about the potential. I ran informal lunch-and-learn sessions with zero mandate behind them, put together reusable prompt templates in a shared repo, and documented specific wins, like a case where AI-assisted review saved about two hours on a single PR cycle. Within three months, roughly 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted code review and generation day to day, and I ended up the informal lead on AI tooling for the org. It was slower than a top-down rollout would have been, but it stuck, because people who adopted it on their own terms went on to advocate for it themselves, which isn't something I could have manufactured with a decree from above. That pattern, earning adoption rather than mandating it, is exactly how I'd want to build engineering culture and hiring standards as an equal co-founder rather than as someone's direct report.
