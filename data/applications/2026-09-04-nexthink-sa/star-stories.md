# STAR Stories: Staff Backend Engineer, Nexthink SA

Prepared for a data platform / backend role: expect questions on scaling, cross-team ownership, production support, and mentoring.

---

## 1. Joining a large Java microservices codebase and earning cross-team pull ("Tell me about ramping up on an unfamiliar system")

When Benevity acquired Alaya in 2021, I went from being the person who'd written most of Alaya's Node.js and PostgreSQL codebase to one engineer among many inside a Java, microservices-heavy platform I hadn't touched before, on a team roughly ten times the size of what I was used to. Nobody expected me to have opinions in week one, and honestly I didn't have any worth sharing yet. I spent the first month reading service boundaries, sitting in on incident reviews, and asking dumb questions about why things were structured the way they were before I proposed anything. That patience paid off faster than I expected. By the time I did start pushing for changes, people already trusted that I understood the tradeoffs behind the existing design, not just the parts I disliked. Within a year I was the person other teams looped in when a cross-service change touched something in the European product surface, and I ended up contributing to three architecture decisions that spanned team boundaries. If I did it again I'd probably compress that listening phase by a couple of weeks. Some of the caution was well spent, but not all of it needed to be that slow.

## 2. Extracting the two highest-traffic modules from a monolith without a rewrite ("Tell me about a scaling decision you made under pressure")

At Alaya, our monolith started showing real cracks as enterprise clients scaled up, and the team was split between a full microservices rewrite and just living with it. Investors wanted new features that quarter, not an infrastructure project with no visible output. I ran a two-week spike comparing both approaches with actual load tests instead of opinions, then took the results to the CTO and investors and proposed a strangler-fig migration instead: pull out only the two modules taking the most traffic, leave everything else monolithic, and set clear criteria for when we'd extract more. We shipped the first extraction in six weeks and saw roughly a 40% improvement on our busiest endpoints. The rest of the monolith stayed boring and stable all the way through the acquisition, which in hindsight was exactly what it needed to do. The hard part wasn't the technical work, it was convincing a room that "don't rewrite everything" was the ambitious call, not the timid one. Data did most of that convincing for me. Next time I'd build the load-test comparison earlier, before the debate started, so there's less room for the conversation to turn into opinion versus opinion.

## 3. Getting engineers to adopt new tooling with zero authority to require it ("How do you influence teams that don't report to you?")

As a Senior Staff IC at Benevity with no direct reports, I wanted two teams to start using AI-assisted development in their daily workflow, and I had exactly no mandate to make that happen. So I didn't try to mandate it. I found three engineers who were already curious, built small internal demos that showed concrete time saved on real PRs, and ran informal lunch sessions with no pressure to attend. I wrote up specific results, like a case where AI-assisted review cut two hours off a PR cycle, and put reusable prompt templates in a shared repo anyone could take or ignore. It was slower than announcing a policy would have been. Within three months about 70% of engineers across both teams were using the workflow anyway, and it stuck because they'd chosen it rather than been told to. I became something like the informal lead for AI tooling across the org without ever having the title. I'm fairly convinced a top-down version of that rollout would have gotten compliance without buy-in, and the tools would have quietly stopped being used within a quarter.

## 4. Building a data and reporting pipeline from nothing while the business was already selling it ("Tell me about owning something end to end")

In 2016 Alaya was a blank page: no customers yet, but sales conversations already promising an impact reporting layer that didn't exist. I chose a pragmatic stack, Node.js, React, PostgreSQL, that could handle the load we actually had rather than the load we hoped to have someday, and built the core reporting and campaign features in about three months to get to a working demo. From there it was iterative: multi-tenancy, an API layer, and the reporting pipeline itself, expanding as real client data came in and exposed what the first version had gotten wrong. I also hired and onboarded our first three engineers along the way, which meant setting up CI/CD and a review culture at the same time I was still writing half the product. We ended up at 50-plus enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and the platform held up well enough through acquisition due diligence that Benevity didn't need to gut it. The thing I'd change is observability. We added proper monitoring embarrassingly late, and a couple of the harder production issues in year two would have taken half the time to diagnose if I'd built that in from month one instead of treating it as a nice-to-have.
