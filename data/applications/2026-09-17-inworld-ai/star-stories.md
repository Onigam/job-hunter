# STAR Stories, Inworld AI, Staff/Principal AI Engineer

Picked for this role: the panel will likely probe how I handle technical trade-offs under pressure, whether I can own a production system end to end, and whether I can move people without formal authority, since this posting leans research-to-production rather than pure platform work. I don't have a story about training or fine-tuning models directly, and I've said so plainly in the cover letter rather than stretching one of these to sound like something it isn't.

## 1. The architecture call under pressure

At Alaya we had a monolith that was starting to show real cracks under load, and the team was split on whether to do a full microservices rewrite or something more modular. The pressure wasn't only technical; investors wanted new features shipped, not a quarter spent rebuilding plumbing. I was asked to make a call that wouldn't blow up either the roadmap or the system. I ran a two-week spike comparing both approaches with actual load tests instead of arguing from first principles, then brought the numbers to the CTO and investors. What I proposed was a strangler-fig migration: pull out the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest of the monolith alone, and set clear criteria for when to extract more. We shipped the first extraction in six weeks, and latency on the busiest endpoints dropped by around 40%. The rest of the system stayed stable all the way through the Benevity acquisition. Looking back, the hard part wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the disciplined choice. If I did it again, I'd run the benchmarks earlier, before the debate turned emotional, so the data could settle things faster.

## 2. Building the thing from nothing

In 2016 I co-founded Alaya with a blank page: no product, no customers, no existing codebase to lean on. My job was to architect and build the whole platform, alone at first and then with a small team I was also hiring, while the business side was already out signing enterprise clients who expected something to exist. I picked a stack that could scale without over-engineering for a scale we didn't have yet, Node.js, React, PostgreSQL, and pushed to get a working demo in front of the first prospects within three months. From there it was constant iteration: multi-tenant architecture, an API layer, reporting features clients actually asked for. I also hired the first three engineers and put the basics in place, code review, CI/CD, some architecture documentation, before we strictly needed them, which mattered once the team grew. We ended up with more than 50 enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity, a Canadian unicorn, acquired the company in 2021. If I'm honest about what I'd change, I would have put real observability in place months earlier, and I underestimated how expensive it would be to retrofit multi-tenancy after the fact instead of designing for it from day one.

## 3. Owning a platform through someone else's acquisition

When Benevity acquired Alaya, I went from being the most senior engineer on a five-person team to one voice among many on a team ten times the size, with a different stack, Java and microservices instead of what we'd built, and different processes I didn't fully understand yet. My job was to get Alaya's core capabilities integrated into Benevity's platform without losing the velocity that had made us worth acquiring in the first place. I spent the first month doing almost nothing but mapping their existing architecture before proposing a single change, and I built relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing any technical opinions. That patience paid off: I ended up contributing to three major cross-team architecture decisions and became the person connecting the two orgs on infrastructure questions. My first instinct, honestly, was to move fast and start fixing things I assumed were broken. They mostly weren't. Slowing down and listening first is the thing I'd tell my earlier self to do from day one, not week five.

## 4. Getting a team to adopt something new without a mandate

At Benevity I was a senior IC with no direct reports, but I wanted two teams that didn't report to me to change how they worked, specifically to bring AI-assisted development, agent-based tooling and prompt patterns, into their daily workflow. Ordering that from the top wasn't an option, and it probably wouldn't have stuck even if it were. So I started small: three engineers who were already curious became early adopters, and I built demos that showed concrete time saved rather than making abstract claims. From there it was lunch-and-learn sessions with no mandate attached, plus a shared repo of reusable prompt templates people could take or leave. Within about three months, roughly 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted review and generation regularly, and I'd become the informal lead on AI tooling for the org. It was slower than a top-down rollout would have been, but it stuck, because people adopted it because it worked for them, not because someone told them to.
