# STAR Stories: Principal/Staff Software Engineer, Synthesia

Prepared for the Delivery and Impact interview and the System Design / Leadership rounds. Picked for fit with enterprise platform ownership, multi-tenant and compliance-adjacent systems, and driving change across teams without formal authority, since that's most of what the posting describes.

## Building the platform that got acquired

In 2016 I co-founded Alaya with a blank page and no customers, and I ended up architecting the whole platform, alone at first, while the business was closing its first enterprise clients. I picked a pragmatic stack, Node.js, React, PostgreSQL, that could scale without turning into a science project, and got the core features to a first demo inside three months. From there it was constant expansion: multi-tenant architecture, an API layer, impact reporting, and eventually hiring and onboarding the first three engineers while setting up CI/CD and a review culture that didn't exist before. The platform reached production with over 50 enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity, a Canadian unicorn valued north of a billion dollars, acquired the company in 2021. Looking back, I'd have invested in proper observability much earlier; we bolted on APM late, and I underestimated how expensive retrofitting multi-tenancy would be compared to designing for it from day zero, which is exactly the kind of enterprise-grade foundation work this role seems to care about.

## A hard call between a rewrite and shipping features

At Alaya, our monolith was starting to crack under load right as investors were pushing hard for new features, not a rewrite. Rather than pick a side in the abstract debate, I ran a two-week technical spike comparing a full microservices rewrite against a modular monolith, backed by actual load tests, and brought the CTO and investors concrete numbers instead of opinions. The path I proposed was a strangler-fig migration: extract only the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest monolithic, and define clear criteria for when to pull out more. We shipped the first extracted module in six weeks and saw about a 40% performance improvement on the busiest endpoints, while the remaining monolith stayed stable all the way through the acquisition. No big-bang rewrite needed. The hard part wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the disciplined choice; data closed that debate faster than argument ever could, and next time I'd front-load even more of that benchmarking before the discussion starts.

## Landing inside a much bigger org after the acquisition

After Benevity acquired Alaya in 2021, I joined a team roughly ten times the size of what I'd known, with a different culture, a Java microservices codebase, and processes I hadn't built myself. My job was to get Alaya's capabilities integrated into Benevity's platform without losing the product velocity we'd had as a five-person team. I spent the first month just mapping the existing architecture before proposing a single change, and I made a point of building relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing any technical opinions on them. That groundwork let me become a cross-team architecture liaison for the Europe-facing product surface and contribute to three major architecture decisions, on top of driving early adoption of AI-assisted workflows across the org. My honest reflection is that I moved too fast at first, trying to fix things that weren't actually broken in the new context; slowing down and listening changed how effective I was almost immediately.

## Getting two teams to adopt AI tooling with no mandate

As a Senior Staff IC at Benevity, with no direct reports, I wanted to get two teams that didn't report to me to actually adopt AI-assisted development instead of treating it as a novelty. I started small, with three engineers who were already curious, and built internal demos that showed concrete time saved rather than pitching the idea in the abstract. From there it was informal lunch-and-learn sessions with no mandate behind them, reusable prompt templates committed to a shared repo, and documented wins like a two-hour reduction on a single PR review cycle. Within three months, about 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted review and generation regularly, and I'd become the informal AI tooling lead for the org without anyone appointing me to it. It was slower than a top-down rollout would have been, but it stuck, because people who opt in on their own terms end up advocating for the thing themselves, which I couldn't have manufactured with a policy memo.
