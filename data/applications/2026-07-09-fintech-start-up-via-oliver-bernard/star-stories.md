## Building Alaya from a blank page

Best for: "Tell me about building something from zero" and "how do you handle ambiguity."

In 2016 I co-founded Alaya, an employee purpose and impact platform, with no product, no customers, and no team yet. My job was to architect and build the whole thing myself at first, while the business was already out signing its first enterprise clients, so there wasn't room to spend months on a perfect design. I picked a stack, Node.js, React, PostgreSQL, that could scale without being over-engineered for a company that didn't exist yet, and had core features working in about three months so we had something to demo. From there it was iteration: multi-tenant architecture, an API layer, impact reporting, all bolted on as real usage told us what mattered. I hired and onboarded the first three engineers along the way and set up CI/CD and a code review culture before the team got big enough that skipping it would've hurt. Alaya reached production with over 50 enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity, a Canadian unicorn valued above a billion dollars, acquired the company in 2021. Looking back, I'd invest in observability sooner; we bolted on proper APM late and paid for it in a few late-night debugging sessions. I also underestimated how expensive it would be to retrofit multi-tenancy after the fact, so next time I design for it from day zero.

## A hard architecture call under investor pressure

Best for: "Tell me about a technical trade-off" or "how do you handle technical debt."

At Alaya, our monolith started showing scaling cracks around the same time investors were pushing hard for new features, not a rewrite. The team was split between a full microservices rewrite and just patching the monolith, and I needed to make a call that wouldn't tank delivery either way. I ran a two-week spike comparing both approaches with real load tests rather than opinions, then took the numbers to the CTO and investors instead of a slide deck of arguments. What I proposed was a strangler fig migration: pull out the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest of the monolith alone, and set clear criteria for when to extract the next piece. The first extracted module shipped in six weeks and cut latency 40% on our busiest endpoints, and the rest of the monolith stayed stable straight through the acquisition. No big-bang rewrite ever happened. The hard part wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the disciplined choice. Data did most of that convincing for me. If I did it again I'd front-load even more benchmarking, since it would've cut the internal debate down by a couple of weeks.

## Driving adoption with no formal authority

Best for: "Tell me about leading a team" or "how do you influence without authority."

After joining Benevity as a Senior Staff IC, I wanted to push AI-assisted development practices across two teams that didn't report to me and had no reason to listen to someone new. Rather than asking for a mandate, I started with three engineers who were already curious, built small internal tooling demos that showed concrete time savings, and ran informal lunch-and-learn sessions with nothing forced about them. I wrote up reusable prompt templates, committed them to a shared repo, and documented specific wins, like the time a PR review cycle dropped by two hours because of a template someone used. Within three months, about 70% of engineers across both teams had adopted AI-assisted code review or generation on their own, and I ended up the informal go-to person for AI tooling across the org. It was slower than a top-down rollout would've been, but it stuck, because people who opt in on their own terms tend to defend the thing afterward instead of quietly dropping it.

## Landing in a codebase ten times bigger

Best for: "Tell me about adapting to a new environment" or "cross-functional collaboration."

When Benevity acquired Alaya in 2021, I went from a five-person team I'd built from scratch to an engineering org roughly ten times the size, a different primary language, Java instead of Node, and a microservices architecture I hadn't designed. My task was to fold Alaya's capabilities into that platform while keeping up the product velocity we'd had before, without becoming the person who shows up and tries to fix everything on day two. I spent the first month just mapping the existing architecture and talking to the platform and infra teams before I proposed a single change, which felt slow at the time but built the trust I needed later. From there I helped migrate the core Alaya features into Benevity's platform, contributed to three major cross-team architecture decisions, and became the liaison other teams came to for the Europe-facing product surface. I did move too fast in a couple of early conversations, trying to fix things that weren't actually broken in the new org, just unfamiliar to me. Slowing down and listening first is the thing I'd tell my earlier self to do from week one, not month two.
