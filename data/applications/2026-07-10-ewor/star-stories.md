# STAR Stories: EWOR (AI Co-Founder / CTO Fellowship)

## Building Alaya from zero to acquisition

In 2016 I co-founded Alaya, a purpose and impact platform for employee engagement, and the starting point was as blank as it gets: no customers, no product, just a plan. My job was to architect and build the whole thing from scratch, alone at first, while the business side was already out signing its first enterprise clients, so there wasn't much room to overthink the stack. I picked Node.js, React and PostgreSQL because they let me move fast without locking us into something we'd regret later, got the core features built in about three months so we had something to demo, and then expanded iteratively from there: multi-tenant architecture, an API layer, impact reporting, postal campaign tooling. Once we had traction I hired and onboarded our first three engineers and put in place CI/CD and a real code review culture, things that are easy to skip early and painful to retrofit. By the time Benevity, a Canadian company now valued north of a billion dollars, acquired us in 2021, we had fifty-plus enterprise clients across Switzerland and France running on that platform, which felt like the clearest validation the early technical and product calls were the right ones. If I did it again I'd invest in observability much sooner, we bolted on proper APM late, and I'd design for multi-tenancy from day zero instead of retrofitting it once it already hurt.

## A hard architecture call under investor pressure

A couple of years in, Alaya's monolith started showing real scaling cracks, and the team was split between a full microservices rewrite and a more modest modular monolith, right as investors were pushing hard for new features rather than infrastructure work. I needed a decision that held up technically without stalling the roadmap everyone upstream cared about. So I ran a two-week technical spike comparing both approaches with actual load tests, then took the trade-offs to the CTO and investors as numbers rather than opinions, since opinions are what start these debates in the first place. Out of that came a strangler fig plan: extract the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest of the monolith alone, and define clear criteria for when to peel off more. The first extracted module shipped in six weeks and cut latency by about 40% on our busiest endpoints, and the remaining monolith stayed stable straight through the acquisition, no big-bang rewrite required. The hardest part honestly wasn't the engineering, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the disciplined choice rather than the lazy one. Data got us there faster than arguing would have, and next time I'd push for that early benchmarking sooner just to cut the debate short.

## Integrating into Benevity without losing momentum

When Benevity acquired Alaya in 2021, I landed on a team roughly ten times the size of what I was used to, running a different codebase in Java and microservices, with different culture and different process entirely. The task was to fold Alaya's capabilities into Benevity's platform while still shipping new features, without letting the product velocity we'd built up quietly die in the transition. I spent the first month just mapping the existing architecture before I proposed anything, and made a point of building relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing any technical opinions their way. From there I helped drive adoption of AI-augmented workflows, things like Copilot and shared prompt patterns, across the wider engineering org, and ended up as the informal architecture liaison for the Europe-facing side of the product. We got the core Alaya features migrated cleanly, I contributed to three major cross-team architecture calls, and became the person people went to for AI tooling questions across two teams. Looking back, I moved too fast early on trying to fix things that genuinely weren't broken in the new org, and slowing down to listen first is what actually made me effective there.

## Getting people to adopt something with no authority to mandate it

At Benevity I was a senior IC with no direct reports, but I still needed two teams that didn't report to me to change how they worked day to day and start using AI tooling seriously. Since I couldn't just tell anyone to do anything, I started small, with three engineers who were already curious, and built internal demos that showed real time saved rather than making abstract claims about productivity. From there it was informal lunch-and-learn sessions with no mandate attached, just demonstrations, plus a shared repo of reusable prompt templates and documented wins, like the time we shaved a couple hours off a single PR review cycle. Within about three months, something like 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted review and generation regularly, and I'd become the de facto AI tooling lead for that part of the org. It was slower than a top-down rollout would have been, but it stuck, because people who adopt something on their own terms end up advocating for it themselves, which isn't something a decree from above can really produce.
