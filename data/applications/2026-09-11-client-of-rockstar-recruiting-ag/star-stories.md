# STAR Stories: Applied AI Founding Engineer (Agentic Workflows)

Prepared for the Rockstar Recruiting AG client role. Picked for founding-engineer scope, agentic/AI tooling relevance, and ownership under ambiguity.

---

## 1. Building Alaya from a blank page to acquisition

This is the story I'd lead with for a founding engineer conversation, because it's the closest thing I have to actually doing the job before. Back in 2016 I co-founded Alaya, a purpose and impact platform for employee engagement, and there was nothing to inherit: no customers, no codebase, no team. My job was to architect and build the whole thing while the business was out signing its first enterprise clients, which meant the platform had to exist before I'd fully decided what it should look like long term. I picked a pragmatic stack, Node.js, React, PostgreSQL, that let us move fast without betting on infrastructure we didn't need yet, and got core features to a first demo within about three months. From there it was constant expansion: multi-tenant architecture, an API layer, impact reporting, and eventually hiring and onboarding the first three engineers while setting up CI/CD and a review culture that didn't exist before I wrote it down. By the time Benevity, a Canadian unicorn valued north of a billion dollars, acquired us in 2021, we had 50+ enterprise clients across Switzerland and France running on infrastructure I'd designed from nothing. If I did it again, I'd invest in observability much earlier, since we didn't add proper APM until it was already overdue, and I'd design for multi-tenancy from day zero instead of retrofitting it once it started hurting.

## 2. Deciding against a full rewrite under investor pressure

At Alaya, the monolith started showing real scaling cracks, and the team was split between a full microservices rewrite and something more incremental, right as investors were pushing hard for new features instead of infrastructure work. I was the one who had to make a call that would hold up technically and be defensible to people who weren't engineers. I ran a two-week spike comparing both approaches with actual load tests rather than opinions, then took the results to the CTO and investors with numbers on the table. What we landed on was a strangler fig migration: pull out the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest of the monolith alone, and set clear criteria for when to extract the next piece. The first extracted module shipped in six weeks and cut latency on our busiest endpoints by around 40%, and the remaining monolith stayed stable all the way through the acquisition, so we never needed the big rewrite everyone was afraid of. Looking back, the hard part wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the disciplined choice. I'd push for the benchmarking earlier next time, since that data ended up doing most of the persuading anyway.

## 3. Driving AI-assisted workflow adoption with no formal mandate

After the Benevity acquisition I was a Senior Staff IC with no direct reports, but I wanted two teams that didn't report to me to actually change how they worked and start using AI tooling in their day-to-day development. Rather than pushing a mandate down from above, which wasn't mine to give anyway, I started with three engineers who were already curious and built small internal demos that showed concrete time savings instead of abstract promises. I ran informal lunch-and-learn sessions, nothing mandatory, and put together reusable prompt templates that I committed to a shared repo so people could just take them rather than start from scratch. I also documented real outcomes as they happened, like a specific case where a PR review cycle dropped by about two hours once AI-assisted review was part of it. Within three months, something like 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted code review and generation as part of their normal flow, and I'd become the person people came to for AI tooling questions without ever having that in a title. The tradeoff was speed: the bottom-up approach took longer to reach critical mass than a top-down rollout would have, but the engineers who adopted it on their own terms ended up advocating for it themselves, which a mandate never would have produced.

## 4. Integrating into a much larger, unfamiliar codebase after acquisition

When Alaya was acquired, I went from a team I'd built from scratch to a Benevity engineering org roughly ten times the size, working in Java and microservices instead of the Node.js monolith I knew inside out. The task wasn't just learning a new codebase, it was continuing to ship features for Alaya's product while doing it, without the acquisition stalling the velocity we'd built up over five years. I spent the first month almost entirely reading, mapping the existing architecture and sitting in on platform and infra team discussions before I proposed anything, which felt slow given how used to moving fast I was. That patience paid off: by the time I did push technical opinions, I had enough context and enough trust with the platform team that the changes landed instead of getting relitigated. We successfully migrated Alaya's core capabilities into Benevity's platform, and I ended up contributing to three major cross-team architecture decisions and becoming an architecture liaison for the Europe-facing product surface. My honest reflection is that I moved too fast in the first few weeks trying to fix things that weren't actually broken in the new org, just unfamiliar, and slowing down to listen first is what actually made the difference.

## 5. Choosing a pragmatic tech stack under real constraints

Early at Alaya, before there was a team to debate with, I had to choose the technical foundation for a platform that didn't exist yet, knowing that whatever I picked would either compound in our favor or become the thing I'd be apologizing for two years later. The constraint was real: limited runway, no infrastructure team, and a need to get to a first paying client demo fast. I chose Node.js and React specifically because they let one person move across the whole stack without context-switching between languages, and PostgreSQL because I wanted relational guarantees I could reason about without building custom tooling around a more exotic database. That choice let me hit the first demo in about three months and kept onboarding simple once I started hiring, since new engineers could be productive in days rather than weeks. The platform scaled on that same foundation all the way to 50+ enterprise clients and the eventual acquisition, without a rewrite of the core stack. If anything, I'd apply the same instinct to an agentic systems build now: pick boring, well-understood infrastructure for the parts that need to be reliable, and save the experimentation budget for the actual agent behavior, which is where the real uncertainty lives.
