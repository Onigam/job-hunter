# STAR Stories: Gradle Technologies, Founding Backend Engineer

## Building Alaya from a blank page

Back in 2016 I co-founded Alaya, an employee purpose and impact platform, and there was nothing to start from: no product, no customers, no codebase. My job was to architect and build the whole thing, alone at first and then with a small team, while the business side was already out signing enterprise clients who expected something working. I picked a pragmatic stack, Node.js, React, PostgreSQL, that could scale without forcing me into infrastructure work before there was a product worth scaling. I got a working demo out in the first three months, then kept expanding it piece by piece: multi-tenant architecture, an API layer, impact reporting, postal campaigns for clients who wanted physical mail alongside digital. Once we had traction I hired the first three engineers and set up the boring but necessary stuff, CI/CD, code review norms, some actual architecture documentation, so the thing wouldn't collapse under its own growth. By the time Benevity acquired us in 2021 we had over fifty enterprise clients across Switzerland and France running on that platform. Looking back, I'd invest in observability much earlier than we did; we bolted on proper APM late and paid for that gap more than once. I also underestimated how expensive it would be to retrofit multi-tenancy after the fact. If I built something like this again, that decision would happen on day zero, not eighteen months in.

## Choosing a strangler fig migration over a rewrite

At Alaya, our monolith started showing real scaling cracks as traffic grew, and the team split into two camps: rewrite everything as microservices, or keep it as a modular monolith and fix the hot spots. Investors wanted new features shipped, not months spent on an invisible rewrite, so I couldn't just pick the technically cleaner option and hope everyone came along. I ran a two week spike comparing both approaches with actual load tests rather than opinions, then took the results to the CTO and investors with numbers instead of a slide full of architecture diagrams. What I proposed was a strangler fig migration: extract the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest of the monolith alone, and define clear criteria for when something else earned extraction. We shipped the first extracted module in six weeks, performance improved around 40% on the busiest endpoints, and the remaining monolith stayed stable all the way through the acquisition. No big-bang rewrite ever happened, which in hindsight was the right call given how much risk that would have added at exactly the wrong time. The hardest part wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was actually the disciplined choice. Data made that argument for me. Next time I'd push for earlier benchmarking so that debate doesn't eat as many weeks as it did.

## Driving AI tooling adoption with no formal authority

After joining Benevity as a Senior Staff IC, I had no direct reports but I wanted two teams that didn't report to me to actually change how they worked, specifically by folding AI-assisted development into their daily workflow. Mandating that from a role with no management authority wasn't an option, so I started small: three engineers who were already curious signed on as early adopters. I built internal demos that showed concrete time saved rather than abstract promises, ran informal lunch and learn sessions with no mandate attached, and put together reusable prompt templates that I committed to a shared repo anyone could pull from. I documented wins as they happened, things like shaving two hours off a specific PR review cycle, because a story with a number in it travels further than a slide about productivity gains. Within about three months, something like 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted review and generation as part of their normal process, and I ended up as the informal lead for AI tooling adoption across the org. It was slower than a top-down rollout would have been, but it stuck in a way a decree wouldn't have. Engineers who opted in on their own terms were the ones who defended the practice to their peers later, which isn't something you can order into existence.

## Integrating Alaya into Benevity without losing momentum

When Benevity acquired Alaya in 2021, I went from a five-person engineering team to an org roughly ten times the size, with a different codebase, Java and microservices instead of our Node monolith, and a different set of processes I hadn't had a hand in building. My task was to get Alaya's capabilities folded into Benevity's platform while still shipping new features, without the acquisition turning into a year of standing still. I spent the first month just mapping their existing architecture before I proposed a single change, and I built relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing any technical opinions their way. From there I drove adoption of AI-augmented workflows across the wider engineering org and became the architecture liaison for the Europe-facing product surface, which put me in rooms where Alaya's context actually mattered. We migrated the core Alaya features into Benevity's platform successfully, I contributed to three major cross-team architecture decisions, and I became the person people pulled in for AI tooling questions across two teams. Honestly, I moved too fast early on trying to fix things in the new org that weren't actually broken, just unfamiliar. Slowing down and listening first is what actually made me effective there, and it's the instinct I'd bring into any role that starts with "founding" at a company that already has fifteen years of working product behind it.
