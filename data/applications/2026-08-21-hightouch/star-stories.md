# STAR Stories — Hightouch, Staff Engineer AI Productivity

## 1. Driving AI-assisted development adoption without formal authority

At Benevity I was a Senior Staff IC with no direct reports, but I wanted two teams that didn't report to me to actually change how they worked day to day. The obvious move would've been asking a manager to mandate it, and I knew that would produce compliance, not adoption. Instead I found three engineers who were already curious about AI-assisted coding and started there. I built small internal demos that showed concrete time saved on real tasks, not hypothetical ones, and ran informal lunch-and-learn sessions with no mandate attached, just people showing other people what worked. I put together reusable prompt templates and committed them to a shared repo so nobody had to start from a blank prompt, and I documented specific wins, like the time a PR review cycle dropped by about two hours, so the case for trying it was concrete rather than aspirational. Within three months, roughly 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted code review and generation regularly, and I'd become the person people pinged when something in that workflow broke or looked off. What I'd do differently is start measuring adoption more formally from week one; I had good qualitative signal but had to reconstruct some of the quantitative picture after the fact. This is close to the core of the role Hightouch is describing, the model matters less than whether the documentation, context, and social proof around it actually get engineers to trust and use the tooling.

## 2. Building Alaya's platform from a blank page to acquisition

In 2016 I co-founded Alaya, an employee purpose and engagement platform, with nothing built yet and no customers signed. My job was to architect and build the whole thing largely alone at first, while the business side was already out signing enterprise clients who'd expect something working. I picked a pragmatic stack, Node.js, React, PostgreSQL, that wouldn't force an early rewrite but also wouldn't trap us in unnecessary complexity before we had traction. The first three months went into getting a real demo in front of prospects, and from there it was iterative: multi-tenant architecture, an API layer, impact reporting, campaign tooling. As the team grew I hired and onboarded the first three engineers and set up the basics that keep a codebase sane past the first year, CI/CD, code review norms, and enough architecture documentation that new hires weren't guessing. The platform ended up serving fifty-plus enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity, a Canadian unicorn valued north of a billion dollars, acquired the company in 2021. If I were doing it again I'd invest in observability much earlier, we only got proper APM in place later than we should have, and I'd design for multi-tenancy from day zero instead of retrofitting it once it started to hurt.

## 3. Choosing a strangler-fig migration over a full rewrite under investor pressure

Alaya's monolith was starting to strain under load, and the team was split between a full microservices rewrite and something more incremental, while investors made it pretty clear they wanted new features, not months of internal plumbing work. I ran a two-week technical spike comparing both paths with actual prototype load tests instead of arguing from intuition, then presented the trade-offs to the CTO and investors with numbers rather than opinions. The plan we landed on was a strangler-fig approach: extract the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest of the monolith alone, and define clear criteria for when the next module would be worth pulling out. The first extracted module shipped in six weeks and cut latency on our busiest endpoints by around 40%. The rest of the monolith stayed stable straight through the acquisition, so we never had to justify a big-bang rewrite that would have eaten a quarter or more. The hard part honestly wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the disciplined choice. Data closed that argument faster than debate did, and next time I'd push for early benchmarking sooner just to shorten that whole conversation.

## 4. Integrating into a much larger engineering org after acquisition

When Benevity acquired Alaya in 2021, I went from a small team I'd shaped from scratch to a Senior Staff Engineer role on a team roughly ten times the size, running Java microservices instead of the Node stack I knew, with processes I hadn't built and didn't fully understand yet. My instinct early on was to start proposing changes to things that looked inefficient, and that instinct was wrong. I spent the first month mostly mapping the existing architecture and building relationships with the platform and infra teams before I pushed any technical opinion at all. Once I'd earned enough context and trust, I became a cross-team architecture liaison for the Europe-facing product surface and contributed to three major cross-team architecture decisions, while also driving the AI tooling adoption work that became its own thread across two teams. Looking back, I moved too fast in the first few weeks trying to fix things that weren't actually broken, just unfamiliar. Slowing down and listening first turned out to be the thing that made the rest of the integration work, and it's a pattern I now apply by default whenever I join something with existing structure I didn't build.
