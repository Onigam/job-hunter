# STAR Stories: VP Engineering, Daedalean AI

Prepared for the VP Engineering conversation. Daedalean builds certifiable AI avionics and just merged into Destinus, a much larger hardware and systems company, so the stories below lean toward multi-domain integration, ambiguity, and leading without a mandate rather than pure IC depth.

## Building Alaya from zero to acquisition

I co-founded Alaya in 2016 with no customers and no product, just a belief that companies needed a better way to run employee purpose and impact programs. My job was to architect and build the whole platform, alone at first and then with a small team, while the business was already signing its first enterprise clients, so there wasn't much room to get the foundations wrong. I picked a pragmatic stack, Node.js, React, and Postgres, that wouldn't force a rewrite the moment we hit real scale, and got a working demo out in about three months. From there we expanded into multi-tenancy, an API layer, and impact reporting, and I hired and onboarded the first three engineers, setting up the CI/CD and review habits that let the team grow without slowing down. We ended up with over fifty enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity, a Canadian unicorn, acquired the company in 2021, which told me the early technical bets had held up. If I did it again I'd bring in observability much earlier; we bolted on proper APM late, and I'd design for multi-tenancy from day one instead of retrofitting it once it started to hurt.

## Integrating into a much larger org after acquisition

When Benevity acquired Alaya, I joined a team roughly ten times the size of what I'd known, with a different codebase in Java and microservices, a different culture, and different processes than the ones I'd built from scratch. My task was to get Alaya's capabilities folded into Benevity's platform without losing the product speed that had made the acquisition worth doing in the first place. I spent my first month just mapping their architecture before proposing anything, and I made a point of building relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing technical opinions on them. That patience paid off: I ended up driving the adoption of AI-assisted development workflows across the org and became the architecture liaison for the Europe-facing product surface, contributing to three major cross-team decisions along the way. What I'd change is the pace I moved at in the first few weeks. I tried to fix things that weren't actually broken in the new org, and slowing down to listen first is what made the rest of it work.

## A hard architecture call under investor pressure

At Alaya, our monolith was starting to crack under load right as investors were pushing hard for new features, not a rewrite. I needed a decision that balanced long-term scalability against short-term delivery, so I ran a two-week technical spike comparing a full microservices rewrite against a modular monolith, complete with prototype load tests. I brought the CTO and investors real numbers instead of opinions, and proposed a strangler-fig migration: pull out the two highest-traffic modules first and leave the rest of the monolith alone, with clear criteria for when to extract more. The first module shipped in six weeks and performance on our busiest endpoints improved roughly 40%, and the rest of the system stayed stable straight through the acquisition without ever needing the big rewrite. The hard part wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the right call, and the data is what did that. Next time I'd front-load even more of the benchmarking so that debate doesn't eat as much calendar time.

## Driving change across teams that didn't report to me

As a Senior Staff IC at Benevity, I had no direct reports but needed two teams to adopt AI-assisted development practices, which meant I couldn't just tell anyone what to do. I started with three engineers who were already curious, built small internal demos that showed concrete time savings, and ran informal lunch-and-learn sessions with no mandate behind them at all. I also put together reusable prompt templates in a shared repo and wrote up specific results, like a case where a PR review cycle went from a couple hours to a fraction of that. Within three months, about 70% of engineers on both teams were using AI-assisted review and generation on their own, and I'd become the informal AI tooling lead across the org without anyone appointing me to it. It was slower than a top-down rollout would have been, but it stuck, because people who adopt something on their own terms end up advocating for it themselves in a way a decree never produces.

## Why this maps to Daedalean and Destinus

These stories share a pattern that seems relevant here: building structure inside real ambiguity, integrating orgs with different cultures and tech stacks after an acquisition, and getting people to move in the same direction without leaning on authority. Daedalean's engineering org is about to do a version of what I went through at Benevity, except spanning software, ML, and hardware domains that speak even less of a common language than Java and Node did. I'd rather talk through how that integration actually goes than promise it's simple, because it wasn't for me either.
