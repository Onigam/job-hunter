# STAR Stories, Purpose:Labs (Co-Founder / CTO)

Picked for a cofounder-level, product x AI conversation: building from zero, technical judgment under investor pressure, driving adoption without authority, and the purpose-platform parallel to Alaya.

---

## 1. Building Alaya from a blank page

In 2016 I cofounded Alaya, a purpose and engagement platform for employees, and there was nothing to start from, no product, no customers, no team. My job was to architect and build the whole thing while the business side was already out signing enterprise clients who expected something to demo soon. I picked Node, React and Postgres, mostly because it let me move without getting stuck in infrastructure decisions I couldn't undo later, and I had a working demo inside three months. From there it was constant expansion: a multi-tenant layer, an API surface partners could build on, impact reporting that finance teams actually trusted. I hired the first three engineers myself and set up code review and architecture docs before we had enough people to need them formally. We ended up with over 50 enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity, a Canadian unicorn, acquired us in 2021. Looking back, I'd have added real observability much earlier; we didn't get proper APM until problems started costing us actual debugging time, and multi-tenancy retrofitted after the fact is far more painful than designing for it from day one.

## 2. Choosing not to rewrite the monolith

A couple of years into Alaya, our monolith started showing real scaling cracks, and the team split into two camps: full microservices rewrite, or keep it as one modular codebase. Investors made it clear they wanted new features, not months spent rebuilding something that technically still worked. I ran a two-week spike comparing both paths with actual load tests instead of opinions, then brought the CTO and investors numbers rather than a slide deck. What I proposed was a strangler-fig approach: pull out the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest alone, and set clear criteria for when anything else would get extracted. The first module shipped in six weeks and cut latency on our busiest endpoints by around 40%. The remaining monolith held up fine all the way through the acquisition, so we never touched the rest. The hard part wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that doing less was the right call, and having the benchmark numbers upfront would have cut that argument short much faster.

## 3. Getting two teams to adopt AI tooling with no mandate

After Benevity, I was a senior staff engineer with no direct reports on either team I wanted to change, and the goal was getting AI-assisted development into their daily workflow without anyone reporting to me or asking for it. I started with three engineers who were already curious, ran informal lunch sessions instead of any kind of rollout plan, and built a shared repo of prompt templates people could just take and use. I wrote up concrete wins as they happened, one story about saving two hours on a single PR review cycle did more than any pitch deck would have. Within three months, about 70% of both teams were using it regularly, and I'd become the person people asked about AI tooling without that ever being an assigned role. It was slower than a top-down rollout would have been, but engineers who picked it up on their own terms ended up selling it to each other, which a mandate never would have produced.

## 4. Learning to slow down after the acquisition

Joining Benevity meant landing on a team roughly ten times the size of Alaya, with a different codebase in Java and microservices, and different everything else. My task was to fold Alaya's capabilities into their platform without losing the speed we'd built our reputation on. I spent the first month just mapping their existing architecture before I proposed anything, and built relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing any technical opinion their way. That patience paid off: I ended up contributing to three major cross-team architecture decisions and became the informal liaison for the Europe-facing product surface. My first instinct, though, had been to move fast and start fixing things that weren't actually broken, just unfamiliar, and it took a few pointed conversations to realize that. Slowing down and listening first made the eventual changes land better than speed ever would have.

## 5. Deciding what to build first with almost no information

At Alaya, before any of the architecture work mattered, there was the harder problem of deciding what the platform should even be, based on a handful of conversations with early enterprise prospects who each wanted something slightly different. I treated the first three months as a bet: build the smallest version that let one real client run a full campaign end to end, then use what broke to decide what came next, rather than trying to spec the whole product upfront. That approach is close to how I'd think about a first build for a life and career navigation engine, where the model of a person's state is going to be wrong in interesting ways until real usage tells you where. The demo we shipped wasn't complete, but it was concrete enough for a client to commit, and that mattered more than completeness would have. If I did it again, I'd instrument that first version more heavily from day one, since the gap between what I assumed people needed and what they actually used was bigger than I expected.
