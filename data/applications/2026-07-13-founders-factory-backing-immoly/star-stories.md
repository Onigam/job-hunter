# STAR Stories, Founders Factory / Immoly CTO & Co-Founder

Picked for this one because it's a founder-level role: full-stack ownership from a working prototype, agentic AI that does real operational work rather than a demo, and a tight working relationship with an existing founder instead of a big team with process already in place.

## 1. Building Alaya from zero to acquisition (full-stack ownership, ambiguity, cofounder mode)

In 2016 I co-founded Alaya with a blank page, no customers, no product, nothing to inherit. My job was to architect and build the whole platform, alone at first, while the business was already signing its first enterprise clients, so there wasn't room to spend months on a design nobody could use yet. I picked a pragmatic stack, Node, React, Postgres, that could scale without over-engineering for a size we didn't have, and got the core features to a first demo in three months. From there it was iterative: multi-tenant architecture, an API layer, impact reporting, and eventually hiring and onboarding the first three engineers while setting up CI/CD and a review culture. The platform reached production with fifty-plus enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity acquired the company in 2021, which felt like proof that the early calls were right when there was nobody around to check them against. If I did it again I'd bring in real observability much sooner; we bolted on proper APM late, and retrofitting multi-tenancy cost more than designing for it from day zero would have.

## 2. The strangler-fig call instead of a rewrite (shipping over rebuilding, judgment under pressure)

At Alaya our monolith started cracking under load right as investors wanted new features, not a quarter spent on architecture. The team was split between a full microservices rewrite and patching what we had, and my task was finding a path that didn't sacrifice either side of that argument. I ran a two-week spike with actual load tests instead of opinions, then took the numbers to the CTO and investors rather than a slide deck of preferences. What I proposed was narrower than either camp wanted: extract only the two highest-traffic modules, leave the rest monolithic, and set clear criteria for extracting more later. The first module shipped in six weeks, the busiest endpoints got roughly 40% faster, and the rest of the monolith held steady all the way through the acquisition. No big-bang rewrite ever happened. Convincing people that restraint was the harder, better choice took longer than the engineering did, and next time I'd front-load the benchmarking even more so the debate doesn't eat the runway.

## 3. Driving AI-tooling adoption without a mandate (agentic AI, honestly framed)

At Benevity I had no authority over two teams I needed to change how they worked, specifically getting them to use AI-assisted development instead of doing everything by hand. Rather than push a policy nobody asked for, I started with three curious engineers, built demos that showed real time saved on real tickets, and ran lunch-and-learns nobody was required to attend. I put together reusable prompt templates and committed them to a shared repo so the habit could spread without me pushing it. Within three months about 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted review and generation on their own. I'll be direct about where this sits relative to Immoly: this is tooling adoption and hands-on LLM integration work, not a customer-facing agent executing property-management operations end to end. It's the closest thing in my background to that muscle, and it's honest to say the gap between the two is real.

## 4. Mapping before proposing at a much larger company (working with an existing founder, trust before change)

After Benevity acquired Alaya, I joined as Senior Staff Engineer on a team roughly ten times the size, a different codebase in Java plus microservices, and processes nobody had asked my opinion on. My task was folding Alaya's capabilities into their platform without losing the speed we'd built, while working with people who had their own established way of doing things and no reason yet to trust mine. I spent the first month just mapping the existing architecture before proposing a single change, and built relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing any technical opinion at all. That patience paid off, I ended up contributing to three major cross-team architecture decisions and became the liaison for the Europe-facing product surface. Early on I moved faster than the relationship could support, trying to fix things that weren't actually broken from where they sat, and slowing down to listen first is what turned that around. Coming into Immoly alongside an existing founder, that's the same instinct I'd lead with, understand why the prototype looks the way it does before I start changing it.
