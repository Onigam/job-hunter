# STAR Stories: Co-founder & CTO, Stealth AI Infrastructure for Physical Commodity Trading

Prepared for interviews with the founder and Merantix Capital. Picked for a founder-level, zero-to-one, messy-real-data role where I'd be working directly across from a trading desk.

---

## Story 1: Building Alaya from zero to acquisition

In 2016 I cofounded Alaya, an employee purpose and impact platform, with a blank page, no customers, and no product. My job was to architect and build the whole thing alone at first, then grow a team, while the business was already signing its first enterprise clients, so there wasn't much room to get the foundations wrong. I picked a pragmatic stack, Node.js, React, PostgreSQL, that could scale without over-engineering for a scale we didn't have yet, and pushed to get a working demo in the first three months. From there I expanded the platform iteratively: multi-tenant architecture, an API layer, impact reporting, postal campaigns, and hired and onboarded the first three engineers, setting up CI/CD and a code review culture as we went. By the time Benevity, a Canadian unicorn valued north of a billion dollars, acquired us in 2021, the platform was running in production for more than fifty enterprise clients across Switzerland and France. Looking back, I'd have invested in observability much earlier, since we bolted on proper APM late, and I underestimated how expensive it would be to retrofit multi-tenancy rather than design for it from day zero. That's the lesson I'd carry into a venture like this one, where the data is going to be messy and the architecture decisions made in month one will still be load-bearing years later.

## Story 2: Making a hard architecture call under investor pressure

A few years into Alaya, our monolith started showing real cracks under enterprise load, and the team was split between a full microservices rewrite and staying the course. Investors wanted new features, not a rewrite, so I couldn't just make an engineering call in a vacuum. I ran a two-week technical spike comparing both approaches with actual prototype load tests, then presented the trade-offs to the CTO and investors using those numbers instead of opinions. Rather than pitch a big-bang rewrite, I proposed a strangler-fig migration: pull out the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest of the monolith alone, and define clear criteria for when to extract more. We shipped the first extracted module in six weeks, performance improved by about 40% on the busiest endpoints, and the remaining monolith stayed stable all the way through the acquisition. The hard part wasn't the technical design, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the right call, and having the load-test data made that conversation shorter. If I did it again I'd run the early benchmarking even sooner, before the debate got emotional.

## Story 3: Leading adoption without formal authority

At Benevity I was a Senior Staff IC with no direct reports, but I wanted to get two teams that didn't report to me using AI-assisted development practices. Nobody had given me a mandate to change how people worked, so I started small: three curious engineers, some internal tooling demos that showed concrete time saved, and informal lunch-and-learn sessions with no pressure attached. I built reusable prompt templates, committed them to a shared repo, and documented results as they came in, things like a specific PR review cycle that dropped by two hours. Within three months, about 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted review and generation, and I'd become the informal AI tooling lead for the org without ever having the title. It was slower than a top-down rollout would have been, but stickier, because the people who adopted it on their own terms ended up advocating for it themselves. That's roughly the situation I'd expect building alongside a trading desk: you don't get authority by default, you earn it by shipping something a domain expert actually wants to keep using.

## Story 4: Integrating into a much bigger, unfamiliar org

When Benevity acquired Alaya in 2021, I joined a team about ten times the size of what I'd been running, with a different codebase, Java and microservices instead of our Node.js monolith, and different processes altogether. My task was to fold Alaya's capabilities into Benevity's platform while still shipping new features, without losing the speed we'd built our reputation on. I spent the first month just mapping the existing architecture before proposing anything, and made a point of building relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing technical opinions on them. From there I helped drive AI-augmented workflows across the org and became a cross-team architecture liaison for the Europe-facing product surface. We migrated the core Alaya features successfully and I ended up contributing to three major cross-team architecture decisions. I moved too fast early on, trying to fix things in the new org that weren't actually broken, and slowing down to listen first is what made me effective after that. Joining a founder's existing trading operation would mean the same discipline: understand what's already working before touching it.
