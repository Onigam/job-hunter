# STAR Stories: Hatchet, Founding Engineer

Picked for a small, fast-moving founding team building a distributed task orchestration platform. These lean on the parts of my background that map to owning infrastructure end to end, making hard calls on a small team, and picking up unfamiliar stacks under pressure.

## 1. Building Alaya from zero to acquisition

In 2016 I co-founded Alaya with a blank page, no customers, and no product. My job was to architect and build the entire platform alone at first, then grow a small engineering team, while the business closed its first enterprise clients in parallel. I chose a pragmatic stack that could scale without over-engineering for a scale we didn't have yet, and got the core features to a working demo inside three months. From there I expanded iteratively into multi-tenant architecture, an API layer, and reporting, and once we had headcount I hired and onboarded the first three engineers and set up CI/CD and a code review culture from scratch. The platform ended up running 50-plus enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity, a Canadian unicorn, acquired the company in 2021, which is about as direct a validation of those early technical calls as you can get. If I did it again I'd invest in proper observability much earlier. We bolted on APM late, and the retrofit cost more time than building it in from day one would have.

## 2. Making a hard architecture call under investor pressure

At Alaya, our monolith started showing scaling cracks right as investors were pushing hard for new features, not a rewrite. My task was to make a defensible call that balanced long-term scalability against short-term delivery pressure, without a full rewrite eating the runway. I ran a two-week spike comparing a microservices rewrite against a modular monolith, backed it with prototype load tests, and brought the CTO and investors numbers instead of opinions. We landed on a strangler-fig approach: extract the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest monolithic, and define clear criteria for when to pull out the next piece. The first extracted module shipped in six weeks and cut latency on the busiest endpoints by around 40%, and the remaining monolith stayed stable all the way through the acquisition with no big-bang rewrite ever needed. The hard part wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the right call. Data got me there faster than argument did, and next time I'd front-load even more of the benchmarking to shorten that debate.

## 3. Ramping on an unfamiliar codebase and culture fast

After Benevity acquired Alaya in 2021, I landed on a team roughly ten times the size of what I'd worked in, with a different codebase in Java and microservices and a different set of norms entirely. My task was to integrate Alaya's capabilities into that platform while still shipping new features, without losing the velocity we'd built as a four-person team. I spent the first month just mapping the existing architecture before proposing anything, and built relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing technical opinions on them. Once I had that footing, I drove adoption of AI-assisted workflows across the org and became the architecture liaison for the Europe-facing product surface. The migration landed cleanly, I contributed to three major cross-team architecture decisions, and became the go-to person for AI tooling across two teams. Early on I tried to fix things that weren't actually broken in the new org, just unfamiliar, and slowing down to listen first is what turned that around.

## 4. Driving adoption with no formal authority

As a senior staff IC at Benevity with no direct reports, I wanted two teams that didn't report to me to actually change how they worked and add AI-assisted development to their daily flow. Rather than push a mandate nobody asked for, I started with three curious engineers, built small internal demos that showed concrete time saved on real PRs, and ran informal lunch sessions with no attendance requirement. I wrote up results as they came in, things like a two-hour PR review cycle cut down concretely, and committed reusable prompt templates to a shared repo anyone could pull from. Within three months, about 70% of engineers across both teams were using the workflow on their own, and I became the informal lead on AI tooling for the org without ever having the title. It was slower than a top-down rollout would have been, but it stuck, because people who opted in on their own terms ended up advocating for it themselves. I wouldn't get that from a decree, no matter how well argued.

## 5. Picking up a new language stack under pressure

Early at Sagemcom, I moved from web-style development into GWT-based software architecture for telecom set-top boxes, a domain and toolchain I hadn't touched before, with hardware constraints that don't exist in typical web work. My task was to get productive fast enough to own architecture decisions, not just contribute code, on a team shipping to real hardware on a schedule. I spent the first couple of weeks reading the existing codebase end to end and pairing with the two engineers who knew the constraints best, rather than guessing at conventions from a manual. That grounding let me start making real architecture calls within the first month instead of waiting until I felt fully comfortable, which realistically would have taken much longer. The team shipped on schedule and I stayed in that architect role for the rest of my time there. The lesson that's stuck with me since is that reading real code beats reading documentation when you're trying to get productive in a new stack fast, which is exactly the position I'd be in picking up Go at Hatchet.
