# STAR Stories: Clicks (YC F25), Software Engineer

Picked for a small, fast-moving agentic infrastructure team: building from zero, owning backend systems in production, navigating ambiguity, and driving change without formal authority.

## 1. Building Alaya from zero to acquisition

In 2016 I co-founded Alaya, a purpose and impact platform for employee engagement, with a blank page and no customers. I had to architect and build the whole thing myself first, then grow a team, while the business was closing its first enterprise clients in parallel, so there wasn't room to overbuild or wait for a clean spec. I picked Node.js, React, and PostgreSQL because we needed to ship fast without boxing ourselves in, got the core product to a first demo within three months, and then kept expanding: multi-tenant architecture, an API layer, impact reporting, postal campaigns. Along the way I hired and onboarded our first three engineers and set up CI/CD and a code review culture from scratch. We got to 50+ enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity, a Canadian company valued north of a billion, acquired us in 2021, which felt like the clearest validation the early technical bets had been right. If I did it again I'd invest in observability much earlier, we didn't have real APM until things were already painful, and I'd design for multi-tenancy from day one instead of retrofitting it later.

## 2. A hard architecture call under investor pressure

At Alaya, our monolith started showing scaling cracks right as investors were pushing hard for new features, not a rewrite. The team was split between a full microservices rewrite and staying the course, and neither answer felt honest without data. I ran a two-week spike comparing both approaches with actual prototype load tests, then walked the CTO and investors through the trade-offs using those numbers instead of opinions. What I proposed was a strangler-fig migration: pull out the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest of the monolith alone, and set explicit criteria for when to extract the next piece. The first extracted module shipped in six weeks and cut latency by around 40% on our busiest endpoints, and the rest of the monolith stayed stable straight through the acquisition, no big-bang rewrite required. The hard part wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the disciplined choice. Next time I'd front-load even more of the benchmarking, since the data ended up doing most of the persuading anyway.

## 3. Post-acquisition integration at Benevity

Joining Benevity after the acquisition meant landing on a team ten times the size of Alaya's, a different stack (Java plus microservices instead of our Node setup), and a culture I hadn't earned any trust in yet. My instinct early on was to start fixing things that struck me as wrong, and that backfired, people don't want the acquired engineer immediately second-guessing decisions they made with context I didn't have. So I spent the first month just mapping the existing architecture and building relationships with the platform and infra teams before I proposed anything. From there I became a cross-team architecture liaison for the Europe-facing product surface and contributed to three major architecture decisions, while also pushing AI-assisted engineering practices into the broader org. The integration of Alaya's core features into Benevity's platform went through without losing the velocity we'd had as a small team. What I'd do differently is trust that instinct to slow down from day one rather than learning it the hard way in the first few weeks.

## 4. Driving adoption with no formal authority

At Benevity I was a senior IC with no direct reports, but I wanted two teams that didn't report to me to actually change how they worked and bring AI tooling into their daily workflow. A mandate from above would have been faster on paper but I doubted it would stick, so instead I started with three engineers who were already curious, built small internal demos that showed real time saved rather than a slide deck, and ran informal lunch sessions with no requirement to attend. I put together reusable prompt templates and committed them to a shared repo so the good patterns didn't live only in my head. Within about three months, roughly 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted review and generation regularly, and I'd become the informal go-to person for that tooling across the org. It was slower than a top-down rollout would have been, but the engineers who opted in ended up advocating for it themselves internally, which a mandate could never have produced.

## 5. Working inside someone else's codebase and constraints

A recurring thread across Benevity and the earlier work at État de Genève and SQLI was walking into systems I didn't design, under compliance or process constraints I didn't set, and still needing to ship. At État de Genève that meant public-sector applications for the education system, where the deployment process and data-handling rules were rigid for good reasons and any shortcut would have caused real problems downstream. I learned to separate what was actually a hard constraint from what was just how things had always been done, and to ask the second kind of question directly instead of assuming it was fixed. That habit is part of why the Benevity integration went as smoothly as it did, I already had practice reading unfamiliar systems for their real limits before touching anything. Looking back, the biggest lesson was that asking a "why is it done this way" question early saves far more time than guessing and being wrong twice.
