# STAR Stories: Lobby (Founding Senior Engineer, Full Stack / Product)

Picked for this role: owning a product vertical end to end, pragmatic stack choices under startup pressure, shipping without waiting for a perfect spec, and influencing engineers who don't report to you. Four stories below, adapted from the master bank.

## 1. Building Alaya from zero (full-stack ownership, ambiguity)

In 2016 I co-founded Alaya with a blank page: no product, no customers, no code. My job was to architect and build the whole platform while the business was out signing its first enterprise clients, so there wasn't much room to wait for a spec to settle. I picked a pragmatic stack, Node.js, React, Postgres, that could scale without forcing us into premature complexity, and got the core features to a first demo inside three months. From there it was iterative: multi-tenant architecture, an API layer, impact reporting, and eventually hiring the first three engineers and putting in place CI/CD and a real code review culture. We got to 50-plus enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity, a Canadian company valued north of a billion dollars, acquired us in 2021. If I did it again I'd design for multi-tenancy from day zero instead of retrofitting it, and I'd get observability in place a lot earlier than we did. Owning a slice from database schema to the customer-facing screen isn't new territory for me, it's how I built my first company.

## 2. A hard architecture call under investor pressure (ship over perfect)

At Alaya, our monolith started showing real scaling cracks right as investors were pushing hard for new features, not a rewrite. The team was split on a full microservices rewrite versus staying modular, and I needed a decision that would hold up technically and survive a room full of people who wanted velocity, not architecture debates. I ran a two-week spike with prototype load tests on both approaches and brought the CTO and investors numbers instead of opinions. What I proposed was a strangler-fig migration: pull out the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest of the monolith alone, and set clear criteria for when to extract more. The first module shipped in six weeks and performance improved about 40% on our busiest endpoints, no big-bang rewrite required, and the remaining monolith stayed stable all the way through the acquisition. The hard part wasn't the technical work, it was convincing a room that "don't rewrite everything" was the disciplined answer. That's the same judgment call a founding engineer has to make weekly: know when to polish and when to ship the rough version because a customer is waiting.

## 3. Driving AI tooling adoption without a mandate (influence, product intuition)

At Benevity I was a senior staff IC with no direct reports, but I wanted two teams that didn't report to me to actually adopt AI-assisted development instead of dabbling with it. Ordering people to use a tool doesn't work, so I started with three curious early adopters, built small demos with concrete before-and-after time savings, and ran informal lunch sessions with no mandate attached. What made it stick was turning it into something closer to a repeatable practice than a trend: reusable prompt templates committed to a shared repo, and documented outcomes rather than "this feels faster" anecdotes. Within three months, about 70% of engineers across both teams were using the workflows I'd built, and I ended up the informal AI tooling lead for the org. It was slower than a top-down rollout would have been, but it stuck because people adopted it on their own terms and then defended it to their peers. Getting people to trust a new tool by showing them the result, not by decree, is the same skill needed to get non-technical hotel or travel staff comfortable handing decisions to an agent.

## 4. Integrating into a much bigger, unfamiliar system (working fast in someone else's codebase)

When Benevity acquired Alaya in 2021, I went from a five-person team to an org ten times the size, a different codebase in Java and microservices, and processes I didn't know yet. My task was to integrate Alaya's capabilities into Benevity's platform without losing the delivery speed we'd had as a startup. I spent the first month just mapping the existing architecture before proposing anything, and built relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing technical opinions on them. That patience is what let me become a cross-team architecture liaison for the Europe-facing product surface and contribute to three major architecture decisions afterward. Early on I moved too fast trying to fix things that weren't actually broken in the new org, and slowing down to listen first is what made the rest of the integration work. Wiring a new system into decades-old legacy travel infrastructure is the same problem in a different shape: understand the existing constraints before you touch anything load-bearing.
