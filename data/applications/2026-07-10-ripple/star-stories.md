# STAR Stories, Ripple, Staff Software Engineer, Infrastructure

Prepared for the infrastructure/reliability angle of this role: scaling under load, cross-team trust with platform teams, and technical decisions made without a safety net.

---

## 1. Building Alaya's platform from zero (founding infra, ambiguity)

When we started Alaya in 2016 there was nothing yet, no customers, no product, just a purpose and impact platform we needed to get in front of enterprise buyers fast. I was the only engineer for the first stretch, so I had to pick a stack that could actually carry the company rather than one that looked good in a demo. I went with Node.js, React, and PostgreSQL, deliberately avoiding anything exotic, and got a working demo out in about three months while the business was already closing its first enterprise deals in parallel. From there I kept expanding the surface: multi-tenant architecture so client data stayed properly isolated, an API layer, impact reporting, and eventually hired and onboarded our first three engineers, setting up CI/CD and a review culture along the way. By the time Benevity acquired us in 2021, we were running 50-plus enterprise clients across Switzerland and France on infrastructure that held up through the acquisition process itself. If I did it again I'd invest in observability much earlier; we added proper APM later than we should have, and multi-tenancy is far cheaper to design in from day zero than to retrofit once clients depend on it.

## 2. A strangler-fig migration instead of a rewrite (architecture under pressure)

The Alaya monolith started showing real scaling cracks right around the time investors wanted new features, not a rewrite. The team was split between a full microservices rewrite and something more conservative, and I needed a decision that wouldn't just be my opinion against the CTO's. I ran a two-week spike with load tests comparing both approaches, then presented the numbers rather than arguing from instinct. What I proposed was a strangler-fig migration: pull out only the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest of the monolith alone, and define clear criteria for when the next module was worth extracting. The first extraction shipped in six weeks and improved performance 40% on our busiest endpoints, and the remaining monolith stayed stable all the way through the acquisition, so we never needed the big-bang rewrite people were worried about. The hard part wasn't the technical call, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the disciplined choice. Data closed that debate faster than any argument I could have made, and next time I'd run the benchmarking earlier to shorten the back-and-forth.

## 3. Mapping the terrain before touching anything at Benevity (cross-team trust)

Joining Benevity after the acquisition put me on a team roughly ten times the size of Alaya, with a different codebase, Java and microservices instead of what I knew, and different processes entirely. My instinct was to start fixing things I found unfamiliar, but I held off and spent the first month just mapping their existing architecture before I proposed a single change. That patience is what let me build real relationships with the platform and infra teams before I pushed any technical opinions on them, and it's also how I ended up becoming a cross-team architecture liaison for the Europe-facing product surface, contributing to three major cross-team decisions afterward. Looking back, I moved a little too fast in my first few weeks trying to "fix" things that weren't actually broken in the new org, just unfamiliar to me. Slowing down and listening first turned out to matter more than being right quickly, which is a lesson that applies just as much to infrastructure decisions as to product ones.

## 4. Driving AI-tooling adoption with no formal authority (influence without a mandate)

At Benevity I was a Senior Staff IC with no direct reports, but I wanted two teams that didn't report to me to actually change how they worked, specifically by adding AI-assisted development into their daily practice. Rather than pushing a mandate, I started with three engineers who were already curious, built small internal demos that showed concrete time savings, and ran informal lunch-and-learn sessions with nobody required to attend. I created reusable prompt templates, committed them to a shared repo, and documented wins as they happened, things like saving two hours on a single PR review cycle. Within three months, 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted review and generation on their own, and I became the informal AI tooling lead for the org without anyone appointing me to that role. It was slower than a top-down rollout would have been, but it stuck, because the engineers who adopted it did so on their own terms and then advocated for it themselves. I'd use that same mandate-free approach again for infrastructure changes like observability or GitOps adoption, where forcing compliance tends to produce compliance theater rather than real habits.

## 5. Deciding what not to build (technical debt vs. delivery pressure)

This ties back to the same monolith moment at Alaya, but from the angle of resisting pressure rather than making the architecture call itself. Investors wanted visible feature progress, and a rewrite would have stalled that for months, so the task wasn't really technical, it was holding a defensible position under business pressure that favored the flashier option. I used the same two-week spike and load-test data to make the case, framing the strangler-fig approach as the option that let feature work continue in parallel rather than stopping it. That framing mattered as much as the data itself, because it reframed "not rewriting" as the choice that protected velocity instead of the cautious, slower one. The migration held, performance improved, and the business kept shipping features the whole time. What I'd change is starting the benchmarking conversation earlier, before the debate calcified into two camps, since data introduced late has to overcome positions people have already committed to publicly.
