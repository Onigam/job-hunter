# STAR Stories: House of Charts (Founding Engineer)

Prepared for interview prep. Prose form, first person, so they read naturally out loud rather than as a checklist. Picked for the founding-engineer angle: greenfield ownership, technical trade-offs under pressure, and driving AI tooling adoption without a mandate, since that last one lines up directly with the generative and extractive AI work in their posting.

---

## 1. Building a platform from zero (founding-engineer / greenfield ownership)

Back in 2016 I co-founded Alaya, an employee purpose and impact platform, and we started with literally nothing: no customers, no code, no product. My job was to architect and build the whole thing, first alone, then with a small team, while the business was already signing its first enterprise clients, so there wasn't much room to get the foundation wrong. I picked a pragmatic stack, Node.js, React, PostgreSQL, that could grow without forcing us into premature complexity, and pushed to get core features in front of a customer within three months so we'd have real feedback instead of guesses. From there we layered in multi-tenancy, an API layer, and reporting features as the client base actually needed them, and I hired and onboarded our first three engineers along the way, setting up CI/CD and a code review culture before it became urgent. The platform ended up running production workloads for fifty-plus enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity, a Canadian unicorn, acquired us in 2021, which felt like the clearest validation we could have gotten for those early calls. If I did it again I'd invest in observability much earlier, we bolted on proper APM late and paid for it in debugging time, and I'd design multi-tenancy into the schema from day one instead of retrofitting it once it started to hurt. That last lesson matters for a small medical-data team especially, where isolation and auditability aren't things you can bolt on after the fact.

## 2. A hard architecture trade-off under delivery pressure

At Alaya our monolith started showing real scaling cracks, and the team was split between a full microservices rewrite and something more modest. Investors wanted new features, not a rewrite, so I had to find a decision that was actually defensible rather than just the one that felt technically cleanest. I ran a two-week spike comparing both approaches with prototype load tests, then walked the CTO and investors through the numbers instead of opinions, and proposed a strangler-fig migration: pull out the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest alone, and set clear criteria for when we'd extract the next piece. The first extracted module shipped in six weeks, performance on the busiest endpoints improved by roughly 40%, and the remaining monolith stayed stable all the way through the acquisition without ever needing the big-bang rewrite people were originally arguing for. The hardest part honestly wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the disciplined choice. Data made that argument for me faster than I could have on my own, and next time I'd front-load even more of that benchmarking to cut the debate short earlier. In a four-person founding team, that kind of decision gets made in a day, not a quarter, so I'm used to compressing the process without skipping the rigor.

## 3. Driving AI tooling adoption without formal authority

After Benevity acquired Alaya, I joined as a Senior Staff IC on a team about ten times larger than what I was used to, with no direct reports, but I wanted to get AI-assisted development practices adopted across two teams that had no reason to listen to me by default. Rather than pushing a mandate, I started with three curious engineers, built small internal demos that showed concrete time saved on things like PR review cycles, and ran informal lunch-and-learn sessions with no requirement to attend. I put together reusable prompt templates and committed them to a shared repo so the tooling could spread on its own rather than through me personally. Within three months, about 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted review and generation regularly, and I ended up becoming the informal go-to person for AI tooling org-wide. It was slower than a top-down rollout would have been, but it stuck, because people who adopted it on their own terms ended up advocating for it to their peers in a way I never could have manufactured. That same instinct, working with generative models hands-on rather than just reading about them, is what I'd bring to building the extraction and summarization pipeline itself, not just the culture around it.

## 4. Integrating into an existing system without breaking what worked

When Benevity acquired Alaya, I moved from a codebase and culture I'd built myself into a much larger org with a different stack, mostly Java microservices, and different processes altogether. My task was to fold Alaya's capabilities into their platform while still shipping new features, without losing the product velocity that had gotten us acquired in the first place. I spent the first month just mapping their existing architecture before proposing a single change, and made a point of building relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing any technical opinions on them. That groundwork let me contribute to three major cross-team architecture decisions and become a de facto architecture liaison for the Europe-facing product surface. Looking back, I moved too fast early on trying to fix things in the new org that genuinely weren't broken, just unfamiliar, and slowing down to listen first is what actually made the integration work. It's a habit I'd carry into a hospital-facing product too, where the existing clinical workflow deserves the same respect the Java codebase did.
