# STAR+R Stories: Magino Marveaux-Cochet
_Prepared for: Founding Engineer @ Metorial_

---

## 1. Building Alaya from a blank page (founding engineer, ambiguity, ownership)

In 2016 I co-founded Alaya, an employee purpose and impact platform, with no product and no customers yet, just a signed enterprise client waiting on something that didn't exist. My task was to architect and build the whole thing, alone at first, while the business kept selling before there was anything to show. I picked a stack I could move fast in without painting myself into a corner, Node.js, React, Postgres, and got a working demo in front of that first client inside three months. From there I built out multi-tenancy, an API layer, and impact reporting as the client list grew, and hired and onboarded the first three engineers along the way, setting up CI and review habits before the team got big enough that skipping them would've hurt. By the time Benevity acquired us in 2021, we had somewhere around 50 enterprise clients across Switzerland and France running on that platform, which is about as direct a validation of the early technical calls as you can get. If I did it again I'd invest in observability much earlier, we didn't have real APM until fairly late, and I underestimated how expensive it would be to retrofit multi-tenancy instead of designing for it from day one. That's the same bet a team of four is making right now: the infrastructure decisions you skip early because there's no time tend to be the ones you pay for at scale.

## 2. Choosing a strangler-fig migration over a rewrite (infra judgment under pressure)

At Alaya, our monolith started showing real scaling cracks right as investors were pushing hard for new features, not a rewrite. My task was to make a defensible call between a full microservices rewrite and something more modest, with the business breathing down the timeline. I ran a two-week spike prototyping both approaches with actual load tests instead of arguing from intuition, then presented the trade-offs to the CTO and investors with numbers, not opinions. From there I proposed and led a strangler-fig approach: pull out the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest of the monolith alone, and define clear criteria for when the next module was worth extracting. The first extracted module shipped in six weeks and cut latency on our busiest endpoints by around 40%, and the rest of the monolith stayed stable straight through the acquisition, no big-bang rewrite required. The hard part wasn't the engineering, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the right call, and having the benchmark data ready made that conversation much shorter than it would've been on gut feel alone. Distributed infrastructure at an early-stage company is full of these calls, where the disciplined incremental path is less exciting to pitch than the clean rewrite but is the one that actually survives contact with real traffic.

## 3. Mapping the terrain before changing it (working inside someone else's system, cross-team trust)

When Benevity acquired Alaya in 2021, I landed on a team roughly ten times the size of what I'd known, with a different codebase, Java and microservices instead of what we'd built, and a different review culture. My task was to get Alaya's capabilities integrated into Benevity's platform without losing the product speed we'd had as a five-person team. Rather than showing up with opinions, I spent the first month just mapping the existing architecture, reading code, sitting in on reviews, asking questions before proposing anything. Once I had that picture I started building relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing any technical changes, and later became the person other teams pulled in for AI-assisted workflow adoption across the org. The integration went through, I ended up contributing to three major cross-team architecture decisions, and became a go-to person for AI tooling across two engineering teams I didn't formally belong to. Looking back, I moved too fast early on trying to fix things that genuinely weren't broken, just different, and slowing down to listen first is what actually made me effective. Joining a four-person team means the opposite problem in scale but the same instinct applies: understand why something was built the way it was before you propose replacing it.

## 4. Getting engineers to adopt AI tooling without a mandate (influence without authority)

At Benevity I was a senior IC with no direct reports, but I wanted two teams that didn't report to me to actually change how they worked, specifically to bring AI tooling into their day-to-day development. My task was to drive that adoption without a management mandate to lean on. I started small, three curious engineers, and built internal demos that showed concrete time saved rather than talking about the idea in the abstract. From there I ran informal lunch sessions, no requirement to attend, just a demo and real examples, and put together reusable prompt templates in a shared repo so people could copy something that already worked instead of starting from a blank prompt. I also wrote up specific wins, like a PR review cycle that dropped by about two hours, because a concrete story travels further than a slide about productivity gains. Within three months, around 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted review and generation, and I'd become the informal lead on AI tooling for the org without ever being asked to take that on. The adoption was slower than a top-down rollout would have been, but it stuck, because people who opted in on their own terms ended up advocating for it to their peers. That same pattern, using AI-assisted tools to write better code faster without lowering the bar, is exactly what a founding team of four needs from everyone on it, not just the person with the idea.

## 5. Making a feature survive real production data (technical depth, DevOps discipline)

At Benevity, teams increasingly came to me when an AI-assisted feature worked fine in a demo but needed to hold up against actual production data, real inputs, real edge cases, not the clean sample set it was built against. My task on more than one of these was to take something that worked in a notebook and get it to a state where it wouldn't quietly fail on the messier inputs a real user would throw at it. The action was less glamorous than the initial prototyping: instrumenting the pipeline to see where things actually broke, tightening the logic against the failure cases instead of the happy path, and pushing back when a proposed feature was elegant but fragile in favor of a narrower version that held up under load. The result was AI-assisted workflows that teams kept using after the initial demo excitement wore off, which is a much higher bar than a good launch. My reflection is that the gap between "works in the demo" and "survives concurrent sessions with real OAuth flows and real usage patterns" is where most infrastructure work actually happens, and it's the part that's hardest to show off in an interview but the part that decides whether an integration holds up in production.
