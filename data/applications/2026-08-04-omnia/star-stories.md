# STAR Stories: Omnia, Co-Founder & CTO

Picked for a founding CTO conversation: why I can build a platform from nothing, how I make architecture calls under pressure, how I drive an AI-heavy roadmap without a team already in place, and how I've handled joining an org that isn't a blank page anymore. Source stories in `profile/star-bank.md`.

---

## 1. Building Alaya from zero to acquisition

This is the one I'd lead with, since it's the closest match to what Omnia needs right now: a technical co-founder who can take a roadmap and an existing business co-founder and turn it into a real product. Back in 2016 I co-founded Alaya, a purpose and impact platform for employee engagement, starting from a completely blank page with no customers and no product. My job was to architect and build the whole thing myself at first, then bring on a team, while the business side was already signing enterprise clients who expected something to actually exist. I picked a stack that could scale without being over-engineered for a company that didn't have product-market fit yet, Node.js, React, PostgreSQL, and pushed to get core features in front of people within three months so we'd have something to demo. From there I kept expanding: multi-tenant architecture, an API layer, reporting, campaign tooling, and eventually hired and onboarded the first three engineers, setting up CI/CD and a code review culture along the way. By the time Benevity acquired us in 2021, we had over 50 enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, which felt like a pretty direct validation that the early technical bets held up. If I did it again, I'd invest in observability much earlier, we added proper monitoring later than we should have, and I'd design for multi-tenancy from day zero instead of retrofitting it once it got painful.

## 2. Making a hard architecture call under pressure

Omnia's founding stage means someone's going to have to make an unpopular technical call at some point without perfect data, and this is the story that shows how I do that. At Alaya, our monolith started showing real scaling cracks, and the team was split between a full microservices rewrite and a more conservative modular monolith, while investors were pushing hard for new features rather than a rewrite. I ran a two-week technical spike comparing both approaches with actual prototype load tests instead of just opinions, then took the trade-offs to the CTO and investors with concrete numbers. What I proposed was a strangler-fig migration: pull out the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest of the monolith alone, and set clear criteria for when we'd extract the next piece. The first extracted module shipped in six weeks and performance improved about 40% on the busiest endpoints, and the rest of the monolith stayed stable all the way through the acquisition, no big-bang rewrite required. The hard part honestly wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the right call; having the load-test data made that conversation much shorter than it would have been otherwise.

## 3. Driving AI adoption without formal authority

Given Omnia's whole thesis runs through an AI layer, this one speaks to how I get a team to actually use new AI tooling rather than just mandate it. At Benevity I was a Senior Staff IC with no direct reports, but I wanted to get two teams that didn't report to me to adopt AI-assisted development practices. Instead of asking for a mandate, I started with three curious engineers, built internal demos that showed concrete time savings, and ran informal lunch-and-learn sessions with no obligation attached. I put together reusable prompt templates, committed them to a shared repo, and documented specific wins, one story was saving about two hours on a single PR review cycle. Within three months, around 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted review and generation, and I ended up as the informal AI tooling lead for the org. It was slower than a top-down rollout would have been, but it stuck better, because the engineers who picked it up did so on their own terms and then went and sold it to their teammates themselves, which I couldn't have manufactured with a policy memo.

## 4. Navigating an org that already exists

Omnia already has a co-founder and presumably some early process in place, so this is less about a blank page and more about how I show up somewhere that already has its own shape. After Benevity acquired Alaya in 2021, I joined a team about ten times the size of Alaya, with a different codebase, Java and microservices instead of our Node stack, and a different culture entirely. Rather than showing up with opinions, I spent the first month mapping the existing architecture before proposing anything, and built relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing on technical direction. From there I helped migrate core Alaya capabilities into Benevity's platform, contributed to three major cross-team architecture decisions, and became an informal architecture liaison for the Europe-facing product surface. My first instinct was actually to move too fast and start "fixing" things that weren't broken in the new org, and slowing down to listen first is what made the rest of it work.
