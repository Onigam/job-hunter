# STAR Stories, Hostaway Staff Engineer (React/Node.js, Accounting)

Picked for this role because it's greenfield, financial-system stakes, cross-functional (Finance, Legal, Product), and expects mentoring plus early architectural ownership.

---

## 1. Building Alaya from zero (greenfield ownership, ambiguity)

In 2016 I co-founded Alaya with a blank page. No customers, no product, no existing codebase to lean on, which is close to what a trust accounting subsystem built from scratch feels like. My job was to architect and build the whole platform while the business was already signing its first enterprise clients, so there wasn't room to spend six months on a perfect design before anything shipped. I picked a pragmatic stack, Node.js, React, PostgreSQL, that could scale without over-engineering for a scale we didn't have yet, and got the core features to a first demo in three months. From there I expanded iteratively: multi-tenant architecture, an API layer, impact reporting, and eventually hired and onboarded the first three engineers, setting up CI/CD and a code review culture as I went. The platform reached production with fifty-plus enterprise clients across Switzerland and France and was acquired by Benevity in 2021, which felt like validation of decisions made when there was nobody to validate them against. If I did it again I'd invest in observability much earlier; we bolted on proper APM late, and retrofitting multi-tenancy cost more than designing for it from day zero would have.

## 2. The strangler-fig migration (architecture under pressure, auditable systems)

At Alaya our monolith was starting to crack under load, and the team was split between a full microservices rewrite and patching the modular monolith we had. Investors wanted new features, not a rewrite, so my task was finding a technical path that didn't sacrifice either. I ran a two-week spike comparing both approaches with actual prototype load tests instead of opinions, then presented the trade-offs to the CTO and investors with numbers attached. What I proposed was a strangler-fig migration: extract only the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest monolithic, and define clear criteria for when to extract more. We shipped the first extracted module in six weeks and performance on the busiest endpoints improved by roughly 40%. The rest of the monolith stayed stable all the way through the acquisition, so no big-bang rewrite was ever needed. Convincing people that not rewriting everything was the right call was the hardest part, oddly enough; data got us there faster than argument did, and next time I'd front-load the benchmarking even more to shorten that debate.

## 3. Mapping before proposing at Benevity (cross-functional collaboration, joining a larger org)

After the acquisition I joined Benevity as Senior Staff Engineer on a team roughly ten times the size of Alaya, with a different codebase in Java plus microservices and different processes entirely. My task was integrating Alaya's capabilities into their platform without losing the product velocity we'd built, while working across teams that didn't report to me and had their own established ways of doing things. I spent the first month just mapping the existing architecture before proposing a single change, and built relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing any technical opinions. That patience paid off: I contributed to three major cross-team architecture decisions and became the liaison for the Europe-facing product surface. Early on I moved too fast trying to fix things that weren't actually broken in the new org, and slowing down to listen first is what made the rest of it work. That's the same instinct I'd bring to working with Finance and Legal on an accounting system, understand the existing constraints before redesigning around them.

## 4. Driving AI-tooling adoption without authority (mentoring, influence)

At Benevity I had no direct reports but needed two teams outside my chain to change how they worked, specifically adopting AI-assisted development practices. Rather than push a mandate nobody asked for, I started with three curious engineers, built internal demos showing concrete time savings, and ran informal lunch-and-learn sessions with no requirement to attend. I created reusable prompt templates and committed them to a shared repo so the habit could spread on its own. Within three months about 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted review and generation, and I ended up the informal lead for AI tooling across the org. It was slower than a top-down rollout would have been, but stickier, since engineers who opted in on their own terms were the ones advocating for it to their peers. That's roughly how I'd approach mentoring on a new team here too: show, don't mandate.

## 5. Reasoning about compliance-adjacent constraints (reflection on judgment under uncertainty)

One thing I'd add, since this role touches trust accounting specifically: at Alaya, impact reporting for enterprise clients had its own version of an audit trail requirement, clients needed to prove exactly how funds and impact data moved through the system, which meant every schema decision had compliance consequences I couldn't undo later without a migration headache. I learned to treat those data models differently from ordinary product features, reviewing them with more scrutiny and documenting the reasoning behind field-level decisions so a future audit or a new engineer could reconstruct why something was built a certain way. I didn't always get this right early on, and the cost of fixing an under-specified data model after the fact is real, which is part of why I'd rather over-invest in the ledger design up front on something like this than optimize for shipping the UI first.
