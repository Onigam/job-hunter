# STAR+R Stories: Head of Engineering, Frida

Picked for a first Head of Engineering hire at an early-stage consumer AI product: founding-from-zero, technical judgment under pressure, leading without formal authority, and adapting to a much bigger org after an acquisition.

---

## Building Alaya From a Blank Page

In 2016 I co-founded Alaya, a purpose and impact platform for employee engagement, with no customers, no product, and no team. My job was to architect and build the entire platform from scratch, alone at first, while the business was already signing its first enterprise clients, so there was no room for a long runway before something real shipped. I picked a pragmatic stack, Node.js, React, PostgreSQL, that could scale without over-engineering for a company that didn't exist yet, got the core features to a first demo within three months, and then expanded iteratively into multi-tenant architecture, an API layer, and impact reporting as clients signed on. Once the product had traction I hired and onboarded the first three engineers and set up CI/CD, code review culture, and architecture docs, none of which existed until I built them. The platform reached production with more than fifty enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity, a Canadian unicorn valued north of a billion dollars, acquired the company in 2021, which is about as direct a validation of those early technical calls as you can get. Looking back, I'd invest in observability much earlier, we added proper APM late, and I underestimated the cost of retrofitting multi-tenancy; next time I'd design for it from day zero instead of bolting it on once it hurt.

## The Strangler-Fig Call Nobody Wanted to Make

At Alaya, our monolith started showing real scaling cracks right as the team was split on whether to do a full microservices rewrite or stay with a modular monolith, and investors were pushing hard for new features rather than an internal rewrite. I needed a decision that could survive scrutiny from both engineers and investors, not just an opinion, so I ran a two-week technical spike comparing both approaches with real prototype load tests before proposing anything. I brought the CTO and investors concrete metrics instead of a pitch, then proposed and led a strangler-fig migration, extracting the two highest-traffic modules first while leaving the rest of the monolith alone, with clear criteria for when to pull the next piece out. The first extracted module shipped in six weeks and cut latency on the busiest endpoints by roughly 40 percent, and the remaining monolith stayed stable all the way through the acquisition, no big-bang rewrite required. The hardest part, honestly, was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the right call; the data did most of that work for me, and if I did it again I'd front-load even more benchmarking to shorten the debate.

## Getting AI Tooling Adopted Without a Mandate

After Benevity acquired Alaya in 2021, I joined as a Senior Staff IC on a team roughly ten times the size I was used to, with no direct reports and no authority to tell anyone how to work, but I wanted to push AI-assisted development practices into two teams that didn't answer to me. Rather than ask for a mandate I started with three curious engineers, built small internal demos that showed concrete time savings on real pull requests, and ran informal lunch-and-learn sessions with no obligation attached. I wrote up results as they came in, one engineer saved close to two hours on a single review cycle, and committed reusable prompt templates to a shared repo so the habit could spread on its own. Within three months about 70 percent of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted review and generation day to day, and I ended up the informal go-to person for AI tooling across the org without ever having the title. It was slower than a top-down rollout would have been, but it stuck, because people who adopt something on their own terms end up defending it to their peers, which a decree never gets you.

## Earning Trust Fast in Someone Else's Codebase

Joining Benevity after the acquisition meant walking into a codebase and culture I had no history with, Java microservices instead of the stack I'd built at Alaya, and a real risk of showing up as the founder who thinks he knows best. I spent the first month doing nothing but mapping the existing architecture and talking to the platform and infra teams before proposing a single change, which felt slow given how much I wanted to fix on day one. That patience paid off once I did start proposing things, because I'd already built relationships instead of walking in with opinions nobody asked for, and I ended up as the cross-team architecture liaison for the Europe-facing product surface and contributed to three major cross-team architecture decisions. My first instinct had been to move fast and fix things that, it turned out, weren't actually broken in the new org, they just looked different from what I was used to; slowing down and listening first is the version of this story I'd tell anyone joining a much bigger team than the one they came from.
