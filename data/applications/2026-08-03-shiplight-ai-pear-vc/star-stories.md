# STAR Stories: Shiplight AI, Founding Engineer

_Prepared for: Founding Engineer, AI, Shiplight AI (Pear VC / PearX). Picked for relevance to founding-stage ambiguity, quality-vs-velocity trade-offs, AI tooling adoption, and customer-facing iteration._

### Building Alaya from a blank page to acquisition

Back in 2016 I co-founded Alaya with nothing, no product, no customers, just a bet that companies would pay for a way to measure employee purpose and impact. I was the only engineer for the first few months, so I picked a stack I could move fast in without painting myself into a corner later, Node.js, React, Postgres, nothing exotic. I got a working demo in front of the first prospective clients inside three months, which is what let the founders start closing deals while I kept building. From there it was a constant back and forth between shipping what sales had just promised and building the multi-tenant architecture and reporting layer underneath it so the next ten clients wouldn't break the first ten. We grew to fifty-plus enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity, a Canadian unicorn, acquired us in 2021. Looking back, I underestimated how expensive it would be to retrofit multi-tenancy after the fact, and I'd have put real observability in place a lot earlier instead of debugging production issues half-blind. That's probably the single lesson I carry into any new zero-to-one build now.

### Choosing a strangler-fig migration over a full rewrite

At Alaya, our monolith started cracking under load right as investors were pushing hard for new features, not a rewrite nobody outside engineering cared about. The team was split between a full microservices rewrite and something more conservative, and I didn't think either side had enough data to be right yet. I ran a two-week spike, load-tested both approaches against real traffic patterns, and brought the CTO and investors numbers instead of opinions. What we agreed on was a strangler-fig approach, pull out the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest of the monolith alone, and set clear criteria for when to peel off the next piece. The first extracted module shipped in six weeks and cut response time on our busiest endpoints by around 40 percent, and the remaining monolith stayed stable straight through the acquisition, no big-bang rewrite required. The hard part wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the disciplined choice. If I did it again I'd run the benchmarks even earlier, just to shorten the debate.

### Getting two teams to adopt AI tooling without a mandate

After the Benevity acquisition I was a senior staff IC with no direct reports, but I wanted two engineering teams that didn't report to me to actually change how they worked day to day and start using AI-assisted development instead of treating it as a novelty. Rather than pushing a policy, I started with three engineers who were already curious, built small internal demos that showed real time saved on things like PR review, and ran informal lunch sessions with no mandate attached. I wrote up prompt templates and reasoning patterns that worked and put them in a shared repo so people could take what was useful and ignore the rest. Within three months about 70 percent of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted review or generation regularly, and I ended up being the person people checked with before rolling out new tooling org-wide. It was slower than just issuing a directive would have been, but it stuck, because people adopted it on their own terms rather than being told to. That distinction, between tooling people tolerate and tooling people actually reach for, is exactly the kind of judgment call I expect building agentic QA that developers have to trust.

### Learning a codebase and a culture before pushing my own opinions

When Benevity acquired Alaya, I went from a five-person team I'd built from scratch to one team among many inside a company roughly ten times our size, with a different codebase, mostly Java microservices, and a different way of making decisions. My instinct early on was to start "fixing" things that looked wrong to me, and that instinct was wrong. I spent the first month just mapping how the existing architecture actually worked and why, and building real relationships with the platform and infra teams before I proposed changing anything. That patience is what let me become a cross-team architecture liaison for the Europe-facing product surface and contribute to three major architecture decisions that actually stuck, rather than getting relitigated six months later. In hindsight, moving slower at the start was what made me effective faster overall, and it's the same instinct I'd bring to working with early Shiplight customers: understand their actual QA workflow before proposing how an agent should automate it.
