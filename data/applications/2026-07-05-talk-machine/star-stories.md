# STAR Stories: Talk Machine (Founding Engineer)

Picked for a tiny, product-obsessed team building a voice-first consumer product from near scratch. These lean on 0 to 1 ownership, fast iteration, and technical judgment made without a big org backing it up. Drawn from `profile/star-bank.md`, reframed for the founding-engineer scope.

## 1. Building Alaya from a blank page to acquisition

In 2016 I co-founded Alaya, a purpose and impact platform for employee engagement, and for the first few months my job title was basically whatever needed doing that day. There was no product, no customers, just a bet that enterprise clients would pay for something that didn't exist yet. I picked Node.js, React, and PostgreSQL because they let me move fast without over-building for scale we didn't have, and I had a working demo inside three months, which is what let the founders start closing early conversations. From there I kept expanding it myself for a while, multi-tenant architecture, an API layer, impact reporting, then the postal campaign features clients kept asking for once I brought on the first three engineers and set up CI/CD and code review so new hires wouldn't inherit a mess. We reached 50+ enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity, a Canadian unicorn, acquired the company in 2021. Looking back, I underestimated how expensive retrofitting multi-tenancy would be and added proper observability far too late; both are things I'd design in from day zero on a product like Talk Machine's, where the team is small enough that early debt doesn't stay hidden for long.

## 2. The strangler fig call nobody wanted to make

At Alaya, the monolith started cracking under load right when investors were pushing hardest for new features rather than a rewrite, and the team was split between a full microservices rewrite and just living with the pain. Neither option was defensible without data, so I ran a two-week spike, built prototypes of both, and put them under load tests instead of arguing from opinion. What came out wasn't either extreme: I proposed pulling out the two highest-traffic modules first and leaving the rest of the monolith alone, with clear criteria for extracting more later. The first extraction shipped in six weeks with roughly a 40% performance gain on the busiest endpoints, and the remaining monolith stayed stable all the way through the acquisition. The hard part wasn't the engineering, it was convincing people that doing less was the right call, and the numbers did most of that work for me. I'd try to get that benchmark data even earlier next time, since the debate cost more time than the migration did.

## 3. Driving AI adoption across teams with no mandate to do it

After the Benevity acquisition I had no direct reports and no formal mandate, just a conviction that AI-assisted development would change how the org worked, on two teams that had zero reason to listen to me. Instead of pitching it top down, I found three curious engineers, built small demos showing real time saved on actual PRs, and ran informal lunch sessions with nothing beyond "here's what this looks like." I wrote up the wins as they happened, a two-hour review cycle collapsing to twenty minutes was one, and put reusable prompt templates in a shared repo so people could just take them. Within three months about 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted review and generation daily, and I'd become the person colleagues asked when they wanted to get better at it. It was slower than a mandate would have been, but it stuck, because the people who adopted it did so on their own terms and defended it to their peers themselves rather than me having to.

## 4. Mapping the org before touching anything

Joining Benevity meant walking into a team ten times the size of Alaya, a different language, Java instead of Node, and a culture I didn't understand yet. My first instinct was to start fixing things that looked broken to me, and that instinct was wrong. I caught it early enough, spent the first month just mapping the existing architecture and talking to the platform and infra teams before proposing a single change, and built relationships before I built opinions. That patience is what let Alaya's capabilities get folded into the platform without a turf war, and it's why I ended up as the architecture liaison for the Europe-facing product surface. The lesson I keep coming back to is that moving fast on trust matters more than moving fast on code, especially the first ninety days somewhere new, and that applies just as much joining a seven-person team as a ten-times-bigger one, maybe more since there's nowhere to hide a bad first impression.
