# STAR Stories: Staff Site Reliability Engineer, SonarSource

Tailored for interview questions on reliability, incident response, automation, and cross-team influence without formal authority.

## Story 1: The observability gap I learned the hard way (Alaya, 2016-2021)

In 2016 I co-founded Alaya, a purpose and impact platform for employee engagement, starting from a completely blank page with no customers and no product. My job was to architect and build the whole thing from zero, first alone, then with a small team, while the business was closing its first enterprise clients. I picked a pragmatic stack, Node.js, React, PostgreSQL, that could scale without turning into a science project, and got a working demo in the first three months. From there we expanded into multi-tenant architecture, an API layer, and impact reporting, and I hired and onboarded the first three engineers, setting up CI/CD and a review culture as we went. The platform eventually ran in production for over fifty enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity, a Canadian unicorn, acquired the company in 2021, which was a pretty direct validation of the calls we made early on. What I'd change, and this is the part that's most relevant to an SRE role, is that we treated observability as a nice-to-have for too long. We didn't add proper APM until incidents were already costing us time to diagnose, and retrofitting monitoring into a system that was never designed to be observed is a lot more painful than building it in from month one. That's a lesson I now apply reflexively: instrument first, especially before scale hides the problem.

## Story 2: Landing in a 10x bigger, unfamiliar system (Benevity, 2021)

After the acquisition, I joined Benevity as a Senior Staff Engineer on a team roughly ten times the size of Alaya, with a different codebase, Java and microservices instead of what I'd built, and a different set of processes I hadn't shaped. My task was to integrate Alaya's capabilities into Benevity's platform without losing the product velocity we'd had as a small team. Rather than showing up with opinions, I spent the first month just mapping how the existing architecture actually worked, then built relationships with the platform and infra teams before proposing any changes. Once I had that context, I pushed AI-augmented workflows, things like structured Copilot usage and shared prompt patterns, across the wider engineering org, and became a de facto architecture liaison for the Europe-facing side of the product. The migration landed cleanly, I contributed to three cross-team architecture decisions in that first year, and I became the person people asked about AI tooling on two separate teams. Looking back, I moved too fast early on trying to fix things that weren't actually broken in the new org, they just looked unfamiliar. Slowing down and listening before touching anything made me considerably more effective once I did start changing things.

## Story 3: Choosing not to rewrite everything under pressure (Alaya)

At Alaya, our monolith started showing real scaling cracks, and the team was split between a full microservices rewrite and a more modest modular monolith approach, all while investors wanted new features, not an infrastructure project. I needed a decision that balanced long-term scalability against short-term delivery pressure, and one I could actually defend with data rather than instinct. I ran a two-week technical spike comparing both paths with prototype load tests, then presented the trade-offs to the CTO and investors using those numbers instead of opinions. What I proposed and led was a strangler-fig migration: extract the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest of the monolith alone, and define concrete criteria for when to extract anything further. The first extracted module shipped in six weeks, performance on the busiest endpoints improved by roughly 40%, and the remaining monolith stayed stable all the way through the acquisition, so we never needed the big-bang rewrite people were originally arguing for. The hardest part honestly wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that doing less was the right call. Having real benchmarks in hand cut that debate down a lot faster than argument alone would have.

## Story 4: Getting engineers to adopt something with no mandate behind it (Benevity)

As a Senior Staff IC at Benevity, with no direct reports, I wanted to get AI-assisted development practices adopted across two teams that had no reason to listen to me beyond whatever I could show them. So I started small, three engineers who were already curious, and built internal demos that showed concrete time saved rather than making any kind of pitch. From there it was informal lunch-and-learn sessions, no mandate attached, plus a shared repo of reusable prompt templates so people could just take what worked. I documented real outcomes as they came in, one story was saving about two hours on a single PR review cycle, and let that spread on its own. Within three months, around 70% of engineers across both teams had it built into how they worked day to day, and I ended up the informal lead for AI tooling across the org without anyone assigning me that role. It was slower than a top-down rollout would have been, but it stuck in a way a mandate never does, because the people who adopted it were the ones telling their peers it was worth doing, not me.
