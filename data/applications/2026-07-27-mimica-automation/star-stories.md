# STAR Stories: Mimica Automation, Head of/VP of Engineering

Selected for this role because it's a first Head of Engineering hire tasked with introducing process at a 50-to-100 engineer scale-up without killing ownership culture, coaching Engineering Managers, and working across eight cross-functional teams. These four cover: building structure from nothing, driving change without formal authority, making a defensible technical call under pressure, and operating inside a much bigger org after a fast scale-up.

## Building Alaya from zero to acquisition

This is the one I'd lead with, since it's the closest parallel to what Mimica is asking for: build the function from scratch, not inherit it. In 2016 I co-founded Alaya with a blank page, no customers, no product. I had to architect and build the whole platform, first alone, then with a small team, while the business was signing its first enterprise clients. I picked a pragmatic stack that could scale without over-engineering, got the core features to a first demo in three months, and then expanded iteratively into multi-tenant architecture and an API layer. Hiring the first three engineers meant also setting up CI/CD, code review culture, and architecture docs from day one, because there was no existing structure to lean on. We reached production with 50-plus enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity acquired the company in 2021, which I still read as the clearest validation of those early technical and process calls. If I did it again I'd invest in observability much earlier, we bolted on proper APM late, and I'd design for multi-tenancy from day zero instead of retrofitting it once it started to hurt.

## Leading without formal authority

This maps directly onto the coaching and cross-team parts of the Mimica role, since a Head of Engineering there will spend a lot of time influencing managers and teams that don't report up through them in the usual sense, at least not on day one. At Benevity I was a Senior Staff IC with no direct reports, but I needed two teams that didn't answer to me to actually change how they worked and start using AI-assisted development. I started small, three engineers who were already curious, and built internal demos that showed concrete time saved rather than making an argument on slides. Lunch-and-learn sessions with no mandate attached, reusable prompt templates committed to a shared repo, and documented wins, one story was saving about two hours on a single PR review cycle. Within three months roughly 70% of engineers across both teams had adopted it, and I ended up the informal AI tooling lead for the org. It was slower than a top-down rollout would have been, but it stuck, because people who opt in on their own terms end up defending the change to their peers in a way a mandate never produces.

## Making a hard architecture call under pressure

Relevant here because the JD explicitly asks for technical vision-setting and knowing how engineering decisions hit product and revenue, not just building process for its own sake. At Alaya our monolith was starting to crack under scale, and the team was split between a full microservices rewrite and a more modular approach, while investors were pushing hard for new features rather than a rewrite. I ran a two-week technical spike comparing both paths with actual load-test prototypes, then presented the trade-offs to the CTO and investors with numbers instead of opinions. What I proposed was a strangler-fig migration: pull out the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest monolithic, and set clear criteria for when to extract more later. The first module shipped in six weeks with about a 40% performance improvement on the busiest endpoints, and the rest of the monolith held up fine all the way through the acquisition. No big-bang rewrite, and the roadmap never stalled. The hard part honestly wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the right call; the data did most of that convincing, and next time I'd run the early benchmarks sooner just to shorten the debate.

## Post-acquisition integration into a much bigger org

Worth including because Mimica is asking someone to operate across eight cross-functional teams plus a dedicated platform team, which is a very different coordination problem than a ten-person startup. After Benevity acquired Alaya in 2021, I joined a team roughly ten times the size of what I'd been running, with a different codebase, in Java and microservices, and a different culture and process set entirely. Rather than showing up with opinions, I spent the first month just mapping the existing architecture and building relationships with the platform and infra teams before proposing anything. From there I became a cross-team architecture liaison for the Europe-facing product surface and contributed to three major cross-team architecture decisions, while also pushing AI-tooling adoption in parallel. The Alaya features migrated into Benevity's platform without losing the product velocity we'd built. My first instinct was to move fast and fix things that weren't actually broken in the new org, and slowing down to listen first is what made the rest of it work.
