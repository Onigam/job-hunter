# STAR Stories, Astoria AI (Founding Full-Stack Engineer)

Picked for a stealth-stage founding role: building from zero, making calls under ambiguity, and driving AI-tooling adoption without a mandate.

---

## Story 1: Building Alaya from a Blank Page to Acquisition

In 2016 I co-founded Alaya, an employee purpose and impact platform, with nothing built and no customers signed yet. The task was to architect and ship the entire platform myself first, then grow a team, while the business was closing its first enterprise deals in parallel. I picked a pragmatic stack, Node.js, React, PostgreSQL, that could scale without over-engineering for a company that didn't exist yet, and got the core product to a first demo inside three months. From there I expanded it iteratively: multi-tenant architecture, an API layer, impact reporting, postal campaigns, and along the way I hired and onboarded the first three engineers and set up CI/CD and a code review culture from scratch. The platform ended up serving 50-plus enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity, a Canadian company valued north of a billion, acquired us in 2021. Looking back, I'd have invested in observability much earlier, we bolted on proper APM late, and I underestimated what retrofitting multi-tenancy would cost; next time I'd design for it from day zero rather than adding it under load.

## Story 2: A Hard Architecture Call Under Investor Pressure

At Alaya, our monolith started showing scaling cracks right around the time investors were pushing hard for new features, not a rewrite. The team was split between a full microservices rewrite and a modular monolith, and I needed a decision that held up technically without stalling the roadmap. I ran a two-week spike comparing both approaches with actual load tests instead of opinions, then presented the trade-offs to the CTO and investors with numbers attached. What I proposed was a strangler-fig migration: extract the two highest-traffic modules first and leave the rest monolithic, with clear criteria for when to peel off the next piece. The first extracted module shipped in six weeks and performance on the busiest endpoints improved by roughly 40%, and the remaining monolith stayed stable all the way through the acquisition, no big-bang rewrite required. The hardest part honestly wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the right call, and having the benchmark data ready made that conversation much shorter than it would have been otherwise.

## Story 3: Driving AI Tooling Adoption With No Authority to Mandate It

After joining Benevity as a Senior Staff engineer with no direct reports, I wanted two teams that didn't report to me to actually change how they worked and bring AI tooling into their daily workflow. I started small, with three engineers who were already curious, and built internal demos that showed concrete time saved rather than pitching the idea in the abstract. From there it was informal lunch-and-learn sessions, no mandate attached, plus a shared repo of reusable prompt templates and a running log of results, things like a specific PR review that got two hours faster. Within three months, around 70% of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted review and generation on their own, and I ended up the informal go-to person for AI tooling across the org. It was slower than a top-down rollout would have been, but it stuck, since people who adopt something on their own terms end up defending it to their peers in a way a decree never produces.

## Story 4: Getting a New Culture Right After an Acquisition

Joining Benevity after the acquisition meant landing on a team roughly ten times the size of Alaya, with a different codebase in Java and microservices and a different set of processes than the ones I'd built myself. My task was to integrate what Alaya had built into Benevity's platform while still shipping new features, without losing the speed we'd had as a small team. I spent the first month just mapping the existing architecture before proposing anything, and made a point of building relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing technical opinions on them. That groundwork paid off: the core Alaya features made it into Benevity's platform, I contributed to three major cross-team architecture decisions, and became the architecture liaison for the Europe-facing product surface. I'll admit I moved too fast early on trying to fix things that weren't actually broken in the new org, and slowing down to listen first is what turned that around, a lesson that matters even more at a founding-stage company where there's no established org to learn from yet.
