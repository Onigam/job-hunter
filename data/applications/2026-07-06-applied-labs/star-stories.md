# STAR Stories - Applied Labs (Founding Engineer)

Picked for a founding-engineer, agent-orchestration role: ambiguity, ownership, hard technical calls, and driving adoption without formal authority.

## Story 1: Building Alaya from Zero to Acquisition

In 2016 I co-founded Alaya, a purpose and impact platform for employee engagement, and we started with nothing: no product, no customers, just a blank page and a couple of enterprise conversations that hadn't closed yet. My job was to architect and build the whole thing from scratch, alone at first, while the business tried to sign its first clients around whatever I was shipping. I picked a pragmatic stack, Node.js, React, PostgreSQL, deliberately avoiding anything clever, and got a working demo out in the first three months. From there it was iterative: multi-tenant architecture, an API layer, impact reporting, postal campaigns, plus hiring the first three engineers and setting up the CI/CD and review culture that let more than one person touch the codebase safely. The platform reached production with 50+ enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and in 2021 Benevity, a Canadian unicorn valued north of a billion dollars, acquired the company, which was about as direct a validation of those early technical calls as I could have asked for. Looking back, I'd have added real observability much earlier; we bolted on proper APM late, and multi-tenancy retrofitted after the fact costs a lot more than designing for it from day zero.

## Story 2: Making a Hard Architecture Decision Under Pressure

By year three at Alaya, the monolith was starting to crack under load, and the team split between a full microservices rewrite and something more modest. Investors wanted new features, not months spent on plumbing, so the task was finding a technical path that respected both realities instead of picking a side to win an argument. I ran a two-week spike comparing both approaches with actual load tests, then brought the CTO and investors numbers instead of opinions. What came out of it was a strangler-fig migration: pull the two highest-traffic modules out first, leave the rest monolithic, and define clear criteria for when the next module was worth extracting. The first extracted module shipped in six weeks and cut latency by roughly 40% on our busiest endpoints, and the remaining monolith stayed stable all the way through the acquisition, no rewrite required. The hard part wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the disciplined choice; benchmarks did most of that convincing, and next time I'd run them even earlier to shorten the debate.

## Story 3: Leading Without Formal Authority

At Benevity I was a Senior Staff IC with no direct reports, but I wanted two teams that didn't report to me to actually change how they worked and bring AI tooling into their daily workflow. Mandating it wasn't an option, so I started small: three curious engineers, a few internal demos showing real time saved on things like PR review, and informal lunch-and-learn sessions with no agenda beyond "here's what this looks like." I wrote up the wins concretely, things like shaving two hours off a review cycle, and put reusable prompt templates in a shared repo anyone could pull from without asking me first. Within three months, about 70% of engineers across both teams had adopted some form of AI-assisted review or generation, and I'd become the person people went to for that kind of tooling question. It was slower than a top-down rollout would have been, but it stuck, because people who'd chosen it themselves ended up selling it to their own teammates, which isn't something you can get from a memo.

## Story 4: Post-Acquisition Integration at Benevity

Joining Benevity after the acquisition meant landing on a team roughly ten times the size of Alaya, in a Java-and-microservices codebase I hadn't written a line of, with different processes and a different culture than the one I'd built. The task was folding Alaya's capabilities into that platform while still shipping new features, without losing the speed that had gotten us acquired in the first place. I spent the first month just mapping the existing architecture before proposing anything, and made a point of building relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing technical opinions on them. From there I drove adoption of AI-augmented workflows across the org and became the informal architecture liaison for the Europe-facing product surface. We migrated the core Alaya features successfully, I contributed to three major cross-team architecture calls, and became the default person for AI tooling questions on two teams. My one regret is moving too fast early on, trying to fix things that weren't actually broken in the new org; slowing down and listening first would have saved me a couple of unnecessary fights.
