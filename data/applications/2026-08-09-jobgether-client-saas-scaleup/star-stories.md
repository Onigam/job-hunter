# STAR Stories: Hands-on CTO, AI-First Engineering (Jobgether client)

Picked for this role because it's a hands-on CTO brief at a profitable SaaS scaleup: they'll want proof I've owned engineering end to end, made hard technical calls under pressure, driven AI adoption without a mandate, and can operate close to founders and external partners. Five stories, chosen to cover those angles directly.

## 1. Building Alaya from zero to acquisition

In 2016 I co-founded Alaya, a purpose and impact platform for employee engagement. There was no product yet, no customers, just a blank page and a small founding team trying to land the first enterprise clients. My job was to architect and build the whole platform, alone at first, then with a small team I'd hire and ramp up while sales kept moving. I picked a pragmatic stack, Node.js, React, PostgreSQL, something that could scale without over-engineering for a company that didn't exist yet, and got the core features working inside three months so we had something real to demo. From there it grew module by module: multi-tenant architecture, an API layer, impact reporting, postal campaigns, plus the first three engineering hires and the CI/CD and review culture that came with them. By the time Benevity acquired us in 2021, we had 50-plus enterprise clients across Switzerland and France running on what I'd built starting from nothing. Looking back, I'd invest in observability earlier, we bolted on proper APM late, and I'd design for multi-tenancy from day one instead of retrofitting it once it started to hurt.

## 2. The monolith decision under investor pressure

A couple of years into Alaya, the monolith was showing real scaling cracks and the team was split: full microservices rewrite, or something more modest. Investors wanted new features shipped, not months spent on a rewrite with no visible output. I needed a decision that held up technically without stalling the roadmap. So I ran a two-week spike comparing both approaches with actual load tests, then took the numbers, not opinions, to the CTO and investors. What I proposed was a strangler-fig migration: pull out the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest of the monolith alone, and set clear criteria for when to extract more later. The first module shipped in six weeks and cut latency roughly 40 percent on our busiest endpoints; the rest of the monolith stayed stable straight through the acquisition, no big-bang rewrite required. The hard part wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the right call. Data got us there faster than argument would have. Next time I'd front-load even more benchmarking to shorten that debate.

## 3. Driving AI adoption with no formal authority

At Benevity I was a Senior Staff IC with no direct reports, but I wanted two teams that didn't answer to me to actually adopt AI-assisted development instead of treating it as a novelty. I started small: three curious engineers, a few internal demos showing real time saved, informal lunch-and-learn sessions with no mandate behind them, just something worth fifteen minutes. I built reusable prompt templates and committed them to a shared repo so the habit had somewhere to live once people wanted to try it themselves, and I wrote up concrete wins, one story was a two-hour PR review cycle cut down to almost nothing. Within three months, about 70 percent of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted review and generation day to day, and I'd become the person people went to for AI tooling questions across the org. It was slower than a top-down rollout would have been, but it stuck, because people adopted it because it actually helped them, not because someone told them to.

## 4. Integrating into a much larger org post-acquisition

When Benevity acquired Alaya in 2021, I joined a team roughly ten times the size of what I'd been running, a different codebase in Java and microservices, different culture, different process entirely. The task was integrating Alaya's capabilities into Benevity's platform while still shipping new features, without losing the speed we'd built as a five-person team. I spent the first month just mapping their existing architecture before I proposed anything, and built relationships with the platform and infra teams before pushing any technical opinions on them. That groundwork let me drive three major cross-team architecture decisions and become the go-to person bridging the two codebases on the Europe-facing product surface. My first instinct was to move fast and fix things that, it turned out, weren't actually broken in their context, they just looked unfamiliar. Slowing down and listening first is what made the rest of it work.

## 5. Balancing product velocity with platform sustainability

Running a startup's entire engineering org means every technical decision competes directly with a feature the sales team already promised. At Alaya that tension never really went away, and the closest thing I had to a system for it was treating technical debt like a backlog item with real cost attached rather than an abstract complaint. The strangler-fig migration is the clearest example, but the same logic ran through smaller calls too, when to add proper test coverage, when a quick fix was actually fine to ship. What kept it from turning into constant negotiation was showing the cost in numbers investors and product could see, latency, incident count, time to ship a feature, rather than arguing architecture in the abstract. It's the same instinct I'd bring to a hands-on CTO role where the tradeoffs are the same shape, just with AI-assisted workflows added to the mix of what has to stay sustainable while the product keeps moving.
