# STAR Stories: Sailor Health, Founding Engineer (Senior/Staff)

Picked for a founding role at a small, fast-moving health-tech team (4th engineer) shipping clinical operations infrastructure and AI-native features, where ownership from zero and comfort with ambiguity matter more than any one framework.

---

## Story 1: Building Alaya from zero, owning the whole stack

In 2016 I co-founded Alaya, a purpose and impact platform for employee engagement, and I was the one who had to turn a blank page into something enterprise clients would trust with their data. There was no legacy system to lean on and no team to hand pieces off to, so the task was to architect and build the entire platform myself at first, then bring people in without slowing the business down while it was already signing its first clients. I picked a pragmatic stack, Node.js, React, PostgreSQL, that could scale without over-engineering for problems we didn't have yet, got the core features to a working demo in three months, and expanded iteratively into multi-tenancy, an API layer, and reporting as real usage demanded it. I hired and onboarded the first three engineers along the way and set up CI/CD and a code review culture before it became urgent. The platform reached production with more than 50 enterprise clients across Switzerland and France, and Benevity, a Canadian unicorn valued north of a billion dollars, acquired the company in 2021. Looking back, I'd invest in observability much earlier; we bolted on proper monitoring late, and retrofitting multi-tenancy cost more than designing for it from day one would have. That's the lesson I'd carry straight into a clinical ops layer built for a company doubling every couple of months: the seams you skip early get expensive fast.

## Story 2: A hard architecture call under investor pressure

A few years into Alaya, the monolith started showing real scaling cracks right as investors were pushing hard for new features, not a rewrite. My task was to make a technical trade-off I could defend to both engineers and the board, not just pick a side and hope. I ran a two-week spike comparing a full microservices rewrite against a modular monolith, backed by actual load tests instead of opinions, and presented the trade-offs to the CTO and investors with numbers on the table. From there I proposed and led a strangler-fig migration: extract only the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest alone, and define clear criteria for when to extract more. The first module shipped in six weeks and cut latency on our busiest endpoints by roughly 40 percent, and the remaining monolith stayed stable straight through the acquisition, so we never needed the big-bang rewrite everyone was afraid of. The hardest part honestly wasn't the technical work, it was convincing people that not rewriting everything was the disciplined choice; more upfront benchmarking would have shortened that debate. For a team building a V1 clinical layer while growing fast, that instinct, ship the narrow thing that unblocks the business instead of the elegant thing that doesn't, is one I'd bring on day one.

## Story 3: Driving AI-tooling adoption with no authority to mandate it

After Benevity acquired Alaya, I was a senior staff IC with no direct reports, but I wanted two teams that didn't report to me to actually change how they worked and adopt AI-assisted development. Since I couldn't hand down a mandate, the task became proving the value informally, one engineer at a time, and letting the results do the persuading. I started with three curious early adopters, built small internal demos showing concrete time saved, and ran lunch-and-learn sessions with zero mandate attached. I documented wins as they happened, things like shaving two hours off a single PR review cycle, and put reusable prompt templates in a shared repo anyone could pull from. Within three months about 70 percent of engineers across both teams were using AI-assisted review and generation, and I'd become the informal go-to person for it across the org. It was slower than a top-down rollout would have been, but engineers who opted in on their own terms ended up advocating for it themselves, which a decree from above never would have produced. Given Sailor already runs on Claude Code, this is the muscle I'd expect to use immediately, on speech-to-text triage and copilots, not just on the codebase.

## Story 4: Working inside a regulated, non-technical environment

Between Alaya and my current run, I spent two years at État de Genève building applications for the public education system, a very different world from a startup: procurement cycles, compliance requirements, and stakeholders who cared about the outcome and had no patience for technical jargon. My task was to ship real software inside those constraints without either blowing past the rules or letting them become an excuse for slow delivery. I learned to translate technical trade-offs into terms a school administrator or a compliance officer could actually evaluate, and to push back on requirements that were about looking safe rather than being safe. The applications shipped and stayed in production use, and I came out of it with a habit I still rely on: assume the non-engineer in the room has a real constraint behind their objection, and find out what it is before arguing. Healthcare has its own version of that, EHR data, clinician workflows, patient trust, and I'd rather have already made that mistake once, in a lower-stakes context, than make it for the first time on a therapist's workflow.
