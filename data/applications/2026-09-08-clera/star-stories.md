# STAR Stories: Clera, Founding Engineer (Potential CTO)

Prepared for a role that's essentially "second technical hire, own the direction." Picked stories that speak to building from zero, making stack calls under pressure, and setting culture without a title to back it up, since that's most of what this job actually is in the first year.

## Building the platform before there was a platform to build

Someone will ask what it's like to join with nothing built yet, and the honest answer is Alaya in 2016. I'd just co-founded the company and there was no product, no customers, just a blank page and a business that was already out talking to enterprise clients before the tech existed to serve them. I had to pick a stack that wouldn't fall over under scale I couldn't predict yet, and I went pragmatic: Node.js, React, PostgreSQL, nothing fancy, nothing I'd have to explain to a future hire. The first three months went into getting to a demo, then I kept layering on: multi-tenancy, an API layer, reporting, campaign tooling, expanding as the business signed more clients. Somewhere in there I hired and onboarded the first three engineers and had to invent our code review culture and CI setup from nothing, since there was no precedent to inherit. By the time Benevity acquired us in 2021, we had over fifty enterprise clients running on that platform, which felt like the clearest possible validation that the early calls held up. If I did it again I'd put real observability in from day one instead of bolting on APM once things were already painful, and I'd design for multi-tenancy up front rather than retrofitting it, since that retrofit cost us more than it should have.

## Choosing not to rewrite everything

At Alaya we hit the point every growing product hits, where the monolith starts creaking and half the engineering team wants to rip it out for microservices while investors want new features, not an architecture project. I ran a two-week spike comparing both paths with actual load tests instead of opinions, then took the results to the CTO and investors and made the case for a strangler-fig approach: pull out the two highest-traffic modules first, leave the rest alone, and set clear criteria for when to extract more. We shipped the first extracted piece in about six weeks and saw something like a 40% performance gain on our busiest endpoints, and the remaining monolith stayed stable all the way through the acquisition. No rewrite, no six-month detour. The hard part wasn't the technical work, it was convincing a room that "don't rewrite everything" was itself a real decision and not just avoidance. Data did most of that convincing for me. Next time I'd run the benchmarks even earlier, before the debate got heated enough that people had already picked sides.

## Getting a team to change how it works without being anyone's manager

After Benevity acquired Alaya, I ended up a senior staff IC on a team ten times the size of what I was used to, no direct reports, trying to get people to adopt AI-assisted development practices that nobody had asked me to introduce. Mandating it wasn't an option and probably wouldn't have worked anyway, so I started small: three curious engineers, a few internal demos showing real time saved on a real PR, informal lunch sessions with no agenda beyond "here's something useful." I built out a shared repo of prompt templates and wrote up concrete results, like the time a review cycle that used to take a couple hours got cut down noticeably. Within about three months, something like 70% of engineers across both teams were using it daily, and I'd become the person people came to for AI tooling questions without anyone appointing me to that. It was slower than a top-down rollout would have been, but it stuck, because people who opt in on their own terms end up defending the thing to their peers in a way a mandate never produces.

## Walking into someone else's codebase and making it legible fast

The month right after the Benevity acquisition is probably the closest thing I have to what this Clera role asks for: dropping into an unfamiliar domain and unfamiliar codebase and needing to be useful fast without breaking what already works. Alaya ran on Node and React; Benevity's platform was Java microservices at a scale I hadn't operated at before. My first instinct was to start proposing changes, and I caught myself doing that too early and slowed down instead, spending that first month just mapping how the existing architecture actually worked and building relationships with the platform and infra teams before I said anything opinionated. That patience is what let me become a credible cross-team architecture liaison for the Europe-facing product surface later, and I ended up contributing to three major architecture decisions that a faster, louder entrance probably would have poisoned. The lesson that stuck: in a new domain, the fastest way to earn the right to make calls is to spend real time understanding what's there before you touch it, even when the instinct is to start fixing things on day one.
